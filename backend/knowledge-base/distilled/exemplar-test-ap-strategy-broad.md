## How to think about AP strategy

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's grade, current/planned courses, intended major, target schools.
- Cross-reference `ap-exams/*.md` for specific exam intelligence.

The honest framework starts with what AP exams actually do for college admissions:

**1. AP courses signal course rigor on the transcript.** This is the primary admissions value. Selective colleges expect to see rigorous course load; AP/honors/IB/dual-enrollment all serve this purpose.

**2. AP scores are a secondary signal.** Most schools don't formally require AP scores in admissions, but strong AP scores (4s and 5s) corroborate the transcript and demonstrate mastery. Weak scores (1s and 2s) on courses where the student got an A can raise questions.

**3. AP credit can save college time and money.** Most colleges grant credit for 4s and 5s on AP exams (some grant for 3s). A student with 8+ AP credits can sometimes shave a semester off college, save tuition, or take more advanced courses earlier.

**4. AP courses teach actual content that matters.** Beyond admissions, AP courses prepare students for college-level academic work. Calculus, English, Sciences, History — content matters even if scores don't directly drive admissions.

**The strategic tradeoffs:**

- More APs = more rigor signal, but also more academic load and risk of grade decline
- Top schools want rigor + sustained excellent performance. 8 APs with all As beats 12 APs with B average
- Beyond a certain AP count (typically 8-10), marginal admissions benefit diminishes
- For elite schools (HYPSM tier), maximum reasonable rigor is expected; ~8 APs is competitive

## Which APs to take

**The core APs every academically strong student should consider:**

These are the most-recognized APs that signal strong general academic preparation:

- **AP English Language and Composition** (junior year typically) — strong writing signal
- **AP English Literature and Composition** (senior year typically) — humanities depth
- **AP US History** (sophomore or junior) — historical analysis, writing
- **AP Calculus AB or BC** (junior or senior) — quantitative foundation
- **AP Statistics** (any year, often junior or senior) — quantitative breadth
- **AP Biology / Chemistry / Physics 1 or C** — science depth
- **AP Foreign Language** (junior or senior) — language proficiency

## When to take APs (timing strategy)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's current grade.

**Freshman year:**

Most students don't take APs freshman year. AP Human Geography is the most-common freshman AP if available. Some students take AP World History freshman year if their school offers it. Generally OK to skip APs freshman year and focus on transition to high school.

**Sophomore year:**

Common APs: AP World History, AP Human Geography, AP Computer Science Principles, possibly AP US History at some schools.

Strategy: 1-3 APs sophomore year typical. Don't overload — sophomore year sets the trajectory.

**Junior year:**

The peak AP year. Common: AP English Language, AP US History, AP Calculus AB or BC, AP Chemistry / Biology / Physics, AP Foreign Language, AP US Gov.

Strategy: 3-5 APs junior year typical for strong students at top schools. Junior year is what AOs see in greatest detail (most recent academic record before applications).

**Senior year:**

Common: AP English Lit, AP Calculus BC (if AB taken), AP Physics, AP Statistics, AP Comp Sci A, AP Foreign Language continuation, AP Macro/Microeconomics, AP US Gov, AP Comp Gov.

Strategy: 3-5 APs senior year typical. Senior year matters for transcript verification and for AP credit at college.

**Honest insight on senior year:**

Senior year grades are submitted as mid-year reports and final reports to schools where you're admitted. Strong senior year matters. "Senioritis" with grade decline can have admit rescission consequences in extreme cases.

## Self-studying APs

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

Some students take AP exams for courses they didn't formally take in school. Honest considerations:

**When self-study makes sense:**

- Your school doesn't offer the AP you want
- You want extra AP rigor signal beyond what your school offers
- Specific AP scores would unlock college credit you want
- You have genuine interest and capacity for the additional load

**When self-study doesn't make sense:**

- You're already overloaded
- Your existing AP performance is mediocre (focus on improving existing performance)
- You're doing it just to seem more impressive (AOs see through this)

**Self-study is most common for:**

- AP Psychology (content tractable for self-study)
- AP Computer Science Principles (less content-heavy than CS A)
- AP Microeconomics or Macroeconomics
- AP Human Geography
- AP Statistics (with strong math background)
- AP Foreign Languages (for native or near-native speakers)

Self-studying core APs (Calc BC, Physics C, Chem) is hard and rarely produces strong results.

## AP scores in admissions

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull `schools/*.md` for target school AP score policies.

**The honest framework:**

- Most schools do NOT require AP scores in admission applications
- AP scores are self-reported on Common App; verified later if admitted
- Strong scores (4s, 5s) on rigorous courses corroborate the transcript
- Mediocre scores (3s) on courses where you got an A signal a gap
- Weak scores (1s, 2s) raise questions

**Do you have to report AP scores?**

Depends on the application:
- Common App: AP scores are optional. You can choose which to report.
- Strategy: report 4s and 5s; consider whether to report 3s; generally don't report 1s and 2s
- Some schools have score-required policies (less common)

**For elite school applications:**

A pattern of 4s and 5s across many APs is a strong signal. A pattern of 3s on courses where you got As suggests grade inflation in the course or a gap in mastery. A pattern of 5s on highly rigorous APs (Calc BC, Physics C, Chem) is admit-positive at top STEM schools.

## AP credit at college

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull `schools/*.md` for AP credit policies at user's target schools.

**AP credit varies dramatically by school:**

- **Highly restrictive (limited or no AP credit):** Stanford, MIT, Princeton, Caltech (treat APs more as placement than credit; use mostly for placement into higher courses)
- **Moderate AP credit:** most Ivies (some credit, varies by department), most top private universities
- **Generous AP credit:** state universities, large public universities (often grant credit liberally for 4s and 5s)

**What credit can do:**
- Place you out of intro-level courses (start with intermediate/advanced)
- Reduce total credit hours required for graduation (potentially shorten time to degree)
- Sometimes count toward general education requirements

**What credit doesn't do:**
- It's not money — colleges don't refund tuition for AP credit
- Some scholarships have minimum-enrollment requirements that AP credit can affect
- Some pre-med advisors prefer that you NOT use AP credit for prerequisite sciences (because med schools sometimes want to see college-level prerequisites)
