# Wayfinder Elementary & Middle School Summer + Enrichment Intelligence

## Executive Summary — How K-8 Camps & Enrichment Actually Work

Most parents of K-8 kids approach summer with a different mental model than parents of high schoolers — and they should. **Elementary and middle school summer programs aren't admissions plays.** They're about: childcare, social development, exposure to varied interests, building independence (especially for sleepaway), and giving the kid memorable experiences that compound into self-knowledge over years.

The Wayfinder DB has growing coverage of K-8 programs (currently 31+ pure ES/MS entries with strong WA depth, expanding nationally). When a parent asks "what should my 7-year-old do this summer?" the right answer is specific named programs filtered by their kid's age, location, family budget, and interests — not generic chatbot advice.

**The four big categories of K-8 summer:**

1. **Day camps** (most common) — typically 9am-3pm, weekly themes, ages 4-13. Cost range $200-600/week. Examples: Camp Galileo, KidsQuest, Burke Museum camps, Woodland Park Zoo camps.

2. **Specialty camps** — focused on one subject (STEM, music, theater, sports, art, coding). Often weeklong intensives. Cost varies wildly. Examples: iD Tech (coding/tech), Pacific Science Center (STEAM), Seattle Children's Theatre (drama), Music Center of the NW.

3. **Sleepaway / overnight camps** — multi-day to multi-week. Ages 6+ for shorter sessions, 8+ for longer. Cost $400-1500/week typically with sliding scale aid. Examples: YMCA Camp Orkila, Camp Sealth, Camp Korey.

4. **Outdoor/adventure camps** — sailing, climbing, wilderness skills, nature immersion. Examples: NW Outward Bound (older), Cascade Mountain School, Sound Experience.

## Cost Calibration — What Parents Should Expect

| Tier | Typical price | What to expect | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free / Subsidized | $0-100/wk | Library reading clubs, parks dept rec, scholarship-funded camps | Public library summer programs, parks dept |
| Budget day camp | $150-300/wk | YMCA day camps, some BGCA, Boys & Girls Club | YMCA branch day camps |
| Mid-tier specialty | $300-500/wk | Museum/zoo camps, science center camps | Burke Museum, KidsQuest, WPZ |
| Premium specialty | $450-700/wk | Brand-name STEM/coding/arts | Camp Galileo, iD Tech, Coyote Central |
| Sleepaway / residential | $400-1500/wk | YMCA residential, ACA-accredited overnight | Camp Orkila, Camp Sealth, Camp Korey |

**Scholarship reality:** most reputable orgs (YMCA, Camp Fire, Pacific Science Center, Woodland Park Zoo, Camp Sealth, BGCA) have meaningful sliding-scale or need-based aid — sometimes covering 100% of tuition. Worth asking. Wayfinder's DB notes scholarship availability when documented.

## When Sleepaway Camp Is Appropriate (and When It Isn't)

**Generally OK starting age 7-8** for shorter (3-5 day) sessions if the kid:
- Has done overnights at friends' houses successfully
- Asks about it / shows interest
- Has reasonable independence at home (dressing, basic hygiene)

**Wait until age 9-10** for longer (1-2 week) sessions.

**Avoid pushing it** if the kid is anxious about it. Forced sleepaway often backfires hard. Plenty of strong day-camp options through age 12.

## What Builds Long-Term Value (vs. Just Filling Time)

Per the existing pre-HS planning brain (admissions-pre-highschool-planning.md): the highest-leverage K-8 summers do ONE of these well:

- **Establish a sustained interest.** A kid who does YMCA Camp Orkila three summers running develops the kind of community + comfort with outdoor settings that compounds into HS leadership tracks.
- **Discover an unexpected passion.** A kid signed up for a chess camp who finds out they love painting — only happens if exposure is varied.
- **Build social skills outside school.** Mixed-school camps surface different friend groups + reduce social ruts.
- **Develop independence and resilience.** Sleepaway, especially. Hard to overstate the developmental value.
- **Give parents a chance to recover.** Don't underweight this. Burned-out parents make worse decisions.

What does NOT add long-term value:
- Stacking 8 weeks of high-priced specialty camps (overscheduling, kids burn out)
- Any camp the kid is reluctantly attending because parents thought it'd "look good"
- Pure academic remediation summers (kids associate summer with school dread — counterproductive)

## Rolling Registration Calendar (rough WA cadence)

- **December-January**: Member registration opens at top-tier museum/zoo camps. Premium camps fill in days.
- **February**: Early Bird discount windows close (Camp Galileo, etc.). Sleepaway camp registration opens.
- **March**: General public registration opens at most Seattle-metro institution camps.
- **April**: Most popular sessions are full. Off-the-radar specialty camps still have space.
- **May**: Last-call registration. Many scholarships still being awarded.
- **June-August**: Camp season; some day camps add late sessions if demand exists.

For the Wayfinder Engine: parents asking in February-April are in the sweet spot. Earlier they need to be told about member registration windows; later they need backup options.

## Common Parent Mistakes (K-8 specifically)

1. **Over-stacking specialty camps** — kid does 8 weeks of different "enrichment" and burns out. Mix in unstructured time.
2. **Treating it like college admissions** — a kid's K-8 summers don't show up in college apps. Picking based on "what looks good" backfires.
3. **Skipping the kid's preferences** — kids who choose what to attend are happier, more engaged, more likely to develop sustained interests.
4. **Assuming free=bad and expensive=good** — parks dept day camps are often excellent. Premium camps are sometimes just well-marketed.
5. **Not asking about scholarships** — most reputable orgs have 50-80% aid available; never advertised.
6. **Sleepaway too early** — pushing a 6-year-old into a week-long overnight because the parent went themselves at that age. Different kid, different generation.
7. **Single-track specialization too early** — a kid does only soccer camps every summer K-8 and we're surprised they don't want to play in HS.
