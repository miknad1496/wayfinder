## How to think about course rigor

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's current grade, current courses, planned courses, intended major, target schools.

The honest framework for course rigor:

**1. Selective colleges expect rigor in context.** They don't expect you to take APs your school doesn't offer; they expect you to take the most rigorous courses available to you given your circumstances. Counselors document school's course offerings in the school profile sent with applications.

**2. The "rigor vs grades" tradeoff has been overrated.** Both matter. The honest pattern: take rigorous courses AND earn good grades in them. "B in AP" is generally read as more rigorous than "A in regular" by AOs at selective schools.

**3. Senior year course load matters more than students think.** AOs see senior year first-semester grades (mid-year report) and final grades. Senior year of light courseload signals senioritis even with strong prior years.

**4. Course selection should align with intended major.** Pre-meds taking minimum sciences raise questions; engineering applicants without strong math signal mismatch.

**5. Rigor is school-relative.** The student at a school offering 25+ APs is expected to take more than the student at a school offering 5. AOs adjust expectations based on school context.

## How many APs is "enough"

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's target schools and current courses.
- Pull `schools/*.md` for any specific rigor expectations.

The honest framework by school tier:

**HYPSM tier (Stanford, MIT, Harvard, Yale, Princeton):**
- Expectation: maximum reasonable rigor at your school
- Typical admit profile: 6-10 APs total across high school
- More important than count: depth in your intended field area + reasonable breadth
- 4 APs at school offering 25+ is light; 6 APs at school offering 8 is appropriate

**Top 10-25:**
- Expectation: strong rigor for your school
- Typical admit profile: 5-9 APs
- Honors and dual enrollment count toward rigor signal

**Top 25-50:**
- Expectation: rigor sufficient to demonstrate readiness for college work
- Typical admit profile: 4-8 APs

**Top 50+:**
- Expectation: meaningful rigor; doesn't need to be maximum
- 3-6 APs typical

**The honest insight on rigor inflation:** there's a point of diminishing return beyond ~8-10 APs. Going from 8 to 12 APs doesn't typically meaningfully improve admit chances at top schools and may hurt by reducing quality (grade slippage, exhaustion, less time for deep ECs).

## Course selection by year

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

**Freshman year:**

Most students don't take APs freshman year. Common APs available at some schools: AP Human Geography, AP Computer Science Principles. Generally OK to skip APs freshman year.

Strategy: take honors courses where available, focus on transition to high school, build foundation in core subjects.

**Sophomore year:**

Common to take 1-2 APs. AP World History, AP US History (some schools), AP Computer Science Principles.

Strategy: continue building rigor incrementally. Don't overload sophomore year — sets the trajectory but you have time.

**Junior year:**

Peak AP year for most students. 3-5 APs typical for strong students at top schools.

Common: AP English Language, AP US History, AP Calculus AB or BC, AP Chemistry / Biology / Physics, AP Foreign Language, AP US Gov.

Strategy: this is what AOs see in greatest detail when reading applications. Strong junior year matters most. Don't sacrifice grades for additional APs.

**Senior year:**

3-5 APs typical. Common: AP English Lit, AP Calculus BC (if AB taken junior year), AP Physics, AP Statistics, AP Comp Sci A, AP Foreign Language continuation, AP Macro/Micro Economics, AP US Gov.

Strategy: senior year course load matters more than students think. Light senior schedule signals senioritis. Maintain rigor through senior year.

## Dual enrollment and post-AP options

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

For students whose school's course offerings are limited or who've exhausted them:

**Dual enrollment at community college:**
- Take college-level courses while in high school
- Earn high school credit + college credit (often)
- Common for students at schools with limited AP offerings or for advanced students who've finished available APs
- Look professional on transcript

**Online courses:**
- AP courses through certified providers (PVOnline, Florida Virtual School, etc.)
- College courses through coursework providers (Coursera, edX with verification)
- Allows access to courses your school doesn't offer

**Independent study:**
- Some schools allow advanced students to do independent study with a teacher mentor
- Document carefully so it counts as rigor on transcript

**The honest framework:** when standard high school courses are exhausted, dual enrollment and online options demonstrate continued intellectual engagement. Don't take "easy" senior year just because available courses don't include more APs.

## Course selection at schools with limited rigor

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

For students at schools with few AP offerings:

**The good news:** AOs adjust expectations based on school context. The school profile sent with your application documents what your school offers. You're not penalized for not taking courses your school doesn't offer.

**The strategic move:** take maximum rigor available + supplement externally:
- All AP courses your school offers
- Dual enrollment at community college
- Online courses for areas not offered
- Independent study with teachers
- Summer programs that demonstrate intellectual depth (RSI, COSMOS, etc.)

**The story to tell:** "I took every available rigorous course at my school and supplemented through [dual enrollment / online courses / independent study / summer programs] to deepen my preparation in [intended major area]." This reads as resourceful and motivated.

## When to drop a course (the honest framework)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

Sometimes students take on too much and need to drop a course. The honest framework:

**When dropping is the right move:**
- The course load is causing meaningful grade decline across multiple subjects
- The course in question isn't critical to intended major
- The drop happens early enough not to leave a "W" (withdrawal) on transcript

**When dropping hurts:**
- The course is critical to intended major (dropping AP Bio as a pre-med applicant)
- The drop happens late enough to leave a "W" (which AOs may notice)
- The pattern of multiple drops over high school suggests inability to manage load

**The decision framework:** is the cost of dropping (signal weakness, schedule disruption) worse than the cost of staying enrolled (grade impact, stress)? Pick the lesser harm.

If dropping, communicate proactively with your counselor so they can frame it appropriately if questions arise.
