**The digital SAT (current version):**

The SAT is now fully digital, taken on Bluebook app on College Board approved devices. Key changes from old paper SAT:
- Adaptive — your performance on first module determines difficulty of second module
- Shorter — total ~2 hours 14 minutes vs 3 hours
- Reading and Writing combined into one section (previously separate)
- Math allows calculator throughout (previously had no-calculator section)
- Built-in graphing calculator (Desmos) provided
- Score range 400-1600 (unchanged)
- Each section scored 200-800

**Score interpretation:**

- 1600: perfect; ~5,000 students per year nationally
- 1500-1599: top 1% nationally; competitive at all top schools
- 1400-1499: top 5%; competitive at top-25 schools
- 1300-1399: top 13%; competitive at top-50 schools
- 1200-1299: top 25%; competitive at top-100 schools
- 1100-1199: top 40%; competitive at most state flagships
- Below 1100: open admissions schools, some moderately-selective regional schools

**Score targets by tier of school:**

For target schools, pull deep files for specific middle 50% bands. Generally:
- HYPSM tier: 1530+ competitive, 1500+ acceptable with strong rest of profile
- Top 10-25: 1480+ competitive
- Top 25-50: 1400+ competitive
- Top 50-100: 1300+ competitive
- State flagships: 1250+ competitive (varies by state and selectivity)

## Section-by-section strategy

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

**Reading and Writing section (combined, ~64 minutes total):**

- Total: 54 questions across two modules (27 questions each, ~32 minutes per module)
- First module: standard difficulty
- Second module: difficulty calibrated to first module performance

Question types:
- Information and Ideas (~26%): main idea, central ideas, supporting evidence
- Craft and Structure (~28%): word in context, text structure, cross-text connections
- Expression of Ideas (~20%): rhetorical synthesis, transitions
- Standard English Conventions (~26%): grammar, punctuation, sentence structure

Strategic notes:
- Each question stands alone (one short passage per question, not long passages)
- Vocabulary in context appears frequently — strong vocabulary helps
- Grammar is concentrated in conventions questions; specific rules tested
- Practice strategies: focus on weakest question types via diagnostic tests; review errors carefully

**Math section (~70 minutes total):**

- Total: 44 questions across two modules (22 each, ~35 minutes per module)
- Calculator allowed throughout
- First module: standard difficulty; Second module: adapts

Question types:
- Algebra (~35%): linear equations, systems, inequalities
- Advanced Math (~35%): nonlinear equations, functions, polynomials
- Problem-Solving and Data Analysis (~15%): ratios, percentages, statistics, data interpretation
- Geometry and Trigonometry (~15%): area, volume, lines/angles, trig basics

Strategic notes:
- Desmos graphing calculator is built in — learn to use it for visualization and equation solving
- Word problems require translation skill; practice converting English to math
- Practice mental math for time efficiency on simple computations
- Strong math intuition helps catch errors

## SAT prep timeline

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's grade for timeline calibration.

**For students starting prep in spring of sophomore year:**

- April-May sophomore year: take a timed practice test for baseline. Begin light vocabulary building, concept review of weak areas.
- Summer between sophomore and junior year: 2-3 hours/week of structured prep. Build to 1-2 full timed practice tests.
- Junior year fall: PSAT in October (NMSF qualifier).
- Junior year winter: ramp prep based on PSAT result.
- Junior year spring: take official SAT (March or April).
- Junior year summer / senior fall: optional retake.

**For students starting prep in summer before junior year:**

- Summer: structured prep 5-10 hours/week. Build to multiple full timed practice tests.
- Junior year fall: PSAT.
- Junior year winter-spring: continue prep, take official SAT in March or April.
- Junior year summer: optional retake.

**For students starting prep junior fall (later but still workable):**

- Fall: PSAT first, then assess.
- Winter: intense prep for spring SAT.
- Spring: take SAT.
- Summer: retake if needed.

**For students starting prep senior year (late but possible):**

- Take SAT in August/October of senior year, retake December.
- Limited time forces choices: focus on highest-yield improvements (typically math for verbal-strong students or vice versa).

## Prep approach options

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

**Self-study (most common for motivated students):**

- Khan Academy's official SAT prep (free, partnership with College Board)
- Bluebook app official practice tests (free, simulates real test)
- College Board's "Daily Practice for the New SAT" app
- Test prep books: official College Board guide, supplemental Erica Meltzer (verbal), Steve Warner (math)

Pros: free or low-cost; flexible scheduling; sufficient for motivated learners
Cons: requires self-discipline; no external accountability; harder to identify systematic weaknesses

**Online courses:**

- Princeton Review, Kaplan online courses ($300-$1500)
- More affordable specialized courses (PrepScholar, etc.)

Pros: structured curriculum; built-in practice tests; reasonable cost
Cons: limited individualization; quality varies

**In-person tutoring:**

- Local tutors, test prep centers ($50-$300/hour)
- Premier tutoring services like AJ Tutoring, Inspirica, Veritas ($150-$500/hour)

Pros: highly individualized; addresses specific weaknesses; accountability
Cons: most expensive; effectiveness depends heavily on tutor quality and student receptiveness

**The honest framework:** for most students, official Khan Academy + official practice tests + sustained effort produces excellent results. Tutoring helps if student has specific significant weaknesses, low motivation, or resources to invest. Course-based prep is middle ground.

## Retake decisions

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's current SAT, target schools.

**When to retake:**

- Score is below the 25th percentile of admits at top target schools (significant gap)
- Score is below threshold for merit aid you're targeting (look up specific thresholds)
- You believe score didn't reflect your actual capability (had a bad day, illness, etc.)
- You've prepared meaningfully since last attempt and expect 50+ point improvement

**When NOT to retake:**

- Score is at or above the 50th percentile of admits at top target schools
- You've already retaken once and got no improvement
- Score is high enough that improvement marginal benefit doesn't justify time
- Stress about score is affecting other application areas

**The 4+ retake question:**

Most students should retake at most 2 times. 3 retakes is acceptable if there's a specific reason (illness on one date, etc.). 4+ retakes start to look like score-chasing on applications and rarely produce additional improvement.

**Score reporting:**

Most schools allow Score Choice (you choose which test dates to submit). A few schools require all scores. Check each target school's policy. With Score Choice, retakes have no downside — submit your best.

- Vanderbilt Cornelius Vanderbilt Scholarship: typically requires 1500+
- USC Trustee Scholarship: typically 1500+

The Wayfinder workflow: cross-reference verified scholarships DB for the user's state and target schools to surface specific score thresholds. This often shifts retake decisions — a 1480 student aiming at USC Trustee should push for 1500+ even if 1480 is "enough" for general admission.
