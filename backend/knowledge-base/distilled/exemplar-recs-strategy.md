## How to think about recommendation letters

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's grade, intended major, target schools.
- Pull `schools/*.md` for any school-specific rec requirements.

The honest framework starts with what recommendation letters actually do for college admissions:

**1. They corroborate (or contradict) the rest of your application.** AOs read your transcript, your activities, your essays — then read your recs to see whether teachers and counselors who actually know you describe the same person. Disconnect between application narrative and rec letters raises questions.

**2. They surface qualities your application can't show directly.** How you behave in class, how you engage with peers, how you respond to setbacks, how you contribute to the learning environment. These are observed by teachers in ways the application can't capture.

**3. They provide context AOs can't get elsewhere.** Why you took (or didn't take) certain courses. How your grades evolved over time. What's distinctive about you in a classroom of strong students. How you compare to other students the recommender has known.

**4. Strong recs amplify strong applications; mediocre recs don't sink strong applications.** AOs expect competent recs from competent teachers. Truly outstanding recs (from teachers who paint specific vivid portraits) can move admit decisions. Truly weak recs (generic, brief, lukewarm) can hurt.

## Who to ask (the strategic decision)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's intended major, current courses, target schools.
- Pull `schools/*.md` for specific rec requirements.

**Most US colleges require:**
- 1 counselor letter (your school's college counselor)
- 1-2 teacher letters (academic teachers from junior or senior year)
- Some allow 1 supplemental letter (mentor, coach, employer, research advisor)

**The strategic teacher choice:**

The right teachers to ask are NOT necessarily the ones who gave you the best grades. The right teachers are:

1. **Teachers who know you well as a person, not just as a student.** Multi-year relationships, advising roles, mentor-style connections.

2. **Teachers in core academic subjects (English, math, science, history, foreign language) — not electives.** AOs weight academic subject teachers more than elective teachers.

3. **Teachers from junior year preferred, senior year acceptable.** Junior year teachers had you for a full year and can speak to growth. Senior year teachers may have only had you a few months by application time.

4. **Teachers in subjects relevant to your intended major when possible** — but not at the cost of the previous criteria. A history teacher who knows you well > a math teacher who knows you barely, even if you're a STEM applicant.

5. **One STEM + one humanities is the conventional pairing** — gives AOs balanced perspective. But not required; pick the strongest combination.

**Who NOT to ask:**

- **Teachers who only know you as a quiet A student.** They'll write a generic letter that doesn't differentiate you.
- **Family friends or relatives.** Conflict of interest; reads as inappropriate.
- **Teachers from middle school or earlier.** Too distant.
- **Coaches, employers, etc., for the academic teacher slot.** They're appropriate for supplemental letters, not the academic slots.
- **Teachers who you suspect may be lukewarm about you.** Better to ask a teacher you're sure is enthusiastic than to gamble on someone you're unsure about.

**The pre-ask diagnostic:** "If I asked this teacher to describe me to a stranger, would they have specific stories and observations to share, or would they have to think hard?" Specific is what you want.

## When and how to ask

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

**Timing:**

- **Best:** late spring of junior year. Teachers have just finished the year with you, you're still vivid in their memory, and they have summer to draft.
- **Good:** early fall of senior year. Most students ask in this window.
- **Late:** mid-fall of senior year. Teachers are buried in dozens of rec requests; quality may suffer.
- **Too late:** less than 4 weeks before deadlines. Many teachers refuse this late.

**The ask:**

In person is best. Brief and specific:

"Mr./Ms. [Name], I'm applying to college this fall and was hoping you might write one of my recommendation letters. I really valued [specific thing about your time in their class — a specific project, a specific moment, a specific way they helped you]. I'm planning to study [intended major] and apply to [briefly mention school types or names]. Would you be willing to write a strong letter for me?"

The phrase "strong letter" matters. It gives the teacher an out — if they're not enthusiastic, they can decline gracefully ("I'm worried I don't know your work well enough to write you the strongest letter"). You want them to either commit to a strong letter or decline.

**After they say yes:**

Provide them with materials to write from:
- Brief resume of activities and accomplishments
- Your transcript
- A list of schools and deadlines (clearly organized)
- A 1-2 page "brag sheet" with specific things you'd want them to mention (specific projects, growth moments, things you're proud of from their class)
- Any specific essay topics or themes you're focusing on (so they can complement, not contradict)

The brag sheet is what most students underuse. Teachers writing 30+ recs appreciate concrete material; better recs come from teachers who have specifics to draw from.

## What strong recs look like (and what you can do to enable them)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

**Strong recs include:**

- **Specific anecdotes** — moments in class, projects, conversations
- **Comparison to other students** — "in my 20 years of teaching, [name] is among the top 3 students I've encountered in [specific quality]"
- **Specific qualities backed by evidence** — not just "intelligent and hardworking" but "intellectually curious — she stayed after class three times to discuss [specific topic] and brought in [specific outside reading] to share with me"
- **Honest assessment with strengths AND minor weaknesses (sometimes)** — strong recs aren't pure flattery; they're credible portraits
- **Connection to college fit** — "I can see [name] thriving at a school like [type], particularly in [specific kind of community]"

**Weak recs include:**

- Generic praise without specifics
- Brief letters (under one page)
- Letters that focus on the teacher rather than the student
- Letters that sound interchangeable across students
- Letters that praise effort without praising substance

**The brag sheet content that helps:**

- 3-5 specific projects, papers, or moments from this teacher's class you're proud of
- 2-3 ways the class shaped your interests or thinking
- 2-3 personal qualities you'd want highlighted with specific examples
- Your intended major and how this teacher's subject relates
- Schools you're applying to and any specific themes in your essays

## Counselor letters

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

The counselor letter has a different role than teacher letters:

**Counselor letters typically:**
- Provide context about your school (resources, course offerings, grading)
- Explain anomalies in your transcript (course drops, grade dips)
- Surface achievements not visible elsewhere (jobs, family responsibilities, leadership)
- Position you within your class context (rank if available, comparison to peers)

**To get a strong counselor letter:**

1. **Make sure your counselor knows you.** At schools with high counselor caseloads (500+ students), your counselor may know you only as a name. Build the relationship — request meetings, attend college info sessions, share your interests.

2. **Provide a brag sheet to your counselor too.** Same materials as teachers. Counselors writing 50+ letters appreciate concrete material.

3. **If you have transcript context to explain (a B in junior year due to family illness, a course dropped due to scheduling conflict), tell your counselor specifically and ask them to address it in the letter.** This is more effective than addressing it yourself in additional info.

4. **Engage with the counselor's process.** If your counselor offers questionnaires, fill them out thoroughly. If they want to meet, meet.

## Supplemental letters (the strategic question)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

Most schools allow 1-2 supplemental letters beyond the required academic recs. The strategic question is whether to use them.

**When supplemental letters help:**

- The recommender knows you in a context the academic recs can't show (research mentor, employer, sustained service partner, athletic coach for non-recruited athlete).
- The recommender will write specific compelling content (not generic praise).
- The supplemental complements rather than duplicates the academic recs.

**When supplemental letters don't help (or hurt):**

- The recommender doesn't add new information.
- The recommender is impressive in name but doesn't actually know you well (Senator family friend, famous person who met you once).
- The supplemental dilutes the application by making AOs read more without adding signal.

**The honest insight:** less is often more. If you're unsure whether a supplemental letter helps, lean toward not submitting it. AOs prefer fewer strong letters over more diluted letters.

## Recovery: what to do if you suspect a weak rec

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

If a teacher you've already asked seems unenthusiastic, gives generic responses to your brag sheet, or has a track record of bland letters:

1. **You can't read the rec (FERPA rights typically waived).** You have to make educated guesses about quality.

2. **You can ask another teacher to write an additional letter.** Most schools allow 1-2 supplemental letters; a stronger supplemental can offset a weaker required letter.

3. **You can ask the original teacher to wait.** "I want to make sure I'm giving you enough time and material to write the strongest possible letter — would late October work better than early October?" This buys time and signals you want quality.

4. **You can withdraw the request gracefully (if early enough).** "I've been thinking about who knows me best for this letter, and I'd like to ask another teacher who I worked with on [specific project]. I really appreciate your willingness to write." Most teachers understand.

5. **You can NOT pull a rec after submission.** Once submitted, it's part of your application.

The honest insight: most rec quality can be improved by giving the teacher better material to work with (brag sheet, specific examples). Most "weak rec" worries are actually "I gave my teacher nothing to work with" problems.
