## How to think about the activity essay class

- Pull `schools/*.md` for any specific school whose activity essay framing differs.

The activity essay is asking: "you've already listed your activities; now show us what one of them actually means to you and what you do in it." It's an opportunity to humanize the activity list — to show the texture and meaning behind a line item.

**The fundamental insight: AOs already have the activity list. What they're scoring is whether you can make ONE of those activities feel like it belongs to a real person.**

The activity list shows: name of activity, your role, hours/week, weeks/year, brief description. It's compressed. The activity essay is the opportunity to show what the compression hides — the specific moments, the unexpected texture, the relationships, the things that don't fit on a resume.

**The diagnostic for whether an activity essay will land:**

Two questions to ask before writing:

1. **Could you talk about this activity for 30 minutes in detail without notes?** Specific scenes, specific people, specific moments, specific things you've learned. If yes, the essay material is there. If you'd run out of material in 5 minutes, pick a different activity or develop deeper engagement.

2. **Is there something about this activity that wouldn't fit on the activity list?** Unexpected details, unlikely friendships formed, ways the activity has changed how you think, things you've struggled with in it. If yes, the essay has something to do. If no, the essay will repeat the activity list.

## What works in activity essays

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Cross-check the activity description against the user's saved EC list.
- Recommend Essay Reviewer with focus on voice/authenticity.

**1. Specific scenes from the activity, not summary descriptions.**

Weak: "Through volunteering at the food bank, I learned the importance of community service."

Strong: "Tuesday afternoons at the food bank, I'm in charge of the bread table. I've learned that the bread table is also where the regulars come to talk — Mrs. Patel about her son in dental school, Mr. Okafor about whether the Mariners will make the playoffs. I started bringing a notebook because I kept forgetting which week was which conversation."

The first claims learning; the second shows what the activity actually looks like in practice.

**2. The activity's hidden texture.**

Strong activity essays surface what the activity list can't show — the specific people you've come to know, the unexpected challenges, the small moments that make the activity matter beyond its resume value. AOs read for this texture because it differentiates real engagement from performative engagement.

**3. What the activity has taught you about yourself or others.**

Not generic "I learned leadership" claims, but specific learning. "I learned that I'm worse at delegating than I thought, and the cross-country captaincy made me figure out why." Specific honest reflection.

**4. Engagement with the activity's frustrations or limits.**

Strong activity essays don't only celebrate the activity. They engage honestly with what's been hard, what hasn't worked, what you've changed your mind about. This honesty signals maturity.

**5. The activity's connection to something larger.**

The strongest activity essays show how the activity has shaped how you see the world more broadly. Tutoring middle school math taught you something about how learning works; debate taught you something about how arguments form; food-bank volunteering taught you something about community.

**6. Specific people from the activity.**

Real activities involve real people. Activity essays that name specific people (with appropriate detail) and show real relationships read as more authentic than essays that treat the activity as a solo journey.

## What DOESN'T work in activity essays

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Recommend Essay Reviewer to flag these patterns.

**1. The activity-list-in-prose essay.** Just elaborates on what's already in the activity list. Wastes word count.

**2. The "I learned leadership" essay.** Generic claim about generic skill. Every team captain claims this.

**3. The accomplishment-listing essay.** "We won state, then nationals, then I was named MVP." If the achievements are noteworthy, they belong in the activity list. The essay is for the texture beneath them.

**4. The hero narrative.** "I single-handedly built the program." Almost always false (real activities involve teams) and reads as immature.

**5. The trauma-of-the-activity essay.** "Cross-country was so hard, the practices were brutal." Doesn't differentiate.

**6. The "this activity made me passionate about [stated major]" tie-in.** Reads as strategic, not authentic. AOs see through it.

**7. The disconnect from documented activity.** If the essay describes the activity in ways that don't match the activity list (different role, different hours, different focus), AOs notice and read as either inflated or inauthentic.

## Choosing which activity to write about

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's documented ECs and prior conversation about which activity matters most.

The right activity to write about isn't necessarily the most prestigious one. Three diagnostic considerations:

**1. The activity that has the most texture.** Texture = specific scenes, specific people, specific learning, specific frustration. Some activities accumulate texture (sustained 3-year engagement), some don't (one-off summer programs).

**2. The activity that humanizes you most.** Sometimes the right essay topic is an activity that's lower on the prestige hierarchy but reveals something specific about who you are. The McDonald's job, the long-term babysitting commitment, the family caregiving role — these can be stronger essay material than the prestigious internship.

**3. The activity that does work the rest of the application doesn't.** If your activity list is heavy on academics and competitions, an activity essay about a humanizing non-academic activity (your job, your community engagement, your art) can balance the application. If your application is heavy on service, an activity essay about your independent intellectual project can balance it.

**The wrong move:** writing about your most prestigious activity by default. AOs already see the prestige in the activity list; the essay is for the texture they can't see.

## Activity essay structures that work

**Structure 1: Single representative scene.**

Open with one specific scene from the activity that captures its essence. Develop through what that scene reveals about the activity, your role, the people, the work. Close with what the activity has taught you that lives in your current behavior.

Example arc: "The bread table conversation with Mrs. Patel → what the food bank actually involves beyond food → what I've learned about community presence → how I now approach situations that look transactional."

**Structure 2: Evolution over time.**

Open with how you started the activity. Develop how it changed for you. Close with what you understand now that you didn't initially.

Example arc: "Joined cross-country freshman year because my parents wanted me in a sport → first year was about finishing → second year was about competing → third year I realized it was actually about the team → captaincy this year is about something I didn't anticipate."

**Structure 3: A specific frustration or failure within the activity.**

Open with the moment something didn't work. Develop what the failure taught you. Close with how the activity has continued.

Example arc: "Spring of junior year, the tutoring program I'd been running fell apart because [specific failure] → what I had to learn → how I rebuilt → what the rebuild taught me about [specific lesson]."

**Structure 4: An unexpected friendship or relationship from the activity.**

Open with the specific person and how the relationship formed. Develop what you've learned from them. Close with what the activity has meant beyond its stated purpose.

Example arc: "Mrs. Patel at the food bank → how I came to know her → what her stories have taught me about [specific] → why showing up at the bread table matters in ways I didn't expect."

## School-specific activity essay framings

**MIT's "tell us about something you do for the pleasure of it" essay:**

Critical context: MIT explicitly rewards intellectual or creative pleasure activities. They're not asking about your most prestigious activity; they're asking what you do because you can't help doing it. Strong responses: building things, music, creative writing, math problem-collecting, reading deeply in a niche, side projects, intellectual hobbies. Weak responses: prestigious activities reframed as "pleasure," generic self-care activities (yoga, reading, walking).

The MIT essay is short (typically 250 words). Lead with specific scene, develop quickly, close with why this activity is the one you can't stop doing.

**Yale's "150-word elaboration" on a single activity:**

Very short. Has to be dense. The right move: pick the activity from your list that needs the most context and use these 150 words to add the specific scene or texture that makes the activity real. Don't try to summarize the activity; add what the activity list compresses out.

**Common App "Additional Activity" supplements:**

Often a slightly longer prompt (250-400 words). Use the structures above; lean into specific scenes and learning.

**Harvard's "Additional Information" activity elaboration:**

Optional but often used to add context to an activity. Use sparingly; only if the additional context substantively changes how AOs would read the activity.
