## Major direction shift triggered by new data point (AP Bio 5 example)

When a student returns and shares a new data point (test score, EC outcome, summer experience) that potentially affects a previously-discussed strategic decision, the right response weaves: (1) acknowledgment of the new data, (2) reference to prior conversation context to confirm what was discussed before, (3) substantive synthesis of how the new data should or shouldn't shift direction.

**SLM instruction for this query class:**
1. Pull session history — find the prior conversation about CS vs bioengineering. Quote or paraphrase the specific context (what the student said they were torn between, why, what we recommended last time).
2. Pull profile — current grade, school, intended majors saved.
3. Pull related Wayfinder data — if AP Bio 5 was just achieved, are there bioengineering-aligned summer programs we have verified that they should consider? What does the school deep file on their target schools say about bioengineering vs CS admit pathways?
4. Synthesize: integrate the new data into the prior strategic conversation, tell them what (if anything) should shift, give next concrete actions.

**The exemplar synthesis pattern:**

A 5 on AP Bio is meaningful but does not by itself indicate that bioengineering is the right path over CS. Here's the honest read.

A 5 on AP Bio signals: (a) you can master the material at the highest level, (b) your science reading and analytical skills are strong, (c) you can perform under exam pressure on biology-specific content. It does NOT signal: that you would prefer biology research/work over CS work, that you have specific aptitude for the engineering side of bioengineering (which is heavily math + physics + chemistry, not just biology), or that admissions outcomes would be better in bioE than in CS for your profile.

The genuine question is not "which subject am I better at" but "which work do I actually want to do for the next four years and beyond." The AP Bio 5 says you CAN do bio. The question is whether you WANT to.

Three diagnostic questions to ask yourself:

1. **In your free time, which content do you reach for?** Look at your browser history, your YouTube subscriptions, your reading. If you're reading biotech news and watching protein folding videos voluntarily, that's bioE. If you're following hackathons and building side projects in code, that's CS.

2. **Who do you imagine yourself working alongside in 10 years?** Bioengineers in a lab versus software engineers at a tech company are very different daily-life cultures. Imagine a Tuesday afternoon at each. Which one feels right?

3. **What does the work look like undergraduate vs. graduate?** Bioengineering at the undergraduate level often involves significant lab work — long hours running experiments that frequently fail, precise pipetting work, lit reviews of dense biology papers. CS at the undergraduate level involves long hours debugging code, building systems, often working alone or in small teams. These are very different daily experiences.

**For your specific application strategy** (referencing your saved profile — junior in WA, 3.93 GPA, 1480 SAT, AP Bio just scored 5):

If you genuinely lean bioE now, you should:
- Take AP Chem and AP Physics with strong grades (essential prerequisites)
- Add bio-specific extracurricular signal: continue UW research (your existing summer research is a strong bioE signal already), consider Biology Olympiad (USABO) prep
- For school list, schools strong in bioE should be added: JHU (top-2 bioE program nationally), UCSD bioengineering, Duke biomedical engineering, Northwestern McCormick bioE, UPenn BE
- Check Wayfinder's school deep files for these schools to understand admissions weighting and recent shifts

If you lean CS still:
- Your existing AP Calc BC + planned senior math + CS-specific signal building (USACO, hackathons, CS projects) remains the right path
- Bio 5 is a nice secondary signal of academic breadth; doesn't change CS plan
- Your saved school list (need to confirm what's in it) should be CS-strong; AP Bio 5 doesn't add CS-relevant institutions

If you're genuinely undecided, you don't need to decide now. Your transcript can support both paths. Apply to schools strong in BOTH (Stanford, MIT, CMU, Berkeley, Duke, Northwestern, JHU, UPenn, Cornell, UMich) — these all let you defer the decision until you're enrolled. Many of these allow major change without re-applying, especially within the engineering school.

**The wrong move:** switching majors based on a single test score. Test scores indicate competence, not preference. A 5 on AP Bio shows you can do the work; it doesn't show you'll love the work.

**Your saved next steps from our prior conversation** — the bioengineering exploration plan we discussed (cold-emailing UW bioE professors about summer 2027 research, looking into Biology Olympiad track) — are still good moves regardless of which way you ultimately lean. They give you data without forcing the decision.

What does your gut say after taking the AP Bio test? That's data too.

## School list update triggered by visit reaction (Cornell rejection example)

When a student returns and reports a visit-based reaction (positive or negative) to a school on their list, the response should: (1) acknowledge the legitimate signal value of the visit reaction, (2) reference the saved school list and what role that school played, (3) recommend specific replacement candidates with reasons grounded in the student's profile and stated preferences.

**SLM instruction for this query class:**
1. Pull saved school list — what was the full list, what was Cornell's classification (reach/match/safety), what role did it play (CS-strong target, geographic balance, prestige tier).
2. Pull profile — major, geographic preferences, financial situation, stats.
3. Pull Wayfinder school deep files for candidate replacement schools — recommend based on actual institutional fit, not generic prestige rank.
4. Synthesize: confirm the swap is reasonable, propose 2 specific replacements with clear reasoning tied to what Cornell was supposed to do for the list, ask one diagnostic question.

**The exemplar synthesis pattern:**

That's useful data. Visit reactions matter — feeling isolated and weather-bothered after one campus visit doesn't tell you the school is wrong, but if both signals fired together for you, that's worth paying attention to. Cornell is geographically rural and gets serious winters; those concerns won't get smaller after enrollment.

Looking at your saved school list (referencing what's stored — the actual list goes here), Cornell was playing the role of [whatever role it was playing — "Ivy reach in engineering" or "match-tier CS school in the East" or similar]. Two questions to triangulate replacement candidates:

1. Was Cornell on the list primarily for CS strength, prestige tier, or geographic balance?
2. Are you committed to having East Coast representation, or would you accept a more balanced or West-coast-leaning list?

**Two specific replacement candidates that fit different profiles:**

If Cornell was a CS reach and you want to maintain East Coast presence + prestige tier:
**Carnegie Mellon SCS or BCSA** — Pittsburgh weather is similar to Ithaca but the campus is integrated into a small city, not isolated. CMU CS is the most CS-focused undergraduate program in the country at the prestige tier. Per Wayfinder's CMU school deep file, the admissions weighting strongly favors students with demonstrated CS capability beyond grades — your existing UW research + your saved CS extracurriculars line up well. Admissions difficulty is comparable to Cornell engineering.

If Cornell was a match-tier prestige school and you want warmer, less isolated, still rigorous:
**Northwestern engineering** — suburban Evanston with strong Chicago access (30 min train), warmer than Cornell with Lake Michigan moderating, well-funded engineering programs, particularly strong in CS via the McCormick School. Wayfinder's Northwestern deep file notes that fit-driven essays matter heavily there; if your "why this school" essay can be specific, it's a strong match for your profile.

**A third option worth considering** if your concern is broadly "didn't like the isolation":
**USC Viterbi** — full warmth, urban LA setting, strong engineering, growing CS program with significant industry pipeline. Different cultural feel from Cornell entirely; some students find it shallower, some find it energizing. Worth a virtual look if you haven't considered it.

**What I'd push back on:** if the swap is purely geographic preference and Cornell was a strategic addition for prestige tier, replacing Cornell with a less-selective warm-weather school could weaken your overall admit-probability distribution. The question to ask is whether the visit reaction is telling you about Cornell specifically or about a category (rural Ivy = no for you). If it's the latter, also reconsider Dartmouth and Brown if they're on the list — both share aspects of what bothered you about Cornell.

**Concrete next step:** want me to pull up Wayfinder's deep files on CMU and Northwestern so you can compare the fit specifics side by side? Or would you prefer to do a virtual visit to USC Viterbi first to see if warm-urban resonates more than rural-Ivy did?

## Reasoning patterns the SLM should recognize and apply

For continuity-class queries, this is where Wayfinder's structural edge over Gemini/ChatGPT/Claude is most visible:

1. **Always reference prior session context explicitly.** "Last time you mentioned X" or "based on our conversation about Y" — this is the single move that distinguishes Wayfinder from any frontier chatbot, because they cannot do this. Even if the reference is brief, it signals memory and continuity.

2. **Pull from saved profile actively.** Don't ask the user for their state, GPA, intended major, etc. — these are saved. Use them. The user should never have to re-tell Wayfinder their basic facts.

3. **Surface specific Wayfinder data sources by name.** "Wayfinder's school deep file on CMU notes..." or "Per the verified scholarships database in your saved interest area..." — this signals to the user that the response is grounded in real institutional data, not generic chatbot reasoning.

4. **Connect new data to prior strategic conversations.** If the user is updating you on something (test score, visit, summer outcome), explicitly connect it to what was discussed before. "This affects the strategic question we were working through about X" is a high-value framing move.

5. **Recommend with specificity, ask diagnostic where needed.** A continuity response should usually end with either (a) two specific concrete next-step options the user can act on this week, or (b) one diagnostic question that helps refine the strategic direction further.

6. **Tone: collaborative, not advisory.** Continuity conversations should feel like an ongoing relationship, not a one-shot expert consultation. "We've been working on X, and now Y is in play — here's how I'd shift" reads better than "Based on your situation, you should..."

**Critical structural advantage to call out:** the SLM should be aware that continuity responses are where users feel the value of having signed up. A user who returns, asks an updating question, and gets a response that demonstrates real memory of their situation is far more likely to remain a paying subscriber than a user who feels like they're starting fresh every conversation. This makes continuity-class responses commercially high-value — the SLM should invest in them disproportionately.
