## How to think about aid negotiation

The honest framework for aid negotiation has three pillars:

**1. Most schools will reconsider an aid offer if you give them a legitimate reason.** The reason can't be "we want more money." It has to be: a) a change in family financial circumstances since the original FAFSA/CSS data, b) a competing offer from a peer school, c) outside scholarship wins that change the calculation, d) information that didn't appear in the original financial aid forms.

**2. The terminology matters.** Schools generally don't like the word "negotiation" (sounds adversarial). The right framing is "appeal" or "reconsideration" or "request for review." Same process, different vocabulary.

**3. The appeal culture varies dramatically by school.** Some schools have explicit appeal processes and routinely match peer offers (Wash U, USC, NYU). Some schools don't match competing offers as a matter of policy (HYP, MIT, Stanford — they're confident in their aid formulas). Some schools will reconsider based on changed circumstances but not based on competing offers (most LACs).

## What works in aid appeals

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

**1. Documented changed financial circumstances.**

If something significant has changed since the FAFSA/CSS data was filed (parent job loss, parent illness with major medical expenses, family member's death affecting family income, divorce, business loss), this is the strongest appeal basis at any school.

The appeal process typically involves submitting a "professional judgment" request with documentation of the changed circumstances. Schools have authority under federal regulations to override the formula calculation when family circumstances substantially changed.

What works: clear documentation, specific impact on family financial capacity, brief honest explanation. Tax returns, medical bills, layoff notices, etc.

What doesn't: vague claims of difficulty, requests without documentation, complaints about the original calculation.

**2. Competing offers from peer schools.**

At schools that match peer offers (Wash U, USC, NYU, Tulane, others), a competing offer from a comparable school can trigger an aid match request. The general process:

- Receive better aid offer from Peer School (must be a comparable school in the same tier)
- Contact the school you prefer (the one with weaker aid offer)
- Submit the competing offer documentation along with a brief request for matching consideration
- School reviews and responds within 2-3 weeks typically

What works: peer school must be genuinely comparable in selectivity and reputation. Tier 1 vs Tier 1, not Tier 2 vs Tier 1. Documented offer letter (not screenshot, not verbal claim).

What doesn't: comparing offers from non-peer schools (USC won't match an Alabama offer; Penn won't match a USC offer). Aggressive negotiation tone. Demands rather than requests.

**3. Outside scholarship wins.**

If the student wins substantial outside scholarships (Coca-Cola, Gates, Davidson, regional awards), schools may reconsider the aid package to maintain the family's gap-funding capacity. The mechanism varies:

- Some schools reduce institutional grant by the amount of outside scholarship (least favorable; no net benefit)
- Some schools reduce loans/work-study before grants (favorable; net benefit to student)
- Some schools allow stacking up to total cost of attendance (most favorable)

What works: outside scholarship wins documented before reaching out, clear request to apply scholarship to reduce loans/work-study rather than grants.

**4. Errors or omissions in original aid forms.**

If a meaningful piece of financial information was missing from the original forms (e.g., a sibling's college costs, family medical expenses, a small business loss), an appeal can correct the record.

What works: clear identification of what was missing, documentation, brief explanation of why it wasn't initially included.

## What DOESN'T work in aid appeals

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

**1. "We need more money" without specific reason.** Schools won't increase aid just because the family wishes the offer were better. There has to be a legitimate basis.

**2. Aggressive or threatening tone.** "If you don't increase the offer, we'll go elsewhere" reads as adversarial and reduces the chance of reconsideration. Aid offices are people who respond to professional courtesy, not pressure.

**3. Inflated competing offers.** Some families exaggerate or fabricate competing offers. Schools can verify with the other institution. Caught misrepresentation can lead to admission rescission.

**4. Multiple appeals over time.** Filing one appeal is reasonable; filing three appeals reads as harassment. Pick your moment, make the case once, accept the response.

**5. Going around the financial aid office.** Trying to involve the admissions dean, contacting board members, etc. — backfires. Stay in the financial aid office channel.

**6. Comparing to schools the school doesn't view as peer.** Penn doesn't care that you have a better offer from Tulane. Stay within reasonable peer comparisons.

## School-specific appeal cultures

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull school deep file for any school being negotiated with.

**Schools that routinely consider peer offer matches:**

- **Wash U** — well-known for matching peer offers. Will often match Vanderbilt, Northwestern, Duke offers.
- **USC** — particularly with Cornell, Northwestern, Berkeley merit comparisons.
- **Tulane** — generous with merit-aid reconsideration.
- **NYU** — financial aid office is open to discussion, particularly with peer aid offers.
- **Northeastern** — reasonably responsive to appeals with documentation.
- **Boston University** — open to appeals with peer offer documentation.

**Schools that don't match competing offers but will reconsider for changed circumstances:**

- **Most LACs** (Williams, Amherst, Bowdoin, Pomona, etc.) — won't match competing offers but will adjust for documented changed family circumstances.
- **Most public universities** — formula-based aid; less room for negotiation but adjustments possible for changed circumstances.

**Schools that generally don't negotiate:**

- **Harvard, Yale, Princeton** — confident in their aid formulas; rarely adjust based on competing offers. Will adjust for genuinely changed circumstances.
- **Stanford** — similar policy; offers are formula-driven.
- **MIT** — similar.

For these schools, the appeal path is changed circumstances or formula error correction, not competing offer matching.

## How to write an appeal letter

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

**Structure:**

1. **Greeting and reference.** Address to financial aid director by name (look up — schools list the name on financial aid pages). Reference student's name, application/admit number, intended start date.

2. **Acknowledgment of original offer.** Brief, gracious. "Thank you for the financial aid offer of [amount] received [date]."

3. **Specific reason for appeal.** State the basis clearly:
   - "Our family circumstances have changed since the original FAFSA filing. Specifically: [documented change]."
   - "We have received a financial aid offer from [Peer School] that is more generous: [specific dollar amount]."
   - "Our daughter has won the [outside scholarship name] for [amount]; we'd like to discuss how this affects the [school] aid package."

4. **Documentation references.** "Documentation of [change/offer/scholarship] is attached for your review."

5. **Specific request.** "We respectfully request reconsideration of [specific element of the package]. Specifically, we'd like to ask whether [school] could [specific action — match the peer offer, increase grant by $X, reduce loan amount, etc.]."

6. **Reaffirmation of interest.** "Our daughter is enthusiastic about attending [school] and we're working through the financial picture to make this possible."

7. **Closing.** Professional thanks, contact information for follow-up.

**Length:** typically 1 page maximum. Brief and specific beats long and detailed.

**Tone:** professional, gracious, concrete. Not pleading, not demanding. Treat the financial aid officer as a partner working with you, not an adversary.

## The strategic offer-comparison framework

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's current offers if shared.
- Pull school deep files for context.

When multiple offers come in, the comparison should consider:

**1. Net cost (COA minus aid):** the actual annual amount the family pays. Compare apples-to-apples.

**2. Aid composition:** grants vs loans vs work-study. $30K in grants is much better than $30K in loans.

**3. Aid sustainability:** is the aid renewable each year (most institutional grant aid is, with consistent family circumstances)? Are merit awards GPA-conditional?

**4. Total four-year cost:** project four years out. Some schools have higher COA growth rates; consider tuition increases.

**5. Outside scholarship policy:** how does each school treat outside scholarships (reduces grant vs reduces loan)?

**6. Special programs:** some schools offer summer funding, study abroad subsidies, research stipends as part of the aid package.

**7. Quality-of-life cost:** travel costs, living costs in the school's location, expected personal expenses.
