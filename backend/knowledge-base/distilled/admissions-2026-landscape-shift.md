# 2026 Admissions Landscape — The Post-Landscape, Post-SFFA, Funding-Stressed Era

This is a synthesis of three converging shifts that together reshape US college admissions for the 2026 cycle and beyond. Wayfinder advisors must understand how these interact, especially for international applicants (Korean families being a primary market).

## Shift 1 — The Landscape tool's quiet retreat

College Board developed the **Environmental Context Dashboard (ECD)** in 2017-2019, nicknamed by media the "adversity score." Public outcry over reducing students to a single 1-100 number forced a 2019 rebranding to **Landscape**, which removed the single score and instead surfaced 5-7 contextual data points per applicant: high school's average AP enrollment, neighborhood crime index, family income tier, percentage of HS graduates attending 4-year college, etc. By 2020-2024 most selective colleges used Landscape as one input alongside transcript and essays.

**2025-2026 reality**: many schools are quietly de-emphasizing or discontinuing Landscape:
- The federal political climate (post-2024 election) created scrutiny of any tool that could be characterized as proxying for race or socioeconomic class in admissions.
- Public flagships (Ohio State, several Big Ten and Sun Belt schools) have explicitly announced they will not use contextual demographic data going forward.
- Private universities have not made public statements but admissions officers report internally that Landscape data carries less weight than 2-3 years ago.
- College Board itself has signaled it is "evaluating" the product and has not aggressively re-marketed it.

**What replaces it operationally**: schools fall back on (a) the high school profile sheet (still required, still gives context but at the school level not the applicant level), (b) the application essays themselves where students self-disclose context, (c) first-gen and Pell-grant flags (these are factual, not modeled), (d) school-specific recruiter relationships with target high schools.

## Shift 2 — Post-SFFA continuation

The Supreme Court's June 2023 decision in **Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard** eliminated explicit consideration of race in college admissions. Schools have spent two cycles (2024 and 2025) figuring out how to maintain diversity goals within the new legal frame. Common adaptations:

- **Essay-as-channel**: schools added or expanded prompts that invite students to discuss "how their background has shaped them" without requiring race disclosure but allowing it.
- **Recruiting shifts**: more aggressive outreach to under-represented communities through partnerships, application-fee waivers, regional visit increases.
- **Major-blind first-pass reads**: some schools moved race-flag fields to a later read step to ensure they don't anchor early evaluators.
- **Geographic and SES proxies**: zip code, first-gen status, Pell eligibility as legally-cleaner indicators that correlate with the diversity outcomes schools want.

The 2026 cycle is the **first full cycle without Landscape** at many schools AND the **third cycle post-SFFA**. AOs have less data on individual applicant context than at any point in the last decade. This makes the application narrative — essays especially — work harder.

## Shift 3 — The funding squeeze

Several pressures are colliding on the revenue side of US higher ed:

- Federal research funding cuts: NIH and NSF appropriations contracted in 2025 fiscal cycles. Research-heavy universities (Johns Hopkins, MIT, Stanford, UMich, UNC, UCSF, Emory) absorb the hit through smaller grad cohorts and tighter postdoc lines.
- Endowment volatility: 2024-2025 returns underperformed long-term assumptions at several schools (notably Harvard's negative real-return year). Operating draws are constrained.
- Demographic cliff: the long-anticipated drop in 18-year-olds is hitting harder in the Northeast and Midwest. Domestic enrollment is down at non-elite schools.
- ICE / international policy uncertainty: visa friction has pushed some international applicants toward UK, Canada, Australia. But the schools that retain international pipelines are MORE protective of those revenue streams.

**The convergent effect**: international full-pay students are **financially more valuable** to mid-tier US privates and certain publics than at any time in the last decade. Top elites (HYP + MIT + Stanford) have so much endowment cushion this barely shifts their calculus. But the next 30-50 schools (Emory, USC, Vanderbilt, Tulane, Tufts, BU, NYU, Northwestern, Rice, WashU, Notre Dame, BC, Wake, Carnegie Mellon, Northeastern, etc.) face real pressure to fill seats and meet revenue targets.

## How these three shifts compound for applicants

For **domestic US applicants** the post-Landscape, post-SFFA reality means:
- Essays carry substantially more weight. They are the primary place to communicate context.
- "Why X" supplements and the personal essay are the most direct channels to translate background into narrative.
- First-gen status and Pell eligibility are more salient (they're legally clean diversity proxies).
- Strategic schools-list construction is more important — full-need-met schools will admit students who fit even without aid context.

For **international applicants** (Korean / Chinese / Indian / etc.) the picture is *very* different and most of the discourse misses this:

1. **SFFA didn't change international applicant evaluation.** SFFA was about US-resident racial categories. International students are evaluated separately on a different rubric — citizenship, English proficiency, school context, financial fit.

2. **Landscape was largely a US-domestic tool.** International student evaluation never depended on it heavily. Its retreat doesn't directly affect Korean applicants.

3. **Funding pressure quietly favors strong full-pay international applicants.** Schools that can fill an admit slot with a full-pay Korean student getting 1530 SAT and a strong essay are more receptive in the 2026 cycle than they were in 2022. This isn't "buying a seat" — schools still have admit-quality bars they will not cross — but in the marginal-applicant zone (e.g., Emory, USC, Tulane, BU, Tufts), full-pay international applicants with strong-but-not-extraordinary profiles see better odds than they did pre-2024.

4. **The need-blind for international list is unchanged**. Harvard, Yale, Princeton, MIT, Amherst, Bowdoin, Williams, Dartmouth (recently announced) remain need-blind for international applicants. For Korean families requesting financial aid, these are still the only schools where requesting aid does not penalize the application.

5. **Essay differentiation is more valuable for Korean students.** Schools have less context-data (no Landscape on the international side either), so the essay is the primary place where Korean students can break out of the "high-scoring international Asian applicant" perception. Authentic specificity, family-dynamic honesty, and Korean cultural context that the AO has not seen 1,000 times all carry more weight than they did in 2022.

6. **Merit aid for international students is increasingly a strategic lever.** Schools competing for top international applicants offer named merit awards (Vanderbilt Cornelius, Duke Robertson, Emory Scholars, Notre Dame Hesburgh-Yusko, USC Trustee, BU Trustee). With financial pressure on the institution but also the need to attract top international talent, these awards are slightly more accessible to strong applicants than they were 5 years ago.

## Practical advisor guidance derived from these shifts

When advising Korean (or other international) families:

- **Need-blind list (8 schools) for full-aid applicants**: still the only path where requesting aid carries no admit penalty. Apply broadly across the 8 if aid is required.
- **Full-pay strategy for need-aware schools (the rest)**: a real lever right now. Schools won't lower the academic bar, but at the margin a strong full-pay applicant has better odds than 2-3 years ago.
- **Merit aid targeting**: identify the 3-5 named merit programs that fit the student's profile and apply early/strategically. Expect roughly 5-15% of admits to receive named merit at competitive schools.
- **Essay weight**: invest disproportionately in essays. The personal essay + 2-3 strongest "Why X" supplements drive marginal admit decisions more than they did pre-2024.
- **List construction**: build wider in the target tier (12-15 schools rather than 8-10) to absorb the variance in how individual schools weight financial fit this cycle.

When advising US domestic families post-SFFA + post-Landscape:

- **First-gen / Pell flags**: ensure they are accurately set on Common App. These are the cleanest legal-frame diversity indicators.
- **Background essays**: schools want context without race-explicit framing. The student should write specifically about lived experience, community, family, not in racial-category terms but in concrete-detail terms.
- **High school context matters even without Landscape**: if your HS is under-resourced or first-gen-heavy, the school profile sheet still surfaces this. Counselors should write strong school-context paragraphs.
- **AOs are reading more carefully because they have less data**: a clear, concise narrative arc across essays + activities + transcript is rewarded.

## What advisors should NOT say

- "Race-based admissions are gone, so it's all merit now." False. Schools still pursue diversity goals; SFFA just removed one tool. The whole apparatus has reorganized.
- "Landscape is dead so context doesn't matter." False. Context still matters; schools just gather it through different channels (essays, school profile, first-gen flag).
- "Schools are desperate for international students so getting in is easier." Misleading. Schools want strong full-pay internationals; the academic bar is unchanged, but marginal-applicant odds in the target tier have shifted.
- "Need-blind got expanded to more schools." False. Only Dartmouth recently added to the list. The other 7 remain unchanged.
