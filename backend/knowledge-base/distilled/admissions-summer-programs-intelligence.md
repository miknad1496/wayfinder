# Wayfinder Summer Programs Intelligence — Deep Dive for High School Families

## Executive Summary — Read This First

Summer programs for high school students are one of the highest-leverage decisions a family makes during the 9th-11th grade window. They can dramatically strengthen a college application, expose a student to a field deeply, and provide signals that admissions readers actively look for. They can also waste $5,000-$10,000 of family budget on essentially branded summer camp that doesn't move the admissions needle at all.

**The most important framing:** there are roughly four tiers of summer programs, separated by selectivity and cost. Tier 1 (highly selective, free) is what genuinely moves admissions decisions. Tier 4 (open enrollment, paid) does almost nothing for admissions despite often being marketed by families and counselors as "Harvard summer program." Most parents come in convinced they should pay $7,000 to send their kid to "Harvard for the summer" — and that program (Harvard Secondary School Program) is open enrollment with a high acceptance rate and confers minimal admissions advantage. Meanwhile, the truly transformative programs — RSI, MITES, SSP, TASP — are FREE, and most families have never heard of them.

**This document covers:** the full landscape of programs, the prestige and selectivity hierarchy, what each tier actually signals to admissions, application timelines (mostly Dec-Feb deadlines for the following summer), grade-level-specific guidance, field-specific recommendations, and the common mistakes families make. It also flags WA-specific options (Dan's family is in Seattle metro).

**For the Wayfinder Engine handling these questions:** lead with the database. The Programs module (`backend/data/scraped/programs.json`, surfaced in the Programs sidebar tool) has 800+ curated entries filterable by grade, state, format, category, cost, selectivity. When a parent asks "what summer program should my sophomore do?" the move is: (1) acknowledge the question with a confident framing, (2) give specific, calibrated direction in chat (not generic), (3) point them to the Programs tool to filter and explore. Never produce a generic "consider research, internships, or pre-college programs" answer — that's worthless.

## Part 1: The Four Tiers — What Each Actually Signals

### TIER 1: Highly Selective, Free, Prestige-Setting

Acceptance rates 5-15%. Often free including room and board. These are the programs that actually function as admissions signals at the highest level. Admissions officers at MIT, Stanford, Caltech, the Ivies, U Chicago recognize them by name and treat them as evidence of demonstrated excellence in the relevant field.

**STEM-flagship Tier 1 programs:**

- **Research Science Institute (RSI)** — Center for Excellence in Education + MIT. The single most prestigious high school STEM summer program in the world. ~80 students globally per year. 6 weeks of mentored research at MIT (or partner institution). Acceptance rate ~3%. **Free.** Application due mid-January. Required: top-10% class rank, very high test scores (PSAT 99th percentile), serious science/math achievement (Olympiad medals, science fair regional winners). Application includes essays, recommendations, research interest.
  - Source: cee.org/research-science-institute

- **MIT MITES** (formerly MITES, now expanded into MITES Summer, MITES Saturdays, MOSTEC) — 6 weeks at MIT, focused on rising seniors from underrepresented groups in STEM. Acceptance rate ~5%. **Free** including travel. Application due mid-February.
  - Source: mites.mit.edu

- **Summer Science Program (SSP)** — Astrophysics or biochemistry research at one of 5 sites (Caltech, NMT, U Colorado Boulder, Indiana U, Purdue). 39-day intensive. ~36 students per site. Acceptance rate ~10%. ~$8,500 list price but extensive financial aid available; 60%+ on aid. Application due late January.
  - Source: summerscience.org

- **Stony Brook Garcia Summer Scholars** — Polymer materials science research at Stony Brook. ~50 students. Free for accepted students. Application due February. Strong choice for students interested in materials science / chemistry.
  - Source: garcia.stonybrook.edu

- **NIH HiSTEP** — High School Scientific Training and Enrichment Program. Free, residential at NIH in Bethesda MD. For rising 12th-graders interested in biomedical research. Highly selective. Application due February.
  - Source: training.nih.gov

- **Clark Scholars Program (Texas Tech)** — 7-week paid research program ($750 stipend). 12 students per year. Acceptance rate ~2-3%. For rising seniors. Application due February. Free including travel and meals.
  - Source: clarkscholars.ttu.edu

- **Simons Summer Research Program (Stony Brook)** — Lab research at Stony Brook for rising seniors. ~50 students. ~$4,800 fee, financial aid available. Application due February.
  - Source: simons.stonybrook.edu

- **Math Olympiad Summer Program (MOP)** — Invitation-only training camp for top performers at USAMO. About 60 invitees. Free. Not an application — by invitation based on USAMO results.
  - Source: maa.org/programs/maa-awards/usamo

- **Canada/USA Mathcamp** — 5-week residential math camp. ~120 students. ~$5,000 fee, generous financial aid. Application is an extensive math problem set due in March-April (rolling).
  - Source: mathcamp.org

- **PROMYS (Boston University)** — 6-week intensive number theory program. ~80 students. ~$6,000 fee, full need-based aid. Application due March, includes 9 challenging math problems.
  - Source: promys.org

- **Ross Mathematics Program (Ohio State / OSU)** — 6-week program, similar to PROMYS but slightly different style. ~50 students. ~$6,000, financial aid available. Application due late March.
  - Source: rossprogram.org

**Humanities/social sciences flagship Tier 1 programs:**

- **Telluride Association Summer Program (TASP)** — 6-week humanities seminar for rising seniors. Top 5 most prestigious high school humanities program in the US. Free including travel. Acceptance rate ~10%. Application due mid-January, requires extensive essays. Two seminars at U Michigan and Cornell.
  - Source: tellurideassociation.org/our-programs/high-school-students/summer-program-juniors-tasp/

- **Telluride Association Sophomore Seminar (TASS)** — Same but for rising juniors. Free. Acceptance rate ~12%. Application due January.
  - Source: tellurideassociation.org/our-programs/high-school-students/sophomore-seminar-tass/

- **Iowa Young Writers' Studio** — Two-week creative writing residency at U Iowa. Acceptance rate ~30%. ~$2,300 fee, financial aid available. Application due February. The strongest signal for serious writing.
  - Source: iowayoungwriters.uiowa.edu

- **Kenyon Young Writers Workshop** — Two-week creative writing program. ~$3,500. Application due May (rolling).
  - Source: kenyon.edu/young-writers-workshop

- **Sewanee Young Writers' Conference** — 12-day creative writing program. ~$2,500. Application due April.
  - Source: sewanee.edu

**Mixed/leadership Tier 1 programs:**

- **Bank of America Student Leaders Program** — 8 weeks paid internship at a local nonprofit + week-long Washington DC summit. ~300 students nationally. Application due January.
  - Source: about.bankofamerica.com/en/making-an-impact/student-leaders

- **Coca-Cola Scholars Foundation Leadership Programs** — Various leadership programs through the foundation. Free. Application varies.
  - Source: coca-colascholarsfoundation.org

- **JCamp (Asian American Journalists Association)** — 6-day journalism program. Free including travel for accepted students. Application due February.
  - Source: aaja.org/programs-and-events/jcamp

- **NSLI-Y (National Security Language Initiative for Youth)** — Department of State-funded summer language immersion abroad. Languages: Arabic, Chinese, Hindi, Indonesian, Korean, Persian, Russian, Turkish. Acceptance rate ~10%. **Free** including airfare. Application due early November (extra-early!).
  - Source: nsliforyouth.org

- **YES Abroad (Kennedy-Lugar Youth Exchange and Study Abroad)** — Year or summer programs abroad in Muslim-majority countries. Free. Application due January.
  - Source: yesabroad.org

- **CIEE Global Navigator Scholarships** — Need-based scholarships up to $5,000 for CIEE summer abroad programs. Application due Feb.
  - Source: ciee.org/go-abroad/high-school-study-abroad

### TIER 2: Selective, Free or Stipend, Strong Credential

Acceptance rates 15-30%. Free or with stipend. Recognizable to admissions readers in the relevant field. Solid signal.

- **Bay Area Apprenticeship Programs** (UC Berkeley SHARP, Stanford SUMaC) — SUMaC is Stanford University Mathematics Camp, ~$5,200 with strong financial aid. Highly competitive math. Application due February.
  - Source: sumac.spcs.stanford.edu

- **NIH High School Summer Internship Program (HS-SIP)** — 8 weeks paid biomedical research at NIH labs. Up to $2,400 stipend (depending on grade). Open to rising 12th graders. Application due early February.
  - Source: training.nih.gov/programs/sip

- **Smithsonian Internship Programs** — Various Smithsonian museums offer summer internships. Some paid, some unpaid. Application varies.
  - Source: internships.si.edu

- **Future Investigators in Medical Research (FIMR)** at Cornell, Hopkins, Brown — paid medical research summers. Selective.

- **Knowles Teacher Initiative Future Teacher Program** — for students considering teaching career. Funded.
  - Source: knowlesteachers.org

- **Princeton Summer Journalism Program** — Free 10-day program for rising seniors from low-income families. Acceptance rate ~10%. Pays travel, housing, meals.
  - Source: psjp.princeton.edu

- **Mellon Mays Undergraduate Fellowship Track** — Various pre-MMUF summer programs leading into the prestigious MMUF in college. Free.
  - Source: woodrow.org/fellowships/mellon-mays/

- **AFS Intercultural Programs Summer Abroad** — Need-based scholarships make it accessible. ~$5,000-7,000 depending on country.
  - Source: afsusa.org

- **Adams Scholarship Programs (TASP-style for rising sophs)** — TASS focused on rising 11th, but a few smaller variants exist. Check yearly.

- **JSA Summer School at Stanford / Princeton / Georgetown** — 3-week intensive in government/policy. Selective, ~$5,000 fee. Some financial aid.
  - Source: jsa.org/summer

### TIER 3: Selective, Paid, Brand-Heavy

Acceptance rates 25-50%. Cost $4,000-9,000. These are real programs but the brand is doing significant work — admissions readers recognize the institution but understand these are PAID summer programs, not selective admissions.

- **Stanford Pre-Collegiate Studies (SPCS)** — Various 2-3 week programs at Stanford for high schoolers. ~$5,000-9,000. Acceptance rate varies by program. Strong subject-specific tracks (genomics, philosophy, etc.).
  - Source: spcs.stanford.edu

- **Yale Young Global Scholars (YYGS)** — 2-week residential program. ~$6,500. Acceptance ~30%. Subject tracks include AI, biotech, justice, politics, science.
  - Source: globalscholars.yale.edu

- **Brown University Pre-College** — 1-7 week programs, various subjects. ~$2,800-9,000. Open enrollment for most courses (but selective for STEM I and Leadership Institute).
  - Source: precollege.brown.edu

- **Harvard Secondary School Program (SSP)** — 7-week residential. ~$13,000+ for residential. NEAR-OPEN ENROLLMENT (acceptance rate ~80%+). This is the program most parents confuse with a "selective Harvard signal." It is not. It's a paid summer college experience that confers little admissions advantage on its own.
  - Source: ssp.summer.harvard.edu

- **Cornell Summer College** — 3- or 6-week pre-college. ~$8,000-13,000. Modestly selective for some tracks.
  - Source: sce.cornell.edu/precollege

- **Columbia Summer Immersion** — 1-3 week programs. ~$3,000-12,000. Open enrollment for most.
  - Source: precollege.sps.columbia.edu

- **Notre Dame Leadership Seminars** — 2-week programs. ~$1,500. More selective than most pre-college (~25% acceptance).
  - Source: precollege.nd.edu

- **Boston University Academy of Pre-College Programs (RISE)** — 6-week research internship at BU labs. ~$5,500. Selective, ~10% acceptance for the research stream.
  - Source: bu.edu/summer/high-school/rise

- **CTY (Center for Talented Youth) at Johns Hopkins** — 3-week subject-deep programs at multiple sites. Requires CTY testing eligibility. ~$5,000. Strong programming.
  - Source: cty.jhu.edu

- **Northwestern College Prep Program** — 1- or 3-week pre-college. ~$3,000-9,000. Open enrollment for most.

- **Georgetown Summer Programs for HS** — Various 1-3 week programs. ~$3,000-5,000.

### TIER 4: Open Enrollment, Paid, Minimal Admissions Signal

Acceptance rate 70%+. Cost $3,000-10,000. Admissions readers know these are pay-to-attend. They do NOT meaningfully strengthen an application beyond what the student does in their normal life.

This includes most "summer at [Big University]" programs you'll see advertised online: Stanford SPCS open courses, Northwestern's general courses, Penn LBW, USC Summer Programs general track, NYU, BU general programs, Tulane Summer Pre-College, GW Summer Pre-College, Tufts Summer Pre-College, Emory Summer Pre-College, Vanderbilt Pre-College, Wake Forest Summer Pre-College, etc.

**Critical parent message:** if your family is paying $7,000+ for one of these and the student isn't getting hands-on research, intensive subject mastery, or transformative leadership experience, you may be paying for a glorified summer camp. The same student doing local research with a community college professor + a part-time job + a personal project produces a stronger application than the Harvard SSP student.

**When Tier 4 IS worth it:**
- The student genuinely benefits from the residential college-life preview
- The specific course material is unavailable elsewhere
- The student needs the structure and would otherwise drift through summer
- The family budget can absorb it without strain
- The student wants exposure to a specific campus they're considering

**When Tier 4 is NOT worth it:**
- The family is stretching financially in hopes of admissions advantage (it almost certainly won't deliver)
- The student is doing it INSTEAD of more substantive options (research, internship, intensive job, personal project)
- The student is being pushed into it by parents and isn't enthusiastic
- The family thinks it provides "Harvard signal" — it doesn't

## Part 2: Grade-Level Specific Guidance

### Rising 10th Graders (Summer After 9th Grade)

This is the EXPLORATION summer. Students don't yet have AP scores, leadership tracks, or much of an academic identity. Most Tier 1 programs are not open to rising 10th-graders.

**What works:**
- Subject-deep summer at local university (community college courses, university outreach programs)
- Volunteer with sustained commitment that becomes the anchor (see volunteer brain file)
- Reading + personal project in a specific area of interest
- Local research outreach: cold-email 10-20 professors at a local university about helping in their lab. Even shadowing 4-6 hours/week is valuable — and FREE.
- Job (cashier, lifeguard, camp counselor) — admissions readers respect work experience
- TASS (rising 11th-only, but can apply for rising 11th summer to plan ahead)
- Mathcamp (some rising 10th get in)
- Fast-tracked language immersion (NSLI-Y is rising 11th+ but local programs work)
- CTY (eligible for some courses)

**What doesn't work yet:**
- Full Tier 1 programs (most require rising 11th or 12th)
- "Pre-college" Tier 4 programs marketed as "Harvard for high schoolers"

**The right framing for parents of rising 10th graders:** "summer of exploration + foundation-building. Don't optimize for admissions credential yet — that comes next year. Use this summer to figure out what your kid actually loves so you can target Tier 1 programs in the application that'll matter in 2 years."

### Rising 11th Graders (Summer After 10th Grade) — THIS IS THE WIFE'S USE CASE

This is the **PIVOTAL** summer. Most Tier 1 programs open up. Application deadlines are December-February of sophomore year (i.e., right now planning for next summer if the student is currently in 10th grade).

**What works at the highest level:**
- TASS (rising juniors specifically — this is THE flagship humanities program for sophomores)
- Stanford SUMaC (math)
- PROMYS, Ross, Mathcamp (math)
- Bank of America Student Leaders (open to rising 11th and 12th)
- NSLI-Y (rising 11th)
- Local research at a research university — sophomores who land lab positions this summer can have 2 years of mentored research by college application time, which is enormously powerful
- Smithsonian internships (some take rising 11th)
- Subject-specific selective summer programs (Iowa Young Writers, Stanford genomics tracks)

**What's not yet open:**
- RSI, MITES, MOSTEC, Garcia (rising 12th)
- NIH HS-SIP (rising 12th)
- Clark Scholars (rising 12th)
- TASP (rising 12th — TASS is the rising 11th version)

**The strongest profile move for current sophomores planning summer:**
1. Identify 2-3 Tier 1 programs aligned to interest. Apply to all (most applications take 4-12 hours each).
2. Independently reach out to 10-20 professors at local universities (UW for Seattle metro) about lab opportunities. This is FREE and can be transformative.
3. As a backup, apply to one Tier 2 or Tier 3 program in field of interest.
4. Have a paid job lined up in case the selective options don't pan out.
5. **Don't put all eggs in Harvard SSP**. Save the $13,000.

**Application timeline for current 10th graders aiming for summer of rising 11th:**
- October-November (10th grade): research programs, ask teachers for recommendations
- November (10th grade): NSLI-Y deadline (early November!)
- December (10th grade): start drafting essays
- January (10th grade): RSI, TASS, TASP deadlines
- February (10th grade): MITES, SSP, Garcia, Bank of America Student Leaders deadlines
- March-April (10th grade): Mathcamp, Ross, PROMYS deadlines (later math camps)
- April-May (10th grade): admit decisions for most Tier 1
- May-June: secure backup plans (job, local research, paid program)
- June-August: PROGRAM

### Rising 12th Graders (Summer After 11th Grade)

This is the LAST LARGE WINDOW before applications are due. Programs done this summer go on the Common App as activities. The summer is short — June-August — and the Common App opens August 1.

**What works at the highest level:**
- RSI, MITES, MOSTEC (the absolute highest-end signals for STEM applicants targeting MIT/Caltech/Stanford)
- Garcia, Clark Scholars, NIH HS-SIP
- TASP (the humanities equivalent of RSI)
- SSP (astrophysics or biochem)
- Continued lab research from sophomore-year start
- Self-directed research culminating in a paper, prize entry (Regeneron, JSHS), or publication
- Substantial paid internship in field of interest
- Substantial personal project (start a nonprofit IF it's real, write a book, build a startup, run a major event)

**What's a missed opportunity:**
- A Tier 4 pre-college program when the student could be doing research, internship, or a substantive personal project
- "I'll just do another year of camp" if they've already done one
- Paid international service trip (voluntourism — see volunteer brain file)

**The strongest profile move for rising seniors:**
- Tier 1 program in field of intended major
- Continue lab work from previous summers
- Begin Regeneron / JSHS submission preparation
- Lock in counselor + teacher recommendations
- Start common app essay drafts

### Post-12th Grade (Summer Before College)

This is RELAX SUMMER for most. Light job + personal interest pursuits + family time. Don't over-program. The student needs rest before college.

For the small minority pursuing post-grad gap year: AmeriCorps, year abroad, structured gap year programs (Carpe Diem Education, Where There Be Dragons, Tinkering School, etc.).

## Part 3: Field-Specific Recommendations

### STEM-Bound Students (Engineering, CS, Math, Physical Sciences)

**Tier 1 priorities by year:**
- 10th: SUMaC (math), Mathcamp (math), CTY STEM courses, local research
- 11th: TASS (humanities backup), local research expanding into a research project, MITES Saturdays (year-round), early SUMaC/Mathcamp
- 12th: RSI, MITES, MOSTEC, Garcia, Clark Scholars, SSP, NIH HS-SIP

**The local-research move:** for STEM-bound students at any grade level, the single most underrated path is cold-emailing 10-20 professors at a nearby research university about lab opportunities. Most kids never try this. The acceptance rate (loosely defined as "professor responds and offers something") is around 10-20% if the email is well-crafted (specific to professor's research, offers concrete time commitment, includes a brief CV). This produces:
- Letter of recommendation from a professor (very strong)
- Continuity across summers (research that builds over years)
- Possible co-authorship on a paper or poster
- Direct exposure to graduate-level work

For the Wayfinder Engine: when a STEM-bound parent asks about summer programs, ALWAYS mention this. Most don't know about it.

### Pre-Med Students

**Tier 1 priorities:**
- 10th: Hospital junior volunteer (sustained), shadowing a physician, biology research at local university
- 11th: Continue hospital + add medical research, Bank of America Student Leaders if interested in healthcare policy, Princeton Summer Journalism (for med-writing types)
- 12th: NIH HS-SIP (biomedical research), continue clinical exposure, SSP biochem, MITES (if URM)

Pre-med uniquely benefits from CONTINUOUS clinical exposure across multiple summers. A student with 4 years of weekly hospital volunteering + summer-intensive shadowing + 2 summers of research is a much stronger profile than a student with one summer of "medical pre-college."

### Humanities & Social Sciences Students

**Tier 1 priorities:**
- 10th: Iowa Young Writers (open to talented sophomores), Sewanee, local journalism (school paper, local nonprofit communications)
- 11th: TASS (the marquee humanities program), Iowa Young Writers, Princeton Summer Journalism (rising seniors only — apply for rising 12th)
- 12th: TASP, Princeton Summer Journalism, JCamp, Mellon Mays pre-programs, NSLI-Y (language immersion)

The local-newspaper move: many students don't realize they can intern at a local newspaper, alternative weekly, or magazine starting at 16. Pitch story ideas, become a fact-checker, write columns. Often unpaid but produces published clips.

### Business, Economics, Pre-Law Students

**Tier 1 priorities:**
- Bank of America Student Leaders (the cleanest signal for high school business interest)
- Wharton LBW (paid Tier 3)
- Local internships at small businesses, nonprofits, or campaigns
- Model UN summer conferences (HMUN, NHSMUN — pay-to-attend but resume-relevant)
- Junior Achievement's Company Program if available
- Coca-Cola Scholars if the student qualifies

Business pre-college programs (Wharton, Tuck, etc.) are mostly Tier 3 — paid, real, but not selective in a way that confers strong admissions signal.

### Arts Students (Visual, Performing, Music)

This is a specialized track with its OWN selectivity hierarchy:

**Tier 1 (highly selective, free or scholarshipped):**
- Interlochen (residential arts academy summer)
- New York State Summer School of the Arts (NYSSSA) — free for NY residents
- Tanglewood Music Center (orchestral)
- Aspen Music Festival
- BUTI (Boston University Tanglewood Institute)
- Brevard Music Center (composition + performance)
- Walnut Hill School Summer
- Idyllwild Arts (residential)
- California State Summer School for the Arts (CSSSA) — free for CA residents
- Governor's Schools for the Arts (varies by state, usually free for residents)

**For arts students, audition matters far more than application essays.** Prepare audition materials with a respected teacher months in advance.

### Entrepreneurship / Startup Students

- LaunchX (Tier 3, paid) — startup-building
- BizGen (Tier 3, paid)
- Real-world: build a business, generate revenue, document growth

## Part 4: Free vs Paid — The Selectivity-vs-Cost Map

Plot any program on these two axes and you can see immediately what tier it sits in:

```
                    SELECTIVITY (acceptance %)
                     <10%        10-30%      30-60%      60%+
              ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────
   FREE       │ Tier 1      Tier 1/2    rare        n/a
              │ (RSI, TASP)  (TASS, NSLI-Y, NIH)
              │
   < $1K      │ Tier 1     Tier 1      rare         rare
              │ (Mathcamp aid)
              │
   $1-3K      │ rare       Tier 2      Tier 2/3     Tier 3
              │ (Iowa Y.W.) (Notre Dame) (most)
              │
   $3-7K      │ rare        Tier 3       Tier 3      Tier 4
              │             (SUMaC, BU RISE) (most)
              │
   $7K+       │ rare        Tier 3       Tier 3/4    Tier 4
              │             (CTY)        (Cornell)   (Harvard SSP)
```

**The key insight:** there's an INVERSE relationship between selectivity and cost in this market. The most selective programs are subsidized by donors / NSF / Department of State / Department of Defense / corporations. The least selective programs are pay-to-attend.

When you see a program that's expensive AND selective, look closely — usually the selectivity is on a specific track within the program (e.g., Brown's Leadership Institute is selective; Brown's general Pre-College isn't), or the selectivity is overstated.

## Part 5: Application Process Detail

### Common Application Components

Most Tier 1 / Tier 2 programs require:
- Online application form (transcript, test scores if available, demographic info)
- 2-4 essays (varies by program)
- 1-2 letters of recommendation (usually 1 academic, 1 personal/character)
- Sometimes: a parent permission form, a self-reported GPA / class rank

### Essay Quality Matters Enormously

Tier 1 program essays are read by people who are looking for genuine intellectual curiosity, not boilerplate. The strongest essays:
- Show specific intellectual engagement with the program's subject
- Reveal personality, voice, and how the student thinks
- Don't repeat the resume — that's already in the application
- Don't try to "sound impressive" — they BE specific

### Letters of Recommendation

Choose carefully:
- For STEM programs: a math/science teacher who knows your work well
- For humanities: an English/history/social-studies teacher
- For research-focused: ideally a teacher who has supervised independent work, OR a mentor / coach who can speak to character
- Brief the recommender. Send them a one-pager about why you want this program and what you'd contribute. Their letter will be 10x better.

### Timeline Discipline

The biggest mistake: applying late. Most Tier 1 applications are due January-February for the following summer. By March, most are CLOSED. Don't tell yourself you'll apply in April — most things you can apply to in April are Tier 3-4.

### Financial Aid

Almost every Tier 1-3 program has need-based financial aid. The application is usually required at the same time as the program application.

- **SSP**: 60%+ of attendees on aid; can be free including travel
- **Mathcamp**: extensive aid; often >50% on full or partial aid
- **PROMYS**: full need-based aid; explicitly designed to make program accessible
- **Brown Pre-College**: limited aid via specific programs
- **Stanford SPCS**: very limited aid (this is a known weakness of the program)
- **Harvard SSP**: limited aid (~15% of attendees on partial aid)

Don't assume your family doesn't qualify. The financial aid bar is generally generous for these programs.

## Part 6: Common Mistakes — Ranked

### Mistake 1: Confusing Open-Enrollment Pre-College for Selective Admission

The single most common parent error: paying $7,000-13,000 for "Harvard / Brown / Cornell summer program" and believing it's a selective signal. It's almost never selective at the headline level. Admissions readers know this. Your kid can attend Harvard SSP and that's nice but it confers very little admissions advantage.

### Mistake 2: Ignoring Local Research Opportunities

The single most underrated path: cold-emailing professors at local universities. FREE, often more substantive than any pre-college program, produces a recommendation letter from a real researcher. Most parents and students never try this because it requires the student to take initiative.

### Mistake 3: Applying Late

Tier 1 deadlines are mostly January-February. If you start researching in March, you've missed everything.

### Mistake 4: One-and-Done Mentality

A student who attends Stanford SUMaC for one summer signals interest in math. A student who returns to PROMYS for a second summer or does Math Olympiad alongside SUMaC signals genuine commitment. Continuity matters.

### Mistake 5: Pursuing Programs With No Through-Line

A student with one summer of pre-med, one of journalism, one of business signals indecision. A student with three years of escalating engagement in one or two coherent areas signals depth.

### Mistake 6: Burnout Programming

A student doing Tier 1 program for 6 weeks + 4 weeks at a different camp + a job + 3 college visits in one summer is overscheduled and probably won't perform well at any of it. Pick ONE intensive thing per summer plus light supplementary activity.

### Mistake 7: Following Friends Instead of Following Interest

Many students apply to whatever their friends are doing. This often means everyone applies to the same Tier 4 program (it's marketed at the school), no one applies to Tier 1 programs they didn't know about. The student loses the chance to differentiate.

### Mistake 8: Ignoring the Personal Project Option

Some of the strongest application narratives come from students who didn't attend a "program" at all but spent the summer building, creating, or studying something independently. A kid who built a startup that generated $5,000 in revenue, or who self-taught organic chemistry from a college textbook and got a 5 on the AP, or who wrote a 100-page novel — these are powerful.

### Mistake 9: Misjudging Acceptance Rates

Many parents (and counselors) overstate selectivity. "Northwestern Pre-College has only 30% acceptance" — actually, that's the headline rate; for many tracks within Northwestern Pre-College it's 60-70%+. Read each program's specifics carefully, not the marketing.

### Mistake 10: Treating "What did you do this summer?" as a Common App Question

It is — students fill out their Common App activities section and summer is part of that. But how you describe your summer matters more than what you did. A student who spent the summer at a Tier 4 pre-college and frames it as "I learned about myself and explored my interests" is weak. A student who spent the summer at a part-time job + reading + a personal coding project and frames it specifically with concrete accomplishments looks strong.

## Part 7: WA / Seattle-Metro Specific Options (Dan's Family Priority)

### University of Washington Programs

- **UW Robinson Center for Young Scholars** — Highly capable youth, longstanding program. Various offerings.
  - Source: robinsoncenter.uw.edu

- **UW Summer Quest** — Pre-college summer programs at UW. Various tracks.

## Part 8: For the Wayfinder Engine — Steering Calibration

This section is specifically for how the Engine should handle "summer programs" questions. **Engine has been generic in past responses. Do not be generic. Be specific, calibrated, and database-aware.**

### When a Parent Asks "What Summer Program Should My X-Grader Do?"

Step 1: Acknowledge the question with confidence — this is one of Wayfinder's strongest areas.
Step 2: Ask for or use whatever context exists (grade, interest, budget, location).
Step 3: Surface 3-5 SPECIFIC programs by name, with why-they-fit reasoning, calibrated to the student's likely interests and the grade-appropriate program tier.
Step 4: Direct the parent to the Programs tool ("we have 800+ curated programs filterable by grade, state, format, cost, selectivity — click Programs in the sidebar to filter").
Step 5: Volunteer to do deeper research or strategic planning if they want.

### Generic Phrases to AVOID

- "Consider research, internships, or pre-college programs"
- "There are many great options out there"
- "It depends on your interests"
- "I'd encourage you to explore" (without specifics)
- "Make sure to apply early" (without dates)

### Specific Phrases to USE

- "For a current sophomore interested in [field], the most strategic options are TASS (humanities), SUMaC (math), or local research at UW. Applications are due January-February."
- "Most parents come in thinking 'Harvard SSP' is the answer — it's actually one of the lower-leverage choices. Here's what actually moves the needle..."
- "We have 800+ programs in our database — let me filter for sophomore + WA + research-focused..."
- "The free Tier 1 programs (RSI, TASP, MITES, NSLI-Y) are dramatically more selective and impressive than the paid Tier 3 programs that get more marketing"
- "Cold-emailing 15-20 UW professors about lab opportunities is the highest-ROI move most students never try"

### When the Student is in 10th Grade

Most Tier 1 programs are not yet open. Lead with TASS (rising 11th flagship), SUMaC, Mathcamp/PROMYS, NSLI-Y, local research, Iowa Young Writers. Frame: "this is the planning summer for what you'll apply to next year."

### When the Student is in 11th Grade

Lead with the highest-prestige programs they're newly eligible for: RSI, MITES, MOSTEC, SSP, Garcia, NIH HS-SIP, TASP, Clark Scholars, Princeton Summer Journalism. Plus the ongoing-research narrative if they have any.

### Cost Sensitivity

If the family signals budget constraint, immediately surface free-or-low-cost options:
- Tier 1 free programs (RSI, TASP, TASS, MITES, NSLI-Y, NIH HS-SIP, Bank of America Student Leaders)
- Local research (free)
- Local jobs (paid)
- State Governor's Schools (free for in-state residents)
- CTY scholarships
- Mathcamp / PROMYS / Ross full need-based aid

Don't let cost-conscious families be steered to Tier 4 paid programs.

### When the Parent is Confused About Tier

Be honest: "Harvard Secondary School Program is open enrollment — paying $13K to attend doesn't carry strong admissions weight. The selective Harvard signal you're thinking of is a different category entirely." This is a teaching moment that builds trust.

### Always Mention the Programs Module

End with: "I can give you a full curated list filtered to your son's grade and interests — click Programs in the sidebar and use the filters." This is the "use the database" pivot Dan wants.
