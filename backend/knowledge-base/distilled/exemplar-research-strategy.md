## How to think about high school research

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's intended major, target schools, current academic profile.
- Cross-reference verified Wayfinder programs DB.

The honest framework:

**1. Research expectations vary by school and major.**

- **MIT, Stanford, Caltech, Princeton, top research universities:** research signal is increasingly important, particularly for STEM majors. Many admits have research engagement (formal or informal).
- **Ivy+ schools (HYP):** research is one of several strong signals. Not required but contributes if real.
- **Top LACs (Williams, Amherst, Pomona):** research less expected at admission stage; intellectual engagement more important.
- **For pre-med specifically:** research becoming more expected at top medical schools, so undergrad research starts mattering for top pre-med-pipeline schools.

**2. Real research vs "research"**

- **Real research:** sustained engagement (months minimum), original work or meaningful contribution to ongoing project, mentor relationship with academic faculty or industry researcher, deliverable (paper, poster, conference talk, shipped tool).
- **"Research":** a one-week shadowing program, a "research" summer camp where you didn't do original work, claiming research because you read papers.

AOs distinguish. The signal is what you produced and what you contributed.

**3. Research routes**

- **Formal university summer research programs** (RSI, Clark, MITES, SSP, university-specific programs)
- **Cold-emailing professors directly**
- **Paid mentorship platforms** (Polygence, Lumiere, Pioneer Academics)
- **Independent research with school faculty support**
- **Industry research internships** (rare for HS but available)

## The competitive university summer research programs

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Cross-reference verified Wayfinder programs DB for current applications.

**Most competitive (~5% acceptance, full national recognition):**

- **RSI (Research Science Institute) at MIT** — 6-week intensive STEM research program; ~80 students annually; free; widely recognized as top HS research program
- **MITES (Minority Introduction to Engineering and Science) at MIT** — engineering-focused, for underrepresented students; free
- **Clark Scholars at Texas Tech** — interdisciplinary research with faculty mentor; ~12 scholars; free; covers travel
- **SSP (Summer Science Program)** — astrophysics or biochemistry research; free or sliding scale; multiple sites
- **TASP (Telluride Association Summer Program)** — humanities-focused; free; intensive seminars (less research-heavy but intellectually rigorous)
- **Garcia Program at Stony Brook** — materials science research; free
- **Concord Review Summer Program** — humanities/history research

**Highly competitive but accessible (~15-25% acceptance):**

- **COSMOS (California State Summer School for Mathematics and Science)** — multiple UC campuses; STEM research clusters; in-state CA priority but accepts OOS
- **Iowa Young Writers' Studio** — creative writing; not research per se but intellectual immersion
- **Stanford Mathematics Camp (SUMaC)** — math-focused
- **Ross Mathematics Program / PROMYS / HCSSiM / MathCamp** — pure math intensives

## Cold-emailing professors

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

The most underused research route. Many professors are open to high school researchers if approached well.

**The framework:**

1. **Research first.** Identify professors at universities you have access to (local universities, family connections to specific universities). Read their recent papers. Understand their work area.

2. **Email 10-30 professors.** High volume because most won't respond.

3. **Subject line:** specific. "High school student interested in [your specific research area]" — not "Research opportunity."

4. **Email body (3-4 paragraphs max):**
   - Para 1: Introduce yourself briefly. Year in school, school name. One sentence on academic interest.
   - Para 2: Specific reference to their work. "I read your 2024 paper on [X] and was particularly interested in [specific finding]." This proves you've actually engaged with their work.
   - Para 3: What you're seeking. "I'd love to learn more about your research and explore whether there's any way I could contribute as a [summer/year-long] research assistant. I'm willing to do whatever entry-level work would be useful — reading group, literature reviews, basic data work, lab cleanup."
   - Para 4: Brief credentials and offer to send CV. Schedule offer.

5. **Follow up if no response after 2 weeks.** One polite follow-up. Then move on.

**Realistic outcomes:**

- Most professors don't respond
- Some respond with "no thanks"
- A few respond with "let's talk"
- Of those, some lead to actual research opportunities

The high-volume approach works because you only need ONE yes.

## Paid mentorship platforms

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

Several paid platforms connect high school students with PhD-student or faculty mentors for research projects:

- **Polygence:** ~$3,000-$6,000 for ~10-session mentorship producing a research paper or project. Sessions 1-on-1 with PhD-student mentor. Quality varies by mentor.
- **Lumiere Education:** similar model
- **Pioneer Academics:** more academic-focused, partnership with Oberlin College
- **Horizon Academics:** research mentorship

**The honest framework:**

These platforms can produce real outputs (a research paper, a project) when student and mentor are well-matched. But:
- Quality of mentorship varies dramatically
- The platform "research paper" output is often not peer-reviewed; it's a portfolio piece, not academic publication
- AOs at top schools may discount paid mentorship outputs vs. independently-acquired research opportunities
- Cost can be significant ($3K-$6K per project)

**When paid platforms make sense:**

- Local university research is genuinely unavailable
- Cold-emailing has produced no responses
- The student has specific interest in a niche topic without local mentor availability
- Family can afford the cost without significant strain

**When they don't:**

- Family financial strain
- Student could pursue local research opportunities with effort
- Student wants the prestige signal of "research" without doing the work

## Original research and publication routes

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**

For high school students with genuine research output:

**Where to publish / present research as a HS student:**

- **Concord Review** — humanities and history research; very competitive; published authors recognized by colleges
- **Journal of Emerging Investigators (JEI)** — STEM research by HS students; legitimate peer review
- **JEI for Young Researchers** — undergraduate-aged
- **Journal of Student Research** — multidisciplinary HS research
- **Research Archive of Rising Scholars** — HS-level
- **Posters at university research symposiums** — many universities allow HS researchers to present at undergraduate research conferences
- **Local science fairs → state science fairs → ISEF (Intel International Science and Engineering Fair)** — competitive but real recognition

**Wayfinder workflow:** cross-reference verified Wayfinder programs/competitions DB for current application periods.

**The honest framework on publication:**

- A real publication in JEI or Concord Review is a strong signal
- "Co-author" status on a faculty paper if you contributed substantively is a strong signal
- Posters / conference presentations are moderate signals
- Self-published "research papers" (no peer review, no editorial process) are weak signals

## Research narrative for the application

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Cross-reference exemplar-essay-intellectual.md for intellectual essay framing.

If you have research engagement, how to present it:

**In activity list:**

- Specific role and accomplishments. "Research Assistant — Prof. Smith Lab, UW Bioengineering" with description "Co-investigator on [specific project]; conducted [specific work]; contributed to [specific output]."

**In essays:**

- Research often anchors intellectual essays (Common App Prompt 6, school-specific intellectual prompts)
- Show specific intellectual engagement, not just "I did research"
- Cite specific aspects of the work that captivated you
- Demonstrate what the research taught you about how the field works

**In recommendation letters:**

- The research mentor's letter is often the most valuable supplemental letter
- Brief them well; provide specific things you've done

**In supplemental essays:**

- "Why this school" essays for research-heavy universities should connect your research interest to specific labs/faculty at the school
