## How to think about choosing a Common App prompt

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's saved profile (background, key ECs, life events mentioned in prior conversation, intended major).
- Surface what's most distinctive about them — what would AOs find unique vs what's generic.
- Pull target school list — the personal statement does more work for some applications than others.
- Recommend Essay Reviewer for any drafting work.

**The 2024-2025 Common App prompts (will likely persist into 2025-2026):**

1. **Background, identity, interest, talent.** "Some students have a background, identity, interest, or talent that is so meaningful they believe their application would be incomplete without it. If this sounds like you, then please share your story."

2. **Lessons from obstacles.** "The lessons we take from obstacles we encounter can be fundamental to later success. Recount a time when you faced a challenge, setback, or failure. How did it affect you, and what did you learn from the experience?"

3. **Questioning a belief or idea.** "Reflect on a time when you questioned or challenged a belief or idea. What prompted your thinking? What was the outcome?"

4. **Solving a problem.** "Reflect on something that someone has done for you that has made you happy or thankful in a surprising way. How has this gratitude affected or motivated you?"

5. **Period of personal growth.** "Discuss an accomplishment, event, or realization that sparked a period of personal growth and a new understanding of yourself or others."

6. **Captivating topic.** "Describe a topic, idea, or concept you find so engaging that it makes you lose all track of time. Why does it captivate you? What or who do you turn to when you want to learn more?"

7. **Free choice.** "Share an essay on any topic of your choice. It can be one you've already written, one that responds to a different prompt, or one of your own design."

**The honest truth about prompt choice:** the prompt matters less than the topic. The same fundamental story can be written under prompts 1, 2, 5, 6, or 7 with appropriate framing. Prompt 7 (free choice) gives maximum flexibility. The wrong move is to pick a prompt and then search for a topic that fits; the right move is to identify the ONE thing about you that AOs absolutely need to know to understand who you are, then frame it under the prompt that best showcases it.

**Topic identification — the diagnostic exercise:**

Ask yourself: if my application gets read at midnight after 200 other applications, what's the ONE thing the AO has to remember about me afterward? The Common App essay is your chance to make sure they remember the right thing.

Three categories of Common App topics that work:

1. **Identity-rooted** — something about your background, family, culture, or context that has shaped who you are. These work when there's a specific lived experience and authentic reflection, not when they're claims of identity-as-virtue.

2. **Intellectual/passion-rooted** — something you genuinely love or have built. These work when the love is specific (a particular obsession, a particular project, a particular moment of discovery) and translates into how you see the world.

3. **Growth/insight-rooted** — a specific moment when you understood something about yourself or the world that you didn't understand before. These work when the moment is grounded in a real scene and the insight is genuinely yours, not a Hallmark-card platitude.

**Three topic categories that DON'T work** (despite being commonly attempted):

1. **The athletic injury / loss recovery** — overdone and rarely as profound as the writer believes. Unless the angle is genuinely fresh, skip.
2. **The mission trip realization** — almost always reads as performative empathy with subtext of class privilege. Unless the framing engages with the privilege honestly, skip.
3. **The single-event tragedy** — a parent's illness, a friend's accident — without sustained reflection beyond the event. Single-event essays can work but require unusual depth of reflection that most 17-year-olds haven't yet developed.

## How to think about Prompt 1 (background, identity, interest, talent)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's saved profile — what backgrounds, identities, interests, talents are documented.
- Pull `intl/korea/strategies/` if user is Korean.
- Recommend Essay Reviewer with explicit voice/authenticity scoring.

Prompt 1 is the most commonly chosen prompt and where the most generic essays live. The trap: students treat it as an opportunity to claim identity-as-credential ("I'm a Korean-American first-generation immigrant student"). Identity claims without specific scenes and authentic reflection read as resume-padding for diversity points — particularly post-SFFA, where AOs have been trained to look for performative identity invocation.

The right way to write Prompt 1:

1. **Pick ONE specific dimension** of background/identity/interest/talent. Not "I'm Korean-American AND first-gen AND a violinist AND an immigrant kid." One.
2. **Anchor it in scenes.** Not "growing up Korean-American taught me X" but "the moment I was eight years old at a Lunar New Year dinner where my grandmother [specific scene with specific sensory details]."
3. **Show, then reflect.** Show the scene first, then let the reflection emerge. Reflection-without-scene reads as abstract; scene-without-reflection reads as memoir.
4. **Make the universal specific.** The best Prompt 1 essays take something specific to your life and show why the rest of us should care — not by claiming universal relevance but by writing the specific so vividly that universals emerge.

## How to think about Prompt 2 (lessons from obstacles)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's saved profile and prior conversation context for life events that fit.
- Recommend Essay Reviewer.

Prompt 2 is where students often default to "athletic injury" or "academic setback" stories. Both are workable but require more depth than students typically deliver.

The honest truth about Prompt 2:

1. **The obstacle isn't the story.** What you learned IS the story. AOs can read 50 essays about ACL injuries; what they're scoring is whether the lesson is genuine and sufficiently specific.

2. **The obstacle should be appropriately scaled.** Don't write Prompt 2 about something genuinely small (a B on a test, a fight with a friend) unless your reflection is unusually deep. Don't write Prompt 2 about something genuinely catastrophic (parent's illness, family violence) unless you've actually processed it and aren't relying on the topic to do emotional work for the essay.

3. **The reflection has to be earned by the work you've done.** "I learned that resilience is important" is a Hallmark conclusion. "I learned that resilience for me means [specific specific specific] — which I now apply when I [specific scene]" is earned reflection.

4. **The "what I learned" should change how you behave, not just how you think.** Show evidence of the learned lesson in subsequent action.

**The trap to avoid:** the redemption arc with a too-clean ending. Real obstacles don't always resolve neatly. Essays that feel too tidy read as inauthentic. Better to land on "and I'm still figuring it out, but here's what I've started doing differently" than "and now I'm completely transformed."

## How to think about Prompt 3 (questioning a belief or idea)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's intended major and ECs — Prompt 3 often ties to academic/intellectual journey.
- Recommend Essay Reviewer.

Prompt 3 is underchosen and powerful when done well. It signals intellectual honesty, which is exactly what selective AOs are scoring for. The risk: if the belief questioned is generic or the reflection shallow, the essay reads as performance of intellectual sophistication.

The honest framework for Prompt 3:

1. **The belief should be one you actually held, not a strawman.** Essays where the writer "questions" a belief they clearly never held read as setup-for-conclusion. AOs detect this immediately.

2. **The questioning should have stakes.** If the belief was peripheral to your life, the questioning is academic. If the belief was central — to your family, your community, your identity, your work — the questioning carries weight.

3. **The questioning should be unfinished or have ongoing stakes.** Conclusions like "and now I see clearly" are usually overclaim. Better: "I'm still working out what to do with the new view, but here's how it's changing how I [specific specific]."

4. **It's okay if you didn't fully change your mind.** The prompt asks about questioning, not converting. An essay that ends with "I still hold the belief, but I now hold it with more humility because of [the questioning]" is a strong Prompt 3 outcome.

**Avoid:** generic political topics treated as personal journey. AOs read 200 essays per season questioning either capitalism or wokeness; both have become tropes. If you're writing about a political belief, the questioning has to be deeply personal and specific to your life, not abstract policy debate.

## How to think about Prompt 4 (gratitude essay)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user profile for relationships and influences.
- Recommend Essay Reviewer.

Prompt 4 was added to the Common App relatively recently and is the most underutilized strong prompt. Students often skip it because "gratitude" sounds soft. AOs love it when done well because it surfaces character and relationship quality.

The honest framework for Prompt 4:

1. **The gratitude should be for something specific, not general.** Not "I'm thankful for my parents." Yes "I'm thankful that my mother taught me to cook the way her mother taught her, even though I didn't realize at the time it was the most important thing she could have given me." Specificity is everything.

2. **The "surprising way" framing matters.** Re-read the prompt: "made you happy or thankful in a SURPRISING way." The surprise is the hinge. The essay should show how gratitude landed unexpectedly, not how you were always grateful.

3. **The "how has it affected or motivated you" demands evidence in current behavior.** Show how the gratitude has changed how you live, not just how you feel.

4. **The person you're grateful TO matters less than what you're grateful FOR.** The essay isn't a tribute to the person; it's a meditation on what the gift was and how it's reshaped you.

This prompt rewards writers with quiet observational skill. If you can capture small specific moments and find their significance, this is your prompt.

## How to think about Prompt 5 (period of personal growth)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user profile for major life chapters or transitions.
- Recommend Essay Reviewer.

Prompt 5 is the most flexible "tell me about a meaningful moment in your life" prompt. Wide latitude, but that wideness means students often default to clichés (summer transformative experience, sports captain leadership growth).

The honest framework for Prompt 5:

1. **A "period" can be longer than students assume.** It can be a multi-year process of growth, not just a discrete event. Often the multi-year framing produces more depth than the single-moment framing.

2. **The "new understanding of yourself or others" is the deliverable.** What did you learn that you didn't know before? The essay's coherence depends on that being clear.

3. **The growth should feel earned by the experience, not pasted onto it.** Show the messiness of the growth process — the false starts, the resistance, the partial understanding before fuller understanding.

4. **Avoid Hallmark conclusions.** "And now I'm a more empathetic person" is empty. Better: "And now when I find myself in [specific scene], I do [specific action] differently than I would have before."

## How to think about Prompt 6 (captivating topic)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user's intended major, ECs, intellectual interests.
- Cross-reference verified Wayfinder programs/competitions in the topic area.
- Recommend Essay Reviewer.

Prompt 6 is gold for students with genuine intellectual passion. The trap: students who pick this prompt without genuine deep engagement produce essays that read as performance of intellectualism.

The honest framework for Prompt 6:

1. **The topic should be one you've actually engaged with deeply, not one that sounds impressive.** AOs can tell the difference. If you've read three philosophy books and want to write Prompt 6 about epistemology, your essay will be paper-thin compared to a student who's read fifty books and run a philosophy club.

2. **Show the engagement, don't claim it.** Specific examples of the work you've done in the topic — not "I love quantum physics" but "the moment I realized that the double-slit experiment isn't just a measurement problem but suggests something genuinely strange about reality, I [specific action you took]."

3. **The "who or what do you turn to" demand is part of the prompt.** Many essays skip this. Don't. Surface the books, mentors, communities, online resources — show you're embedded in the field, not just curious about it.

4. **This is the prompt where intellectual depth shows most directly.** If you've genuinely gone deep on something, this is your prompt and the essay will write itself. If you haven't gone deep on anything specific yet, this isn't your prompt.

**Wayfinder integration:** if the user is writing Prompt 6 on a topic adjacent to their saved major/ECs, surface verified competitions, programs, communities they could engage with that would deepen the topic before writing the essay. "You're writing about computational biology — Wayfinder's verified DB has X program at UW that would give you concrete material to write about" is high-value.

## How to think about Prompt 7 (free choice)

**[SLM data retrieval for this exemplar]**
- Pull user profile and any prior essay drafts.
- Recommend Essay Reviewer.

Prompt 7 is the prompt that lets you submit anything. Most strategic students use Prompt 7 because it removes the artificial constraint of fitting a topic to a prompt. The risk: with no prompt to organize around, weak essays meander.

The honest framework for Prompt 7:

1. **You need a clear coherent topic.** "Anything goes" doesn't mean "no structure." Your essay should have a clear protagonist (you), a clear hook, a clear arc, a clear point.

2. **Most strong Prompt 7 essays are recombinations of Prompt 1, 5, or 6 material with fewer constraints.** If your essay would fit one of the other prompts cleanly, consider whether picking that prompt would help give your essay a clearer structural anchor.

3. **Use Prompt 7 when your essay genuinely doesn't fit the others.** Genuinely unique structures (an essay-as-list, an essay-as-letter, an essay-as-conversation) are appropriate for Prompt 7 because the form would feel forced under another prompt.

4. **Don't over-experiment.** Creative form should serve content. Most students who try gimmicky structures for Prompt 7 produce weaker essays than they would have with conventional form. The form should emerge from what you're trying to say, not impose itself.

## Common App essay common failure patterns

When user shares a draft (or describes their planned content), watch for:

1. **The accomplishment essay disguised as personal statement.** Lists achievements without revealing personhood. AOs can read your activities section for that — the essay should reveal something the activities can't show.

2. **The trauma essay without depth.** Heavy topic with surface-level reflection. Reads as topic-as-shortcut.

3. **The hero-helping-others essay.** "I went to Africa and learned how lucky I am." Performative empathy with subtext of privilege. Avoid unless honestly engaging the privilege.

4. **The political/topical essay used as identity.** Essays that argue a political view without grounding the view in personal life read as advocacy, not personal statement.

5. **The "as I write this essay" meta-essay.** Self-aware essays about writing essays rarely work. Skip the meta.

6. **The list-of-traits essay.** "I'm passionate, determined, curious, and resilient." Telling, not showing.

7. **The polished-but-empty essay.** Reads beautifully but doesn't reveal anything specific or memorable about the writer. Voice without content.

For each failure pattern, the response should: name the pattern, show why it doesn't work, give the user a specific revision direction, recommend Essay Reviewer for further drafting work.
