## Big Ideas

1. **Voter turnout in the US is comparatively low — typically 55–65% in presidential elections, 35–45% in midterms.** Multiple structural factors depress turnout: registration burdens, weekday elections, voter ID requirements, complicated ballots. Demographic patterns show stark differences: older voters, more educated voters, higher-income voters, white voters all vote at higher rates than younger, less educated, lower-income, minority voters.
2. **Political parties serve essential functions even as voters distrust them.** Recruitment of candidates, mobilization of voters, organization of government, articulation of policy positions, providing labels that simplify voter choice. Parties are weaker as organizations than they were in the early 20th century but more polarized in their positions.
3. **Interest groups represent diverse interests but exhibit "free rider" problems and resource imbalances.** Some groups (corporate, professional) are well-organized and well-funded; others (poor people, future generations, environment) face collective action problems. The pluralist model assumes balanced competition; reality often favors organized, resourced interests.
4. **Modern campaigns have become extraordinarily expensive — $14+ billion in 2020.** Campaign finance evolved through landmark cases: Buckley v. Valeo (1976) treated money as speech; McConnell v. FEC (2003) upheld parts of McCain-Feingold; Citizens United (2010) opened door to unlimited corporate/union spending through super PACs.
5. **Media has fragmented from the broadcast era's "common information environment" to today's polarized partisan ecosystems.** This affects political socialization, voter persuasion, and democratic deliberation. The shift from network TV news to cable + social media has had dramatic effects on political behavior.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Voter turnout:**
  - **US presidential elections (typical):** 55–65% of eligible voters.
  - **US midterm elections (typical):** 35–45%.
  - **Compared to other developed democracies:** US is among the lowest. Australia (compulsory voting) has 90%+; many European democracies 70-80%.
  - **Demographic patterns:** older > younger; more educated > less educated; higher-income > lower-income; white > Black/Hispanic (gap has narrowed).
- **Constitutional voting rights expansions:**
  - **15th Amendment (1870):** prohibited racial discrimination in voting (effectively undermined for nearly a century until Voting Rights Act 1965).
  - **17th Amendment (1913):** direct election of senators (previously elected by state legislatures).
  - **19th Amendment (1920):** women's suffrage.
  - **23rd Amendment (1961):** DC residents allowed to vote for president.
  - **24th Amendment (1964):** banned poll taxes in federal elections.
  - **26th Amendment (1971):** lowered voting age to 18 (response to Vietnam War — "old enough to die, old enough to vote").
- **Voting Rights Act (1965):**
  - Banned literacy tests and other discriminatory voting practices.
  - Established federal preclearance for voting changes in covered jurisdictions.
  - Section 5 (preclearance) struck down by Shelby County v. Holder (2013).
- **Models of voter behavior:**
  - **Party identification model:** voters vote for their party regardless of candidates or issues. Most consistent predictor of vote choice.
  - **Issue voting:** voters choose based on policy preferences alignment.
  - **Candidate-centered voting:** voters choose based on personal qualities of candidates.
  - **Retrospective voting:** voters evaluate the incumbent party based on past performance ("are you better off than 4 years ago?").
  - **Prospective voting:** voters choose based on what candidates promise to do in the future.
- **Political parties — major functions:**
  - **Candidate recruitment:** identifying and supporting candidates.
  - **Voter mobilization:** GOTV (get out the vote) operations.
  - **Organization of government:** majority party controls Congressional committees and leadership.
  - **Policy articulation:** developing and promoting policy positions.
  - **Labels:** simplifying voter choice through brand identification.
- **Major US party systems** (historical periods):
  - **First Party System (1790s–1820s):** Federalists vs Democratic-Republicans.
  - **Second Party System (1828–1854):** Democrats vs Whigs.
  - **Third Party System (1854–1896):** Republicans (anti-slavery, then post-CW) vs Democrats.
  - **Fourth Party System (1896–1932):** Republican dominance after McKinley defeated Bryan.
  - **Fifth Party System (1932–1968):** New Deal Democratic coalition vs Republicans.
  - **Sixth Party System (1968–present):** post-civil rights realignment; South shifted Republican; current era of partisan polarization.
- **Critical realignment:** dramatic shift in party support. Examples:
  - 1860 (Lincoln/Republican rise after Civil War).
  - 1932 (FDR/New Deal).
  - 1968 (post-Civil Rights, Republican Southern Strategy).
- **Third parties** in US:
  - **Difficult to win** under single-member plurality electoral system (Duverger's Law).
  - **Functions:** raise issues major parties ignore (often later co-opted), test new ideas, register protest votes.
  - **Notable third parties:** Whigs (became Republican), Populist Party (1892), Progressive (1912 — Theodore Roosevelt; 1924 — Robert La Follette), Reform (Ross Perot), Libertarian, Green.
  - **2000 election:** Ralph Nader's Green Party run arguably gave Florida (and election) to Bush over Gore.
- **Interest groups:**
  - Organizations that try to influence public policy.
  - **Functions:** policy advocacy, voter mobilization, candidate endorsement, lobbying, litigation, public education.
  - **Types:**
    - **Economic:** corporate (Chamber of Commerce), labor (AFL-CIO, individual unions), professional (American Medical Association, American Bar Association).
    - **Public interest:** environmental (Sierra Club), civil rights (NAACP, ACLU), consumer protection (Consumers Union).
    - **Single-issue:** NRA (gun rights), NARAL/Right to Life (abortion), AARP (seniors).
    - **Government:** state/local government associations; foreign government representation.
  - **Free rider problem:** people benefit from interest group success without joining or paying. Why labor unions and public interest groups struggle compared to corporate groups whose participants directly profit.
- **Lobbying:**
  - Direct contact with policymakers to influence policy.
  - Includes drafting legislation, providing research, testifying at hearings, building relationships.
  - Regulated by Lobbying Disclosure Act (1995) — registration, disclosure of activities and finances.
- **Iron triangles** (from Unit 2):
  - Stable relationships among Congressional committees, executive bureaucracy, and interest groups.
  - Each benefits from the others; outsiders have difficulty breaking in.
  - Example: defense procurement (Defense Department + Armed Services Committees + defense contractors).
- **Issue networks:**
  - More fluid alliances around specific issues.
  - Include think tanks, academics, media, multiple agencies, advocacy groups.
  - Increasingly the dominant pattern in modern policy-making.
- **Presidential elections — Electoral College:**
  - Each state has electors equal to its Congressional delegation (House + Senate).
  - 538 total electors; need 270 to win.
  - Most states (48) use winner-take-all (all electoral votes go to plurality winner).
  - Maine and Nebraska use congressional district allocation.
  - Possible to win popular vote but lose Electoral College (2000 Bush v. Gore; 2016 Trump v. Clinton).
- **Primary elections:**
  - **Closed primary:** only registered party members can vote.
  - **Open primary:** any voter can choose which party's primary to vote in.
  - **Caucuses:** in-person meetings where voters discuss and select candidates (used in some states historically; declining in use).
- **Campaign finance:**
  - **FECA (Federal Election Campaign Act, 1971):** original campaign finance regulation.
  - **Buckley v. Valeo (1976):** treated money as speech; struck down spending limits but upheld contribution limits.
  - **BCRA / McCain-Feingold (2002):** banned soft money to parties; restricted issue advertising near elections.
  - **Citizens United v. FEC (2010)** [REQUIRED CASE]: corporations and unions can spend unlimited amounts on independent political activity. Created super PACs.
  - **Super PACs:** independent expenditure committees that can raise and spend unlimited amounts but cannot coordinate directly with candidates.
- **Citizens United v. FEC (2010)** [REQUIRED CASE]:
  - Citizens United (a non-profit) wanted to air a film critical of Hillary Clinton during 2008 primaries, which would violate BCRA's restrictions on corporate spending near elections.
  - Supreme Court (5-4) ruled that political spending by corporations and unions is protected SPEECH under the First Amendment.
  - Government cannot ban political spending by corporations or unions for independent expenditures.
  - Created super PACs and dramatically expanded outside spending in elections.
- **Baker v. Carr (1962)** [REQUIRED CASE]:
  - Tennessee hadn't redrawn legislative districts since 1901 despite massive population shifts.
  - Charles Baker sued claiming malapportionment violated 14th Amendment Equal Protection.
  - Supreme Court ruled redistricting cases CAN be decided by federal courts (overruled prior "political question" doctrine).
  - Established the "**one person, one vote**" principle requiring roughly equal population among districts.
  - Foundation for all subsequent redistricting law.
- **Shaw v. Reno (1993)** [REQUIRED CASE]:
  - North Carolina drew a congressional district predominantly Black (12th district) to comply with Voting Rights Act.
  - Supreme Court ruled the bizarre shape suggested race was the predominant factor in redistricting, violating Equal Protection.
  - Established that racial gerrymandering (even to help minority representation) is constitutionally suspect.
  - Created tension between VRA goals (minority representation) and Equal Protection requirements (no racial classification).
- **Media:**
  - **Functions:** inform citizens, watchdog over government, agenda-setting, framing.
  - **Types:** broadcast (network TV, radio), cable, print (newspapers, magazines), digital (websites, social media).
  - **Evolution:**
    - **Pre-1980s:** dominance of three networks (ABC, CBS, NBC), few national newspapers (NYT, WSJ, WaPo). "Common information environment."
    - **Cable era (1980s–2000s):** CNN, MSNBC, Fox News fragment audience. Partisan-leaning networks emerge.
    - **Digital era (2000s–present):** internet, social media, mobile devices. Information abundance + polarization. Decline of traditional gatekeepers.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why US voter turnout is comparatively low.** Multiple factors:
  - **Registration burden:** many countries automatically register citizens; US requires individual registration with state-level requirements.
  - **Weekday voting:** Tuesday voting (set by 1845 federal law for agricultural reasons) excludes those with inflexible work schedules.
  - **Voter ID requirements:** state-level photo ID requirements add cost/effort.
  - **Single-member districts and Electoral College:** in non-competitive states/districts, voters' votes don't affect outcomes (rational ignorance).
  - **Frequency of elections:** US has many more elections (federal, state, local, primaries, special elections) than other democracies. Voter fatigue.
  - **Inconvenient hours:** some polling places open only during work hours.
  - **Compare:** Australia mandates voting (90%+ turnout). Most European democracies have automatic registration, weekend voting, or proportional representation.
- **Sociological factors driving turnout disparities:**
  - **Age:** older voters turn out at higher rates (~70% for 65+; ~40% for 18-24).
  - **Education:** college-educated turn out at much higher rates than high school or less.
  - **Income:** higher income → higher turnout.
  - **Race:** historically lower turnout among Black and Hispanic voters; gap has narrowed but persists.
  - **Marital status:** married voters turn out at higher rates than single.
  - **Civic engagement:** voters who join civic organizations turn out at higher rates.
- **Political party realignments:**
  - **1860:** rise of Republican Party (anti-slavery), end of Whigs.
  - **1896:** Republican dominance with William McKinley defeat of William Jennings Bryan.
  - **1932:** New Deal coalition (FDR Democrats: urban Northern + Southern white + immigrant ethnic + Black).
  - **1968:** Republican Southern Strategy. Democratic urban coalition splits over civil rights, Vietnam.
  - **2016:** Trump coalition (working-class white + rural). Possible new realignment.
- **Electoral system effects on parties:**
  - **Single-member plurality (US):** tends to two parties (Duverger's Law). Geographic concentration of voters required for representation.
  - **Proportional representation (most of Europe):** allows third parties, multiparty coalitions.
  - **Mixed systems (Germany, Japan):** combine both elements.
- **Citizens United's effects:**
  - **Super PACs** can raise/spend unlimited amounts on independent political activity (no coordination with candidates).
  - **Dark money** — non-profit organizations (501(c)(4)s) can engage in political activity without disclosing donors.
  - **Total spending in 2024 federal elections:** approximately $16 billion (record).
  - **Outside spending now exceeds candidate spending in many races.**
  - **Effect debate:** Citizens United critics argue it amplifies wealthy interests; defenders argue it expands political speech.
- **Modern campaign techniques:**
  - **Voter targeting** (microtargeting): using data analytics to identify likely supporters and persuadable voters; tailored messages.
  - **Door-to-door canvassing:** still important especially for GOTV.
  - **Phone/text outreach:** scaled through automated systems.
  - **Digital advertising:** Facebook, Google, YouTube, increasingly TikTok and Instagram. Allows precise targeting by demographic, interest, location.
  - **Earned media** (free media coverage from press) vs **paid media** (advertising).
  - **Debates:** decreasing attention compared to past elections but remain important moments.
- **Political polarization mechanisms:**
  - **Primary elections:** require appealing to ideological base, pushing candidates toward extremes.
  - **Gerrymandering:** safe districts allow ideological extremes to win, eliminating moderate candidates.
  - **Media echo chambers:** partisan-aligned cable news and social media reinforce existing views.
  - **Geographic sorting:** Americans increasingly live among ideologically similar neighbors.
  - **Partisan identity strength:** voters increasingly feel STRONGLY about both their party and the opposing party (affective polarization).
- **Media as institution:**
  - **Watchdog function:** investigative reporting (Watergate Pulitzer; Pentagon Papers; Boston Globe Spotlight team on Catholic Church abuse).
  - **Agenda-setting:** what media covers becomes salient to public (foreign coverage decisions, missing children stories).
  - **Framing:** how stories are told affects interpretation (immigration as economic issue vs cultural issue vs security issue).
  - **Gatekeeping decline:** once-dominant gatekeepers (network anchors) have less influence; users access news directly.
  - **Fact-checking:** organizations (PolitiFact, FactCheck.org) try to maintain factual integrity. Effectiveness debated.

### Adds for [5]

- **The "money in politics" debate.** Three questions interweave:
  - **Constitutional:** does the First Amendment protect political spending as "speech"? Citizens United said yes; critics challenge this equation of money and speech.
  - **Empirical:** does money buy political outcomes? Research is mixed. Money matters in primaries (helps candidates become known); in general elections, the higher-spending candidate wins ~80% of the time but it's unclear whether spending CAUSES wins or whether stronger candidates ATTRACT spending.
  - **Normative:** even if money doesn't directly buy votes, does the appearance of corruption undermine democratic legitimacy? Buckley v. Valeo (1976) said preventing corruption AND APPEARANCE of corruption justifies contribution limits.
- **The "polarization paradox" again.** While elites have polarized dramatically, the public on most issues hasn't shifted much in terms of policy preferences. Mass polarization is more "affective" — feelings about the other party — than ideological. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle: politicians cater to extreme primary voters; general election voters then have only extreme choices; this reinforces partisan animus.
- **Why "moderate" doesn't necessarily mean "centrist."** A voter holding both very liberal positions on some issues and very conservative positions on others might be called "moderate" if averaged but isn't holding mid-range positions on anything. The two-dimensional ideology map (Unit 4) explains this.
- **Voter suppression vs voter integrity debates.** One side emphasizes barriers to voting (registration requirements, voter ID, poll closures, gerrymandering) as voter suppression. Other side emphasizes integrity concerns (registration accuracy, ineligible voting). Constitutional and policy debates increasingly focus on this fault line. Shelby County v. Holder (2013) struck down preclearance requirements, allowing covered jurisdictions to enact restrictions without federal approval — generating the modern voting rights debate.
- **Why third parties affect outcomes despite not winning.** Even without winning electoral votes, third parties can:
  - **Spoil:** drawing votes from one major party (Nader 2000, Perot 1992).
  - **Set agendas:** raising issues major parties later co-opt (Greens on environment; Tea Party on debt).
  - **Test new positions:** without electoral pressure to moderate.
- **Modern party transformation:** the 2024 Republican and Democratic parties are different from their 1980s versions. Republicans have become more populist on trade, more nationalist on foreign policy, more aligned with non-college white voters. Democrats have become more aligned with college-educated voters, racially more diverse, more progressive on social issues. The major-party realignment is ongoing.
- **Why the Electoral College matters even when popular vote loser wins.** Two such cases in modern era (2000 Bush, 2016 Trump). The system gives small states proportionally more weight (every state has at least 3 electors regardless of population). Defenders argue this protects federalism and prevents populous regions from dominating; critics argue it violates one-person-one-vote.
- **The "media-polarization feedback loop."** Cable news and social media reward attention. Partisan content generates more attention than balanced reporting. Advertisers pay for attention. Producers make more partisan content. Voters consume partisan content. Voters become more polarized. This loop accelerated dramatically in the 2010s with social media's algorithmic amplification of inflammatory content.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Q1 Concept Application

**Hypothetical scenario:** "A political party's national committee considers two strategies: (a) focus on mobilizing existing supporters who don't vote regularly, or (b) try to persuade independent and moderate voters to support their candidates."

**Apply political party functions to:**
(a) the trade-offs of each strategy
(b) which strategy is more common in modern elections
(c) the implications for democracy

- **(a)** Mobilization (GOTV) targets people who already agree but don't vote — efficient turnout boost. Persuasion targets undecided voters — harder, smaller pool, but could shift outcomes in close races. Mobilization works better in safe districts; persuasion matters more in competitive ones.
- **(b)** Modern campaigns typically blend both, but many emphasize mobilization (cheaper, more reliable than persuasion). Microtargeting through data analytics enables sophisticated mobilization. The number of "true independents" willing to switch parties has shrunk, making persuasion harder.
- **(c)** Democratic implications: focus on mobilization can deepen polarization (parties cater to base) while neglecting cross-cutting persuasion that builds democratic consensus. But mobilization also expands participation — bringing more people into the political process is generally democratic.

### Example 2 [4] — Q2 Quantitative Analysis

**Hypothetical chart:** Voter turnout rates by age in 2020 presidential election:
- 18-29: 50%
- 30-44: 62%
- 45-64: 71%
- 65+: 76%

**Explain the pattern AND identify factors:**

- **Pattern:** clear age gradient — older voters turn out at much higher rates. Gap between youngest and oldest voters: 26 percentage points.
- **Likely factors:**
  - **Civic engagement habits:** older voters have established voting habits; civic engagement compounds with age.
  - **Socioeconomic factors:** older voters more likely to be economically established; financial security correlates with turnout.
  - **Stake in policy:** older voters more directly affected by Social Security, Medicare; younger voters more affected by issues that may seem more abstract.
  - **Registration burdens:** older voters more likely to be registered (less mobile, more familiar with process).
  - **Time costs:** older voters less likely to face work-schedule conflicts (retirement).
- **Implications:** policy attention skews toward older voters' interests. Younger voters' policy preferences (education funding, climate, student debt) receive less proportional weight than older voters' preferences (healthcare, retirement). This is a structural issue with US voting patterns.

### Example 3 [4] — Q3 SCOTUS Comparison

**Hypothetical:** "In Doe v. United States, the Supreme Court considered whether a federal regulation limiting independent political spending by labor unions violates the First Amendment. Compare this case to Citizens United v. FEC (2010)."

- **Step 1 (constitutional clause):** Both cases involve the First Amendment's protection of political speech, applied to corporations or unions.
- **Step 2 (link to required case):** Citizens United held that political spending by corporations and unions is protected speech under the First Amendment. The same constitutional principle would apply to Doe v. United States — a federal regulation limiting union political spending would face the same First Amendment scrutiny.
- **Step 3 (compare reasoning):** Citizens United reasoned that political speech is at the core of First Amendment protection, that the speaker's identity (corporate or individual) doesn't change the protection, and that government cannot restrict independent political speech. Doe v. United States would likely follow this precedent — the Court would likely strike down the federal regulation if it limited independent expenditures.
- **Step 4 (implication):** the comparison shows that Citizens United's protection extends symmetrically — both corporations and unions enjoy First Amendment protection for independent political spending. The case's expansive reading of corporate speech rights creates broad limits on government regulation of independent political activity.

### Example 4 [5] — Q4 Argument Essay on Political Parties

**Hypothetical prompt:** "Develop an argument that the modern American political parties have weakened or strengthened American democracy."

**Sample thesis (defending "weakened"):**
"Modern American political parties have weakened American democracy by becoming increasingly polarized and ideologically rigid in ways that prevent the cross-cutting compromise the Constitution's separation of powers requires; while parties remain essential institutions for political organization, their current trajectory toward affective polarization undermines democratic deliberation and policymaking, contradicting Madison's expectation in Federalist No. 10 that diverse factions would interact productively."

**Six-point structure:**

1. **Thesis [1 pt]:** above.

2. **Evidence 1 from required document [1 pt]:** "Madison's Federalist No. 10 envisions diverse factions in an extended republic interacting productively — no faction able to dominate, all forced to compromise. Modern American political parties have not produced this vision; they have instead congealed into two ideologically purified blocs that view each other as existential threats. The 'extended republic' Madison celebrated has produced not faction-balancing but faction-polarization."

3. **Evidence 2 from required document [1 pt]:** "Federalist No. 51's case for separation of powers assumes branches will interact through compromise — 'ambition counteracts ambition.' Modern parties have weaponized institutional rules to prevent compromise: filibusters used hundreds of times per Congress (vs. handful per Congress historically), reconciliation procedures used to bypass minority objection, judicial confirmations becoming partisan trench warfare. The institutional friction Madison expected to PRODUCE compromise now PREVENTS it."

4. **Explanation [1 pt]:** "Both passages assume parties would moderate behavior through electoral competition, institutional cooperation, and the cross-cutting nature of factional interests. Modern American parties violate each assumption: primaries reward ideological purity rather than electability; institutional cooperation has collapsed; voters increasingly view the other party as enemies rather than competitors. The result is a constitutional system designed for compromise that increasingly produces gridlock or polarized swings between unilateral assertions of power."

5. **Acknowledge opposing perspective [1 pt]:** "Critics of this view argue that polarization is itself a sign of healthy parties — voters now have CLEAR choices between distinct policy alternatives, in contrast to mid-20th-century 'me-too' politics where Democratic and Republican candidates were nearly indistinguishable. Polarization, in this view, is responsiveness to voters' actual policy preferences, and the appropriate response to gridlock is electoral competition (winning more seats) rather than artificial moderation."

6. **Refute opposing perspective [1 pt]:** "The 'clear choices' defense identifies a real benefit but understates its costs. Clearer choices have not produced more voter engagement (turnout has not improved markedly) and have produced more affective polarization (negative feelings toward the other party at historic highs). Most importantly, the constitutional system was DESIGNED for cross-cutting interests producing compromise. When parties polarize so thoroughly that compromise becomes impossible — and when voters increasingly view the other side as illegitimate threats — the constitutional design fails to function. The framers expected ambitious factions to balance each other; they did not expect ambitious factions to view each other as enemies of the constitutional order itself. The choice is not between weak parties and polarized parties but between productively competitive parties and dysfunctionally polarized ones."

### Example 5 [4] — Citizens United explained mechanically

The 2010 ruling fundamentally altered campaign finance:

- **What was banned BEFORE Citizens United:** corporations and unions could not spend general treasury funds on "electioneering communications" (ads naming a candidate) within 30/60 days of an election. Corporate political activity required separate PACs (Political Action Committees) funded by individual contributions with limits.
- **What Citizens United held:** these restrictions on INDEPENDENT corporate/union spending violate the First Amendment. The First Amendment protects political speech regardless of speaker (individual, corporation, union).
- **What changed:** "Super PACs" — independent expenditure committees — can now raise and spend UNLIMITED amounts on political activity, as long as they don't directly coordinate with candidates. Corporations and unions can fund super PACs from general treasury funds.
- **Effects:**
  - Outside spending grew dramatically: from $338 million in 2008 to $2.5 billion in 2024 (federal elections alone).
  - "Dark money" (501(c)(4) non-profits engaging in political activity without donor disclosure) grew similarly.
  - Wealthy donors gained dramatically more influence.
  - Candidate fundraising became less central; outside money increasingly drives campaign messaging.
- **Constitutional reasoning:** majority (Kennedy) reasoned that the First Amendment protects political speech as foundational to democracy; the speaker's identity doesn't change the protection; preventing wealthy interests from being heard would itself be censorship. Dissent (Stevens) argued corporations are not natural persons and unlimited corporate spending corrupts the political process.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing primary types.** Closed (only registered party members), Open (any voter chooses party for that election), Caucus (in-person meetings).
2. **Misnaming the Electoral College.** Each state has electors equal to its Congressional delegation (House + Senate). 538 total. 270 to win.
3. **Forgetting Maine and Nebraska are exceptions.** They allocate by congressional district (not winner-take-all).
4. **Treating Citizens United as about candidates' campaigns.** It applies to INDEPENDENT spending. Direct contributions to candidates remain limited.
5. **Confusing PACs and super PACs.** Traditional PACs have contribution limits and can give to candidates. Super PACs have no limits but can't coordinate with candidates.
6. **Misnaming the required cases.** Baker v. Carr (1962, redistricting, "one person one vote"). Shaw v. Reno (1993, racial gerrymandering). Citizens United v. FEC (2010, corporate spending).
7. **Confusing the "one person one vote" principle's source.** Established in Baker v. Carr (1962); reaffirmed in subsequent cases.
8. **Treating gerrymandering as straightforwardly illegal.** Partisan gerrymandering remains legal (Rucho v. Common Cause 2019 declined to set federal limits). Racial gerrymandering is constitutionally suspect (Shaw v. Reno 1993).
9. **Confusing partisan polarization with mass polarization.** Elite politicians have polarized far more than voters on most issues.
10. **Misnaming Voting Rights Act provisions.** Section 5 (preclearance) struck down by Shelby County v. Holder (2013). Section 2 (general prohibition on racial discrimination in voting) remains.
11. **Treating turnout as the main measure of democratic health.** Turnout rates, while important, are one of many measures (responsiveness, representation, deliberation, accountability all matter).
12. **Confusing realignment with dealignment.** Realignment = major shift in party support. Dealignment = decline in strong party identification.
13. **Forgetting the Senate's small-state advantage.** Wyoming and California each get 2 senators; California has 70x Wyoming's population. Senate magnifies small-state influence.
14. **Treating media as monolithic.** Different media (network TV, cable, print, digital) have different audiences, different effects, different political alignments.
15. **Misnaming the major polling organizations.** Pew Research, Gallup, Quinnipiac, ABC/WaPo, NBC/WSJ, ANES — established as credible. Some cable news polls and party polls less reliable.
