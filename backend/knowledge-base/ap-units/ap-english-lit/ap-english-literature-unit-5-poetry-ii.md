## Big Ideas

1. **Figurative language extends beyond simile/metaphor** — metonymy, synecdoche, conceit.
2. **Meter has names and rules** — iambic pentameter is most important.
3. **Syntax shapes meaning** in poetry as much as diction.
4. **Sound patterns** create musicality and meaning.
5. **Tone is often complex and shifting** in mature poetry.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Advanced figurative language:**
  - **Metonymy:** substituting closely associated thing for thing meant ("the crown" for monarch, "Washington" for US government).
  - **Synecdoche:** part for whole or whole for part ("hands" for workers, "the law" for police).
  - **Conceit:** elaborate, extended metaphor (Donne's flea = lovers' bodies; Marvell's coy mistress).
  - **Metaphysical conceit:** intellectually surprising comparison (17th c. metaphysical poets).
- **Meter:**
  - **Foot:** unit of meter (combinations of stressed/unstressed syllables).
  - **Common feet:**
    - **Iamb:** unstressed-stressed (da-DUM): "to-DAY."
    - **Trochee:** stressed-unstressed (DUM-da): "TI-ger."
    - **Anapest:** unstressed-unstressed-stressed (da-da-DUM): "in-ter-VENE."
    - **Dactyl:** stressed-unstressed-unstressed (DUM-da-da): "BUT-ter-fly."
    - **Spondee:** stressed-stressed (DUM-DUM).
  - **Length names:** monometer (1 foot), dimeter (2), trimeter (3), tetrameter (4), **pentameter (5)**, hexameter (6).
  - **Iambic pentameter:** 5 iambs (10 syllables, da-DUM x 5). Shakespeare's blank verse, sonnets.
  - **Blank verse:** unrhymed iambic pentameter.
  - **Free verse:** no regular meter.
- **Scansion:**
  - **Marking** stressed (/) and unstressed (∪) syllables.
  - **Identifying meter** by counting feet.
- **Rhyme types:**
  - **Perfect rhyme:** same ending sound ("cat/hat").
  - **Slant rhyme:** approximate ("worm/swarm"). Common in Dickinson.
  - **Eye rhyme:** spelled like rhyme but doesn't sound it ("rough/cough").
  - **Internal rhyme:** within line.
  - **End rhyme:** at line ends.
- **Stanza types:**
  - **Couplet:** 2 lines (often rhyming).
  - **Tercet:** 3 lines.
  - **Quatrain:** 4 lines.
  - **Sestet:** 6 lines.
  - **Octave:** 8 lines.
- **Sonnet variations:**
  - **Italian (Petrarchan):** octave (ABBAABBA) + sestet (varies). Volta at line 9.
  - **English (Shakespearean):** 3 quatrains + couplet (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG). Volta often at line 13.
  - **Spenserian:** ABAB BCBC CDCD EE.
- **Other forms:**
  - **Ballad:** narrative; often quatrains; common rhyme.
  - **Ode:** formal address.
  - **Elegy:** mourns death.
  - **Villanelle:** 19 lines, 5 tercets + quatrain, 2 refrains (Plath, "Mad Girl's Love Song"; Bishop, "One Art"; Thomas, "Do Not Go Gentle").
- **Syntax:**
  - **Sentence structure** in poetry creates emphasis, surprise.
  - **Inversion:** reversed normal order ("Whose woods these are I think I know").
  - **Caesura:** pause within line.
  - **Enjambment** (review).

### Adds for [4]

- **When meter is regular vs broken:**
  - **Regular meter** establishes expectation.
  - **Broken meter** creates emphasis at the broken spot.
  - **Skilled poets** use both.
  - **E.g.,** Shakespeare's iambic pentameter occasionally substitutes feet for emphasis.
- **Conceit deep dive:**
  - **John Donne's "The Flea":** flea biting both lovers as marriage.
  - **George Herbert's "The Pulley":** human nature as pulley.
  - **Metaphysical poets** delight in surprising comparison.
- **Slant rhyme (Dickinson):**
  - Approximations create unsettled, modern feel.
  - Common in 20th century.
- **Syntax effects:**
  - **Inversion** for emphasis or to fit meter.
  - **Repetition** for rhythm and emphasis.
  - **Long sentences** for accumulation.
  - **Short sentences** for impact.
  - **Caesura** for hesitation, weight.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why meter matters.**
  - **Establishes rhythm** and expectation.
  - **Disruptions** carry meaning.
  - **English-language poetry** has long tradition of meter (Shakespeare, Milton, Dryden, Pope, Wordsworth, Tennyson, Eliot all use it).
- **Why form matters.**
  - **Sonnet form** suits argument with turn.
  - **Villanelle's refrains** suit obsession, return.
  - **Free verse's freedom** suits modern fragmentation.
- **Why syntactic analysis matters.**
  - **Word order in poetry** is deliberately chosen.
  - **Inversion** can be conventional (older poetry) or strategic.
  - **Effects** worth noting in analysis.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Identify advanced figurative

For each, identify device:
(a) "All hands on deck" (sailors).
(b) "The pen is mightier than the sword" (writing/violence).
(c) Donne's "Two as one" (lovers).

- (a) **Synecdoche** (hands for sailors).
- (b) **Metonymy** (pen for writing, sword for violence).
- (c) **Conceit** (extended metaphysical metaphor).

### Example 2 [3] — Scansion practice

Scan: "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?"
- **Iambs:** "Shall I" (∪/) "com-PARE" (∪/) "thee TO" (∪/) "a SUM-" (∪/) "mer's DAY" (∪/).
- **5 iambs = iambic pentameter.**

### Example 3 [4] — Sonnet form

What's the difference between Italian and English sonnet?
- **Italian (Petrarchan):**
  - Octave (8 lines, often ABBAABBA) + sestet (varies).
  - **Volta at line 9.**
- **English (Shakespearean):**
  - 3 quatrains (ABAB CDCD EFEF) + couplet (GG).
  - **Volta often at line 13** (couplet delivers).
- **Both:** 14 lines, iambic pentameter.

### Example 4 [4] — Villanelle structure

Describe a villanelle.
- **19 lines:** 5 tercets + 1 quatrain.
- **Two refrain lines** alternate ending tercets, then both close quatrain.
- **Rhyme scheme:** ABA throughout, then ABAA.
- **Examples:** Bishop "One Art," Thomas "Do Not Go Gentle," Plath "Mad Girl's Love Song."
- **Form's repetition** suits obsession, return, loss.

### Example 5 [5] — Conceit analysis

In Donne's "The Flea," analyze the conceit.
- **The conceit:** flea bites both speaker and lover; their bloods mingle in the flea.
- **Speaker uses this** to argue for sexual union: "We're already united in this flea — why not in person?"
- **Mistress kills the flea:** speaker says she's killed three lives (his, hers, theirs).
- **Wit:** elaborate, surprising, intellectually playful.
- **Metaphysical hallmark:** unlikely comparison sustained as serious argument.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing metonymy and synecdoche.** Metonymy: associated thing. Synecdoche: part/whole.
2. **Wrong meter names.** Iamb = ∪/. Pentameter = 5 feet.
3. **Confusing sonnet types.** Italian: octave-sestet, volta line 9. English: quatrains-couplet, volta often line 13.
4. **Confusing slant and eye rhyme.** Slant: approximate sound. Eye: spelled like rhyme but sounds different.
5. **Forgetting conceit's extent.** Not just any metaphor — conceit is elaborate, sustained, surprising.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For Q1 with metered poem:**
- **Identify meter** (iambic pentameter most common).
- **Note disruptions** of meter — likely emphatic.
- **Connect form to meaning.**

**For Q1 with conceit:**
- **Identify the conceit** clearly.
- **Trace its development** through poem.
- **Connect to thematic claim.**

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The poem's iambic pentameter establishes a steady rhythm, broken at [line X] by [substituted foot or extra syllable], emphasizing [meaning at that moment]."
2. "[Poet]'s extended conceit comparing [X] to [Y] elaborates over [number] stanzas, building intellectually surprising connections that ultimately argue [thematic claim]."
3. "The volta at line [X] turns the speaker's argument from [position 1] to [position 2], reinforced by the shift in [diction/imagery/syntax]."
4. "Through metonymy/synecdoche, the speaker substitutes [associated thing/part] for [whole], compressing meaning and inviting the reader to consider [association]."
5. "The villanelle's repeating refrains enact [theme of repetition/obsession/return], the form embodying the meaning."

## If You Do Nothing Else for This Unit

*Master meter (iamb, trochee, etc.) and iambic pentameter. Master sonnet variations (Italian vs English). Master metonymy, synecdoche, conceit. Master scansion basics. Identify form and connect to meaning.*
