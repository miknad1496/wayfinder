## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** Reinforces Q2 (Prose Analysis FRQ) and prose MCQ.
- **Sub-topics covered:** advanced narrative techniques (perspective, focalization, time), more sophisticated character analysis, complex plot structures, irony, symbolism in fiction.
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Q2 prose passages frequently feature these advanced techniques. MCQ tests recognition of irony, symbol, perspective.

## Big Ideas

1. **Narrative perspective shapes meaning** — what we see, what we don't.
2. **Focalization** is whose viewpoint filters the narrative — distinct from voice (who narrates).
3. **Time can be manipulated** — flashback, flash-forward, non-linear chronology.
4. **Irony is layered** — verbal, situational, dramatic.
5. **Symbols accumulate meaning** through repetition and context.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Perspective vs voice (sophisticated distinction):**
  - **Voice:** who is narrating (first person, third person, etc.).
  - **Perspective/focalization:** whose eyes we see through.
  - **Can differ:** third-person narrator can focalize through different characters.
- **Free indirect discourse:**
  - **Hybrid:** narrator's voice infused with character's perspective.
  - "She would never marry such a man. The thought made her shudder."
  - **Common in modern fiction** (Austen, Woolf, Joyce).
- **Time manipulation:**
  - **Linear:** chronological order.
  - **Flashback (analepsis):** scene from earlier.
  - **Flash-forward (prolepsis):** scene from later.
  - **In medias res:** start in middle.
  - **Non-linear:** events out of order (Faulkner, Toni Morrison).
- **Pacing:**
  - **Scene:** detailed, slow narration.
  - **Summary:** condensed narration.
  - **Pacing varies** within work for emphasis.
  - **Slow scenes** often signal importance.
- **Three types of irony:**
  - **Verbal irony:** says one thing, means another (sarcasm is one form).
  - **Situational irony:** outcome opposite of expectation.
  - **Dramatic irony:** reader knows what character doesn't.
- **Symbolism:**
  - **Object/image with meaning beyond literal.**
  - **Conventional symbols:** rose = love, dove = peace.
  - **Author-created symbols:** acquire meaning within work.
  - **Recurring symbols** accumulate weight.
- **Motif:**
  - **Recurring element** (image, phrase, situation).
  - **Different from symbol** — motif is about repetition; symbol is about meaning.
- **Allegory:**
  - **Extended symbolic narrative** where elements stand for abstract concepts.
  - **Examples:** *Animal Farm* (totalitarianism), *Pilgrim's Progress* (Christian salvation).
- **Tone in fiction:**
  - **Narrator's attitude** toward subject and characters.
  - **Can be ironic, sympathetic, detached, indignant, etc.**
- **Diction in fiction:**
  - **Word choices** characterize narrator AND/OR character.
  - **Register** (formal, colloquial, dialect) signifies.

### Adds for [4]

- **Free indirect discourse function:**
  - **Allows intimacy** with character without first-person.
  - **Allows narrator to maintain irony** while presenting character's view.
  - **Famous example:** Austen's *Emma* — Emma's misjudgments presented through her own consciousness.
- **Non-linear chronology purposes:**
  - **Reveal cause through effect** (start with consequence, work backward).
  - **Mimic memory** (Faulkner's *Sound and Fury*).
  - **Create mystery** (puzzle-piece reveals).
  - **Comment on time itself** (modernist obsession).
- **Dramatic irony power:**
  - **Suspense** (we know danger character doesn't see).
  - **Pathos** (we know hopes will be dashed).
  - **Irony** of mistakes.
- **Symbol vs motif:**
  - **Symbol:** specific instance of meaningful object.
  - **Motif:** repeated pattern (color, phrase, situation).
  - **Often related:** motifs become symbolic through repetition.
- **Layered narrators:**
  - **Frame narrative:** story within story (*Heart of Darkness* — narrator listens to Marlow's tale).
  - **Multiple narrators:** different perspectives on same events.
  - **Adds complexity** and unreliability.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why narrative complexity matters.**
  - **Sophisticated fiction often subverts** simple narration.
  - **Reader's task:** piece together meaning from fragmentary perspective.
  - **Echoes how we actually understand events** (partial, retrospective).
- **Why irony matters.**
  - **Creates layered meaning** beyond surface.
  - **Engages reader** as active interpreter.
  - **Often essential** to author's critique.
- **Why symbols accumulate.**
  - **First appearance:** image/object.
  - **Repetition:** pattern.
  - **By end:** loaded with meaning.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Identify irony type

For each, identify type:
(a) Romeo killing himself thinking Juliet is dead, when she's actually alive.
(b) The fire station burning down.
(c) Saying "Beautiful weather!" during a downpour.

- (a) **Dramatic irony** (audience knows; character doesn't).
- (b) **Situational irony** (outcome opposite of expected).
- (c) **Verbal irony** (saying opposite of meaning).

### Example 2 [3] — Symbol identification

In *The Scarlet Letter*, what does the "A" symbolize?
- **Initially:** Adultery (literal punishment).
- **Through novel:** **Able**, **Angel**, **Hester's pride**, eventually badge of identity.
- **Symbol meaning shifts** as story progresses.
- **Author makes symbol's evolution thematic.**

### Example 3 [4] — Free indirect discourse

Identify free indirect discourse:
"Emma reflected. Mr. Knightley's anger was unreasonable, surely. He had no right to dictate her choices."

- **Narrator** speaks, but **Emma's voice and perspective** infuse the language.
- "Surely" reveals her conviction (which may be wrong).
- **Reader gets** Emma's inner state without explicit "she thought."

### Example 4 [4] — Non-linear chronology

Why does Faulkner's *The Sound and the Fury* use non-linear chronology?
- **Mimics consciousness/memory.**
- **Different sections** voice different characters; each has own time-sense.
- **Reveals truth gradually,** as character understanding evolves.
- **Form embodies theme:** time and memory's instability, Compson family's decline.

### Example 5 [5] — Symbol analysis in passage

In a passage where a character repeatedly notices windows, how might you analyze?
- **First note pattern:** how often, in what contexts.
- **Look for variations:** open/closed, clean/dirty, looking in/looking out.
- **Connect to character's interior:** windows might represent yearning, separation, observation.
- **Connect to theme:** what does the recurring image suggest about the work's concerns?

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing voice and perspective.** Voice: who narrates. Perspective: whose viewpoint we see through. Free indirect discourse blends them.
2. **Confusing irony types.** Verbal (saying), situational (event), dramatic (audience knows what character doesn't).
3. **Confusing symbol and motif.** Symbol: meaningful object/image. Motif: recurring element.
4. **Treating allegory as same as symbol.** Allegory: extended symbolic narrative where elements stand for abstractions.
5. **Missing pacing significance.** Slow scenes signal importance.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For Q2 (Prose Analysis):**
- **Identify perspective/focalization.**
- **Identify ironies present.**
- **Identify symbols/motifs.**
- **Connect technique to meaning.**

**For MCQ:**
- **Identify free indirect discourse** when narrator's voice carries character's perspective.
- **Identify ironies** in passage.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Through free indirect discourse, the narrator presents [character]'s perspective while maintaining ironic distance, allowing the reader to see both [character's view] and [author's critique]."
2. "The dramatic irony — that the reader knows [X] while [character] does not — generates [pathos/suspense/tragic effect] and underscores [thematic claim]."
3. "The recurring motif of [image/phrase] accumulates symbolic weight: from initial [literal/contextual meaning] to deeper [thematic resonance] by the work's end."
4. "The non-linear chronology mirrors [memory/trauma/the impossibility of clear historical truth], formally embodying the work's thematic concerns."
5. "[Author]'s shift in pacing from summary to scene at [moment] signals the importance of this episode, marking it as central to the work's thematic concerns."
