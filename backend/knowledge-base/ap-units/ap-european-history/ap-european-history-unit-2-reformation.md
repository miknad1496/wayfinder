## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 10–15% of the AP European History exam
- **Period:** c. 1450-1648 (combined with Unit 1)
- **Sub-topics covered:**
  - 2.1 Contextualizing the Reformation
  - 2.2 Luther and the Protestant Reformation
  - 2.3 Protestant Reform Continues
  - 2.4 Wars of Religion
  - 2.5 Catholic and Counter Reformations
  - 2.6 16th-Century Society and Politics
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** The Reformation is one of the MOST IMPORTANT events in European history. Luther, Calvin, and Henry VIII are central. Wars of Religion (especially the Thirty Years' War, ending 1648) reshaped Europe.

## Big Ideas

1. **The Protestant Reformation (1517 onward) shattered Western Christianity's unity.** Luther's 95 Theses sparked a revolution.
2. **Multiple Protestant traditions emerged.** Lutheran, Calvinist (Reformed), Anglican (Church of England).
3. **Catholic Church responded with Counter-Reformation.** Council of Trent reformed practices; Jesuits expanded.
4. **Wars of Religion devastated Europe.** Thirty Years' War (1618-1648) ended with Peace of Westphalia.
5. **The 1517 (95 Theses) and 1648 (Westphalia) brackets the religious revolution period.** Per the brain file's signature: 1517 and 1648 are key turning-point dates.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Pre-Reformation Catholic Church concerns:**
  - **Indulgences:** payments for forgiveness of sins. Sale of indulgences widely criticized.
  - **Simony:** sale of church offices.
  - **Nepotism:** church positions to family members.
  - **Wealth and corruption** of papacy.
- **Martin Luther (1483-1546):**
  - German Augustinian monk.
  - **95 Theses (October 31, 1517):** posted on Wittenberg church door, attacking indulgences and church corruption.
  - **Diet of Worms (1521):** refused to recant. Excommunicated; declared outlaw.
  - Translated Bible into German.
  - **Doctrines:**
    - **Sola fide:** salvation by faith alone (not works).
    - **Sola scriptura:** authority is Bible alone (not Pope, tradition).
    - **Priesthood of all believers:** no special priestly class needed.
- **Spread of Protestantism:**
  - **Princely states** of Holy Roman Empire often adopted Lutheranism.
  - **Peasants' War (1524-1525):** German peasants revolted partly inspired by Luther; Luther condemned them.
  - **Peace of Augsburg (1555):** princes of Holy Roman Empire could choose Lutheran or Catholic religion ("cuius regio, eius religio" — whose realm, his religion).
- **Other Protestant reformers:**
  - **John Calvin (1509-1564):** French theologian; based in Geneva.
    - **Predestination:** God has chosen the elect for salvation; no human action affects this.
    - **Geneva** became Calvinist model city — strict moral code.
    - **Calvinism spread:** Netherlands, Scotland (Presbyterian), England (Puritans), parts of France (Huguenots).
  - **Ulrich Zwingli:** Swiss reformer in Zurich.
  - **Anabaptists:** more radical; adult baptism, separation from state. Persecuted by both Catholics and other Protestants.
- **English Reformation:**
  - **Henry VIII (r. 1509-1547):** wanted divorce from Catherine of Aragon (no male heir).
  - **Pope refused.**
  - **Henry broke with Rome (Act of Supremacy, 1534):** Henry head of Church of England.
  - **Anglican Church:** doctrine moderate (closer to Catholic than Lutheran initially).
  - **Mary I ("Bloody Mary," r. 1553-1558):** restored Catholicism briefly; persecuted Protestants.
  - **Elizabeth I (r. 1558-1603):** Protestant; Elizabethan Settlement made England moderately Protestant.
- **Catholic (Counter) Reformation:**
  - **Council of Trent (1545-1563):** reformed Catholic practices but reaffirmed key doctrines.
    - Reaffirmed transubstantiation, seven sacraments, papal authority.
    - Banned sale of indulgences (specifically) but kept indulgences in principle.
    - Standardized Mass (Latin Mass).
    - Improved priest education (seminaries).
  - **Jesuits (Society of Jesus, founded 1540 by Ignatius Loyola):**
    - Highly disciplined Catholic order.
    - Emphasized education, missionary work.
    - Spread Catholicism in Europe and globally (Asia, Americas, Africa).
- **Wars of Religion:**
  - **French Wars of Religion (1562-1598):** Catholic vs Huguenot (Calvinist).
    - **St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre (1572):** ~10,000 Huguenots killed.
    - **Edict of Nantes (1598):** Henry IV granted Huguenots limited religious tolerance.
  - **Dutch Revolt (1568-1648):** Calvinist Netherlands rebelled against Catholic Spain.
  - **Thirty Years' War (1618-1648):** began as religious war in Holy Roman Empire; became broader political conflict.
    - Devastated central Europe (~25% population loss in Germany).
    - **Peace of Westphalia (1648):** ended war; established sovereignty of states; allowed Calvinism alongside Lutheranism.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why Luther's 95 Theses caught fire:**
  - Coincided with printing press technology.
  - German princes had political incentives to support Luther (against Holy Roman Emperor).
  - Existing church criticism widespread.
  - Vernacular (German) Bible accessible to laypeople.
- **Calvinism's distinctive features:**
  - **Predestination:** highly debated doctrine.
  - **"Calling":** even non-religious work could glorify God.
  - **Geneva as model theocracy.**
  - **Spread widely:** Calvinists were energetic missionaries.
- **Why Henry VIII broke with Rome:**
  - Wanted divorce/annulment (need male heir).
  - Pope refused (under Catholic Charles V's pressure).
  - Henry created Church of England with himself as head.
  - **Distinct from Lutheranism/Calvinism** — primarily political, not theological initially.
- **Why Reformation succeeded politically.**
  - **German princes** could seize Catholic Church wealth.
  - **Anti-Italian, anti-papal sentiment** in northern Europe.
  - **Printing press** spread ideas rapidly.
  - **Vernacular Bibles** undermined priest authority.
- **Council of Trent achievements:**
  - Maintained Catholic doctrine.
  - Reformed practices (better priest education, ended sale of indulgences).
  - Made Catholic Church more disciplined and effective.
  - Set Catholic doctrine that lasted into modern era.
- **Jesuits' role:**
  - Education (founded universities and schools).
  - Missionary work in Americas, Asia, Africa.
  - Particularly successful in countering Protestantism in Eastern Europe.
- **Thirty Years' War (1618-1648):**
  - **Began:** Defenestration of Prague (1618).
  - **Phases:** Bohemian, Danish, Swedish, French (last phase political, not religious).
  - **France (Catholic) supported Protestants** against Catholic Habsburgs — political over religious.
  - **Ravaged central Europe:** populations decimated.
  - **Peace of Westphalia (1648):** modern state system; sovereignty over religion within state.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why Reformation matters for modern Europe.**
  - Religious pluralism (Protestant, Catholic, eventually secular).
  - Rise of nation-states (vs universal Christendom).
  - Intellectual freedom (eventually leads to Enlightenment).
  - Capitalism (Weber thesis: Protestant ethic enabled capitalism — debated).
- **Why Westphalia matters.**
  - Established sovereignty principle (states control internal affairs).
  - Foundation of modern international system.
  - Religion subordinated to state.
- **Catholic Reformation's success.**
  - Most of Latin Europe remained Catholic.
  - Catholicism more disciplined post-Trent.
  - Jesuits helped reverse Protestant gains in Eastern Europe (e.g., Poland).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Luther's beliefs

What were Luther's three "solas"?
- **Sola fide:** salvation by faith alone.
- **Sola scriptura:** authority of scripture alone (not Pope or tradition).
- **Sola gratia:** salvation by grace alone (not works).

### Example 2 [3] — Reformation figures

For each: identify the reformer.
(a) German monk who posted 95 Theses.
(b) French theologian based in Geneva; predestination.
(c) English king who broke with Rome over divorce.

- (a) **Martin Luther.**
- (b) **John Calvin.**
- (c) **Henry VIII.**

### Example 3 [4] — Causes of Reformation

Why did Luther's reform succeed where earlier reformers (Wycliffe, Hus) failed?
- **Printing press** spread Luther's ideas rapidly.
- **German princes** politically supported him (against Holy Roman Emperor).
- **Vernacular Bible** accessible to laypeople.
- **Anti-Italian, anti-papal sentiment** in northern Europe.
- **Coincided with Renaissance humanism** that questioned religious authority.

### Example 4 [4] — Council of Trent

What did the Council of Trent (1545-1563) do?
- **Reaffirmed Catholic doctrine** (transubstantiation, sacraments, papal authority).
- **Reformed practices:** banned sale of indulgences (specifically); standardized Mass; improved priest education (seminaries).
- **Set Catholic doctrine** that lasted to modern era.
- **Did NOT reconcile with Protestants** but solidified Catholic Church.

### Example 5 [5] — Thirty Years' War

Explain the Thirty Years' War (1618-1648).
- **Began:** religious conflict in Holy Roman Empire.
- **Evolved:** became broader political conflict.
- **Significantly:** France (Catholic) supported Protestants against Catholic Habsburgs — political over religious.
- **Devastating:** central Europe population reduced ~25%.
- **Ended:** Peace of Westphalia (1648).
  - Sovereignty principle (states control internal affairs).
  - Calvinism alongside Lutheranism allowed.
  - Modern state system established.
- **Significance:** marked end of major religious wars; foundation of modern Europe.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Wrong Luther date.** 95 Theses: 1517.
2. **Confusing Lutheranism, Calvinism, Anglicanism.** Lutheran (faith-centered, less radical). Calvinist (predestination, more radical). Anglican (mostly political; doctrinally moderate).
3. **Misnaming Calvin.** John Calvin (French), based in Geneva.
4. **Wrong Henry VIII reason.** Broke from Rome primarily for divorce/heir reasons; doctrinally Anglican was conservative initially.
5. **Confusing Council of Trent and Jesuits.** Council of Trent: ecumenical council reforming Catholic practices. Jesuits: religious order founded by Ignatius Loyola.
6. **Wrong Thirty Years' War dates.** 1618-1648.
7. **Wrong Westphalia date.** 1648 (one of the brain file's 12 turning-point dates).

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Luther's 95 Theses (1517) attacked the sale of indulgences and other Catholic Church practices, sparking the Protestant Reformation. Luther's three 'solas' — sola fide, sola scriptura, sola gratia — defined Protestant theology."
2. "Calvin's doctrine of predestination held that God has already chosen the elect for salvation; no human action can change this. Geneva became a model Calvinist theocracy under Calvin's leadership."
3. "Henry VIII broke with Rome (Act of Supremacy, 1534) to obtain a divorce and male heir, NOT primarily for theological reasons. The Church of England was initially close to Catholic doctrine."
4. "The Council of Trent (1545-1563) was the Catholic Church's ecumenical response to Protestantism. It reformed practices (banned sale of indulgences, improved priest education) while reaffirming Catholic doctrine (transubstantiation, papal authority, seven sacraments)."
5. "The Thirty Years' War (1618-1648), beginning as a religious conflict in the Holy Roman Empire, became broader European political war. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) established the modern principle of state sovereignty and ended major religious wars in Europe."
