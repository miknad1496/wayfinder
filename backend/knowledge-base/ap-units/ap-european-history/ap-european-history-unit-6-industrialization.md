# AP European History — Unit 6: Industrial Revolution and Its Effects (c. 1815–1914) — Wayfinder Teaching Brain

> Surfaces in chat when student mentions topics in this unit. Use to ground custom teaching responses + on-demand teaching guide generation.

## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 10–15% of the AP European History exam
- **Period:** c. 1815–1914
- **Sub-topics covered:** Industrial Revolution origins (Britain); spread to continent; new technologies; urbanization; new social classes; working conditions; ideologies (liberalism, socialism, Marxism); reform movements.
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Industrial Revolution causes (Britain first) tested constantly. Marx's *Communist Manifesto* (1848) frequent. Working/living conditions and reform movements common.

## Big Ideas

1. **Industrial Revolution started in Britain (~1750-1850)** and spread across Europe.
2. **Transformed economy:** factories replaced cottage industry; steam power; railroads.
3. **Created new social classes:** industrial bourgeoisie, urban working class (proletariat).
4. **Generated new ideologies:** liberalism, socialism, Marxism, anarchism.
5. **Provoked reform movements:** factory acts, labor unions, expanded suffrage.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Origins of Industrial Revolution in Britain:**
  - **Capital from trade** and colonies.
  - **Natural resources** (coal, iron).
  - **Labor supply** from agricultural improvements (enclosure movement).
  - **Stable government** and legal protections.
  - **Colonial markets** for goods.
  - **Scientific Revolution** legacy.
  - **Banking and finance** infrastructure.
- **Key technologies:**
  - **Steam engine** (James Watt, improved 1769).
  - **Spinning jenny, water frame, spinning mule** (textiles).
  - **Power loom.**
  - **Bessemer process** (1856): cheap steel.
  - **Railroads:** **Stephenson's Rocket** (1829); rapid expansion 1830s-1850s.
  - **Telegraph** (1830s-40s).
  - **Internal combustion engine** (late 1800s).
- **Key industries:**
  - **Textiles** (cotton, wool).
  - **Iron and steel.**
  - **Coal mining.**
  - **Railroads.**
- **Spread of industrialization:**
  - **Britain first** (1750s-1830s).
  - **Belgium, France, Germany** later (1830s-1870s).
  - **Germany** became dominant continental industrial power by late 1800s.
  - **Russia** later still (late 1800s).
- **Urbanization:**
  - Massive migration from rural to urban.
  - **London, Manchester, Liverpool** became huge industrial cities.
  - **Slum conditions:** overcrowding, poor sanitation, disease (cholera, typhus).
- **New social classes:**
  - **Industrial bourgeoisie:** factory owners, merchants, financiers.
  - **Proletariat:** urban working class.
  - **Middle class** expanded (professionals, managers, clerks).
  - **Working-class women and children** in factories.
- **Working conditions:**
  - Long hours (12-16 hours/day).
  - Low wages.
  - Dangerous conditions; industrial accidents.
  - Child labor; women in textile factories.
  - **No labor protections** initially.
- **Ideologies:**
  - **Classical liberalism:** free markets; individual rights; limited government (Adam Smith legacy).
  - **Socialism:** social ownership of production; concern for workers.
  - **Utopian socialists:** Robert Owen, Charles Fourier, Henri de Saint-Simon — model communities.
  - **Marxism (Karl Marx, 1818-1883):**
    - *The Communist Manifesto* (1848, with Friedrich Engels).
    - *Das Kapital* (1867).
    - **Class struggle** drives history (proletariat vs bourgeoisie).
    - **Capitalism contains contradictions** that will lead to its overthrow.
    - **Proletarian revolution** will create classless society.
  - **Anarchism:** opposition to state authority.
- **Reform movements:**
  - **Factory Acts** (Britain, starting 1830s): limited child labor; shortened hours.
  - **Labor unions** legalized (1820s-1870s).
  - **Public health reforms** (Edwin Chadwick).
  - **Education** expanded.
  - **Suffrage gradually expanded** (Reform Bills 1832, 1867, 1884 in Britain).

### Adds for [4]

- **Why Britain first:**
  - Combination of capital, resources, labor, markets, stable government.
  - Agricultural revolution freed labor for industry.
  - Naval supremacy gave colonial trade access.
- **Second Industrial Revolution (1870s-1914):**
  - **Steel** (Bessemer process).
  - **Chemicals.**
  - **Electricity** (Edison, Tesla, Westinghouse).
  - **Internal combustion engine.**
  - **Telephone, radio.**
  - **Mass production** techniques.
  - **Germany and US overtook Britain** in some industries.
- **Marx's analysis:**
  - **Historical materialism:** economic base determines social superstructure.
  - **Class struggle** is engine of history.
  - **Bourgeoisie** owns means of production; **proletariat** sells labor.
  - **Surplus value:** workers produce more than they're paid; capitalist takes the difference.
  - **Inevitable revolution** as capitalism's contradictions worsen.
- **Reform movements' impact:**
  - **Factory Acts** improved conditions over decades.
  - **Labor unions** gained right to organize and strike.
  - **Suffrage expansion** in Britain (1832 Reform Bill: middle class; 1867: working class men; 1884: rural workers).
  - **Public health, education, sanitation** improved cities.
- **Women's role:**
  - Working-class women in factories (especially textiles).
  - Middle-class women expected to embody **"separate spheres"** ideology (domestic).
  - **Women's suffrage movement** emerged (especially late 1800s).

### Adds for [5]

- **Why Industrial Revolution mattered.**
  - Most transformative economic shift since agricultural revolution.
  - Created modern industrial economy and class structure.
  - Generated modern ideologies (liberalism, socialism, Marxism).
  - Set Europe and West on path to global dominance.
  - But created severe social problems (poverty, child labor, urban squalor).
- **Why Marx mattered.**
  - Most influential socialist thinker.
  - Marxist parties shaped politics across Europe (especially Germany).
  - 20th century: Russian Revolution (1917) led by Marxists; Chinese Revolution (1949).
  - Cold War (1945-1991) shaped by Marxist-Leninist regimes.
- **Limits of progress.**
  - Working class slowly improved conditions, but inequality remained vast.
  - Imperialism justified by industrial superiority.
  - Environmental degradation began.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Why Britain first

List four reasons Industrial Revolution started in Britain.
- **Capital** from trade and colonies.
- **Natural resources** (coal, iron).
- **Labor supply** (agricultural revolution + enclosure).
- **Stable government** and legal protections.
- **Colonial markets** for manufactured goods.

### Example 2 [3] — Identifying ideologies

For each, identify the ideology:
(a) Free markets; limited government; individual rights.
(b) Class struggle; revolution; classless society.
(c) Social ownership; concern for workers; reform.

- (a) **Classical liberalism.**
- (b) **Marxism.**
- (c) **Socialism** (broadly).

### Example 3 [4] — Marx's analysis

Summarize Marx's key claims.
- **Historical materialism:** economic base determines social/political/ideological superstructure.
- **Class struggle** is engine of history.
- **Capitalism:** bourgeoisie owns means of production; proletariat sells labor.
- **Surplus value:** workers produce more than wages; capitalists pocket difference.
- **Inevitable proletarian revolution** will overthrow capitalism, create classless communist society.

### Example 4 [4] — Working conditions and reform

Describe working conditions and reform responses.
- **Conditions:** 12-16 hour days; low wages; dangerous; child labor; no protections.
- **Reforms:** Factory Acts (Britain, 1830s+) limited child labor and hours; labor unions legalized; public health reforms; education expanded.
- **Suffrage:** Reform Bills (1832, 1867, 1884) gradually expanded vote.

### Example 5 [5] — Comparing Industrial Revolutions

Compare First and Second Industrial Revolutions.
- **First (~1750-1850):** Britain leads. Textiles, iron, coal, steam, railroads.
- **Second (~1870-1914):** Germany and US lead. Steel (Bessemer), chemicals, electricity, internal combustion. Mass production. Larger corporations.
- **Common:** technological transformation; expanded production; social disruption.
- **Difference:** scale; scientific basis; new energy sources (electricity); shift in industrial leadership.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Wrong start of Industrial Revolution.** Britain, ~1750-1850 (sometimes given 1760-1840).
2. **Confusing classical liberalism and modern liberalism.** Classical: free markets, limited government. Modern (later): more government role.
3. **Wrong Marx works.** **Communist Manifesto (1848, with Engels)**. **Das Kapital (1867)**.
4. **Confusing socialism types.** Utopian socialists (Owen, Fourier) vs scientific socialism (Marx).
5. **Wrong British Reform Bills.** **1832** (middle class); **1867** (urban working class men); **1884** (rural workers).
6. **Missing Second Industrial Revolution.** Steel, chemicals, electricity ~1870-1914; Germany and US overtake Britain.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For Industrial Revolution causation essays:**
- Address why Britain first.
- Address multiple factors (capital, resources, labor, markets, government).

**For social impact essays:**
- Address working conditions.
- Address new class structure.
- Address reform movements.

**For ideology essays:**
- Distinguish liberalism, socialism, Marxism, anarchism.
- Connect to industrial conditions.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The Industrial Revolution began in Britain (c. 1750-1850) due to a unique convergence: capital from colonial trade, abundant coal and iron, labor freed by agricultural improvements, stable government, and access to colonial markets."
2. "Steam power (James Watt, 1769), railroads (Stephenson's Rocket, 1829), and the Bessemer process (1856) transformed production, transportation, and the construction of modern industrial society."
3. "Industrialization created two new classes: the industrial bourgeoisie (factory owners, financiers) and the urban proletariat. It generated severe working conditions — 12-16 hour days, child labor, dangerous factories — that drove reform movements and labor organizing."
4. "Marx and Engels's *Communist Manifesto* (1848) argued that all history is a history of class struggle, that capitalism's contradictions would inevitably produce proletarian revolution, and that this revolution would create a classless communist society."
5. "Reform responses included Factory Acts (Britain, 1830s+) limiting child labor, the gradual legalization of unions, expanded suffrage (1832, 1867, 1884 Reform Bills), and public health and education reforms."
