## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 10–15% of the AP European History exam
- **Period:** c. 1648–1815 (overlaps with Unit 3)
- **Sub-topics covered:** Scientific Revolution (Copernicus → Newton); Enlightenment philosophers (Locke, Voltaire, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Smith); enlightened absolutism; salons and the public sphere; impacts on religion, politics, economics.
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Newton's *Principia* (1687) and the Scientific Revolution as foundation. Enlightenment philosophes and their core ideas tested constantly. Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) and capitalism. Direct line from Enlightenment to American + French Revolutions.

## Big Ideas

1. **Scientific Revolution shifted authority from tradition/religion to observation/reason.** Copernicus → Galileo → Newton.
2. **Enlightenment applied reason to society:** politics, economics, religion, education.
3. **Philosophes critiqued absolutism, religious intolerance, mercantilism.** Locke, Voltaire, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Smith.
4. **Enlightened absolutists** (Frederick the Great, Joseph II, Catherine the Great) tried to apply Enlightenment ideas while keeping power.
5. **Enlightenment ideas drove American (1776) and French (1789) Revolutions.**

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Scientific Revolution (1500s-1700s):**
  - **Shift from Aristotelian/Ptolemaic worldview to mathematical, experimental science.**
  - **Heliocentrism replaced geocentrism.**
  - **Universal natural laws** discovered through observation and mathematics.
- **Key Scientific Revolution figures:**
  - **Nicolaus Copernicus (1473-1543):** *On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres* (1543). Heliocentric model.
  - **Tycho Brahe (1546-1601):** detailed astronomical observations.
  - **Johannes Kepler (1571-1630):** laws of planetary motion (elliptical orbits).
  - **Galileo Galilei (1564-1642):** telescope; observed Jupiter's moons; supported heliocentrism. Tried by Inquisition (1633); forced to recant.
  - **Francis Bacon (1561-1626):** **inductive reasoning** (data → theory). Father of empirical method.
  - **René Descartes (1596-1650):** **deductive reasoning** ("I think, therefore I am"). Cartesian dualism (mind/body).
  - **Isaac Newton (1643-1727):** *Principia Mathematica* (1687). Universal law of gravitation; three laws of motion. Synthesized Scientific Revolution.
- **Enlightenment (1700s):** application of reason to human society.
- **Key Enlightenment philosophers:**
  - **John Locke (1632-1704):** natural rights (life, liberty, property); social contract; consent of governed; *Two Treatises of Government* (1690).
  - **Voltaire (1694-1778):** religious tolerance; freedom of speech; critic of Catholic Church and absolutism. *Candide* (1759).
  - **Baron de Montesquieu (1689-1755):** *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748). **Separation of powers** (legislative, executive, judicial). Checks and balances.
  - **Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712-1778):** *The Social Contract* (1762). "General will." More democratic; influence on French Revolution.
  - **Denis Diderot (1713-1784):** edited the **Encyclopédie** (1751-1772). Compendium of Enlightenment knowledge.
  - **Adam Smith (1723-1790):** *Wealth of Nations* (1776). **Free market capitalism**; "invisible hand." Critique of mercantilism.
  - **Mary Wollstonecraft (1759-1797):** *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792). Early feminist; women's rights to education.
  - **Cesare Beccaria (1738-1794):** *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764). Reform of criminal justice; opposed torture and death penalty.
  - **Immanuel Kant (1724-1804):** "Sapere aude!" ("Dare to know!"). Defined Enlightenment as escape from "self-imposed immaturity."
- **Enlightened absolutists:**
  - **Frederick the Great** (Prussia): "first servant of state"; religious tolerance; legal reform; corresponded with Voltaire.
  - **Joseph II** (Austria): abolished serfdom; religious tolerance; legal/educational reform.
  - **Catherine the Great** (Russia): correspondence with philosophes; some reform attempts; ultimately strengthened serfdom.
- **Salons and public sphere:**
  - Wealthy women hosted **salons** in Paris where philosophes discussed ideas.
  - **Madame Geoffrin, Madame de Pompadour** prominent salon hostesses.
  - **Coffeehouses, print culture, newspapers** created public sphere for political discussion.
- **Religious changes:**
  - **Deism:** belief in rational creator God who doesn't intervene; popular among philosophes.
  - **Skepticism toward organized religion** (especially Catholic Church).
  - **Religious tolerance** advocated by Locke, Voltaire.

### Adds for [4]

- **The Scientific Method:**
  - **Bacon's inductive reasoning:** observation → hypothesis → experiment → theory.
  - **Descartes's deductive reasoning:** start with self-evident truths → derive conclusions.
  - **Combined approach** became modern scientific method.
- **Why Galileo was tried:**
  - Supported Copernican heliocentrism in *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (1632).
  - Conflicted with Church doctrine of geocentric universe.
  - Forced to recant; spent rest of life under house arrest.
  - Symbol of conflict between science and religion.
- **Newton's significance:**
  - **Universal law of gravitation:** same forces govern motion of apples and planets.
  - **Three laws of motion** (inertia, F=ma, action-reaction).
  - **Synthesized** Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo into single unified theory.
  - Showed universe operates by **mathematical laws** discoverable by reason.
- **Locke vs Rousseau on social contract:**
  - **Locke:** government legitimate with consent; protect natural rights; right to revolution.
  - **Rousseau:** "general will"; community sovereignty; more radical democratic vision; influenced Jacobins in French Revolution.
- **Adam Smith's economic ideas:**
  - **Critique of mercantilism:** state-controlled economy is inefficient.
  - **"Invisible hand":** individuals pursuing self-interest produce social good.
  - **Specialization and division of labor** increase productivity.
  - Laissez-faire economics.
- **Enlightened absolutism's contradictions:**
  - Adopted Enlightenment rhetoric.
  - But maintained absolute power.
  - Limited reforms; serfdom often persisted.
  - Joseph II's reforms largely undone after his death.
- **Spread of Enlightenment ideas:**
  - **Encyclopédie** (28 volumes) compiled knowledge across fields.
  - **Salons, coffeehouses, print culture** created public sphere.
  - **Literacy expanded** in 18th century.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why Scientific Revolution mattered.**
  - Replaced authority (Aristotle, Bible) with observation and reason.
  - Established mathematics as language of nature.
  - Created intellectual foundation for Enlightenment.
  - Long-term: foundation of modern science and technology.
- **Why Enlightenment mattered.**
  - Provided ideological foundation for American and French Revolutions.
  - Concepts of natural rights, separation of powers, religious tolerance shape modern democracy.
  - Critiques of absolutism, mercantilism, religious intolerance.
  - Created modern public sphere and intellectual culture.
- **Limits of Enlightenment.**
  - Most philosophes were elite men.
  - Few extended reasoning to women's full equality (Wollstonecraft was exception).
  - Enlightenment ideas of "rationality" sometimes used to justify European superiority.
  - Slavery continued; some philosophes had contradictory views.
- **Why Enlightenment led to revolutions.**
  - Locke's natural rights → Declaration of Independence (1776).
  - Montesquieu's separation of powers → US Constitution (1787).
  - Rousseau's "general will" → French Revolution (1789).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Identifying scientists

For each, identify the scientist:
(a) Heliocentrism (1543).
(b) Telescope; tried by Inquisition.
(c) Universal gravitation; *Principia* (1687).

- (a) **Copernicus.**
- (b) **Galileo.**
- (c) **Newton.**

### Example 2 [3] — Identifying philosophers

For each, identify the philosopher and core idea:
(a) Natural rights (life, liberty, property); social contract.
(b) Separation of powers.
(c) "General will"; popular sovereignty.
(d) "Invisible hand"; free markets.

- (a) **Locke** — natural rights and social contract.
- (b) **Montesquieu** — separation of powers (*Spirit of the Laws*).
- (c) **Rousseau** — general will (*The Social Contract*).
- (d) **Adam Smith** — invisible hand (*Wealth of Nations*).

### Example 3 [4] — Galileo and the Church

Why was Galileo tried by the Inquisition?
- Galileo supported **heliocentric (Copernican) model**.
- Church taught **geocentric model** (Earth at center).
- Church viewed challenge to scripture and tradition.
- Galileo tried in **1633**; forced to recant; placed under house arrest.
- Symbolized **conflict between science and religion** in early modern period.

### Example 4 [4] — Locke vs Rousseau

Compare Locke's and Rousseau's social contracts.
- **Both:** government legitimacy comes from people, not divine right.
- **Locke:** protect individual natural rights (life, liberty, property); consent; right to revolution if violated; influenced American Revolution and liberalism.
- **Rousseau:** "general will" of community is sovereign; more radical, democratic vision; influenced French Revolution Jacobins.
- **Difference:** Locke more individualistic; Rousseau more collectivist.

### Example 5 [5] — Enlightenment to revolutions

How did Enlightenment ideas lead to American and French Revolutions?
- **Locke's natural rights** → American Declaration of Independence (1776) ("life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness").
- **Montesquieu's separation of powers** → US Constitution (1787) (three branches with checks and balances).
- **Rousseau's "general will"** → French Revolution (1789) and Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen.
- **Critique of absolutism** undermined Old Regime legitimacy.
- **Concept of natural equality** challenged hereditary privilege.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing scientists.** Copernicus (heliocentrism, 1543). Kepler (elliptical orbits). Galileo (telescope, tried 1633). Newton (gravitation, 1687).
2. **Confusing philosophers.** Locke (natural rights). Voltaire (tolerance). Montesquieu (separation of powers). Rousseau (general will). Smith (invisible hand).
3. **Wrong publication dates.** *Principia* 1687. *Wealth of Nations* 1776. *Spirit of the Laws* 1748. *The Social Contract* 1762.
4. **Confusing Bacon and Descartes.** Bacon: inductive (empirical, observational). Descartes: deductive (reason from first principles).
5. **Calling enlightened absolutists "Enlightenment thinkers."** They were rulers who borrowed Enlightenment ideas but maintained absolute power.
6. **Forgetting Wollstonecraft.** *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792) — early feminist Enlightenment voice.

- Connect Copernicus → Galileo → Newton.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The Scientific Revolution shifted European thought from reliance on Aristotelian and religious authority toward observation, mathematics, and reason. Newton's *Principia Mathematica* (1687) synthesized the work of Copernicus, Kepler, and Galileo into a unified mathematical description of nature."
2. "Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* (1690) argued that government derives its legitimacy from the consent of the governed and exists to protect natural rights — life, liberty, and property — directly influencing the American Declaration of Independence."
3. "Montesquieu's *Spirit of the Laws* (1748) advocated separation of powers among legislative, executive, and judicial branches as a check against tyranny — a framework adopted by the U.S. Constitution."
4. "Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) attacked mercantilism and argued that individuals pursuing self-interest in free markets produce collective prosperity through the 'invisible hand.'"
5. "Enlightened absolutists such as Frederick the Great, Joseph II, and Catherine the Great adopted Enlightenment rhetoric — religious tolerance, legal reform, education — while maintaining their absolute power, illustrating both the appeal and limits of Enlightenment ideas."
