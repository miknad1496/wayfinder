- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Unit 1 establishes the chemical foundation for everything else. Hydrogen bonding (water properties), the four macromolecule families (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids), and protein structure (primary through quaternary) are foundational concepts referenced throughout Units 2-8. Most Unit 1 questions appear in MCQs; FRQs draw on Unit 1 concepts when explaining phenomena like enzyme function, membrane structure, or DNA replication.

## Big Ideas

1. **Water's properties drive life.** Hydrogen bonding produces water's unique properties (cohesion, adhesion, high specific heat, density anomaly, universal solvent). Without water's properties — especially its solvent ability and thermal stability — terrestrial life would be impossible.
2. **Four families of biological macromolecules account for all cellular structures and functions.** Carbohydrates (energy + structure), lipids (energy storage + membranes + signaling), proteins (function), and nucleic acids (information). Each family has characteristic monomers and bond types.
3. **Structure determines function at every biological level.** From small molecules (water's polarity → hydrogen bonding capacity) to macromolecules (protein folding determines what it can do) to cells (membrane structure determines transport), structure-function relationships are the AP Bio's central organizing principle.
4. **Dehydration synthesis builds polymers; hydrolysis breaks them.** All four macromolecule classes are built by linking monomers through DEHYDRATION SYNTHESIS (loss of water) and broken by HYDROLYSIS (addition of water). This shared mechanism unifies macromolecule chemistry.
5. **Nucleic acids store information that proteins execute.** DNA stores genetic information; RNA carries it; proteins do the cellular work. The directional flow (DNA → RNA → protein) is the central dogma — foundational for Unit 6.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Water's properties** (driven by HYDROGEN BONDING):
  - **Polarity:** oxygen pulls electrons more strongly than hydrogen → partial negative charge on O, partial positive on H. Water is a polar molecule.
  - **Hydrogen bonding:** the partial positive H of one water molecule attracts the partial negative O of another. Each water can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds.
  - **Cohesion:** water molecules stick together → surface tension.
  - **Adhesion:** water molecules stick to other polar molecules → capillary action (water moving up plant stems).
  - **High specific heat:** water resists temperature changes (lots of energy to break hydrogen bonds). Temperature stability of oceans, climate moderation.
  - **High heat of vaporization:** evaporative cooling (sweating).
  - **Density anomaly:** ICE is LESS DENSE than liquid water. Hydrogen bonds form a rigid open lattice in ice. Allows ice to float, insulating water below — essential for aquatic life in freezing climates.
  - **Universal solvent:** dissolves polar and ionic substances. Why blood, cytoplasm, sap can transport so many materials.
  - **High cohesion** + adhesion + capillary action → trees can transport water up to ~110 meters.
- **Elements of life:** CHNOPS (Carbon, Hydrogen, Nitrogen, Oxygen, Phosphorus, Sulfur) — make up ~99% of biological molecules.
- **Carbon skeleton:** carbon's 4 bonds enable diverse molecular structures. Foundation of organic chemistry.
- **Functional groups** (the chemistry "verbs" that make organic molecules reactive):
  - **Hydroxyl (-OH):** alcohols. Polar, hydrogen bonding capable.
  - **Carbonyl (-C=O):** aldehydes (terminal) or ketones (internal).
  - **Carboxyl (-COOH):** organic acids (e.g., amino acids, fatty acids). Donates H⁺ → acidic.
  - **Amino (-NH₂):** organic bases (e.g., amino acids). Accepts H⁺ → basic.
  - **Sulfhydryl (-SH):** in cysteine; can form disulfide bonds in proteins.
  - **Phosphate (-PO₄):** in nucleic acids, ATP. Energy currency.
  - **Methyl (-CH₃):** non-polar; affects gene expression when attached to DNA.
- **Four biological macromolecules** — built by dehydration synthesis from monomers; broken by hydrolysis:
  - **Carbohydrates:** monomers = monosaccharides (e.g., glucose). Polymers = disaccharides, polysaccharides.
  - **Lipids:** triglycerides = 3 fatty acids + glycerol. Phospholipids = 2 fatty acids + glycerol + phosphate group. Steroids = 4 fused rings (cholesterol, hormones).
  - **Proteins:** monomers = amino acids (20 types). Polymers = polypeptides → proteins.
  - **Nucleic acids:** monomers = nucleotides. Polymers = DNA, RNA.

- **Carbohydrates:**
  - **Function:** quick energy (glucose), energy storage (starch in plants, glycogen in animals), structure (cellulose in plant cell walls, chitin in insect exoskeletons).
  - **Monosaccharides:** glucose, fructose, galactose. Generally formula (CH₂O)n.
  - **Disaccharides:** sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose), maltose (glucose + glucose).
  - **Polysaccharides:**
    - **Starch:** plant energy storage. Made of α-glucose. Easily digestible.
    - **Glycogen:** animal energy storage. Highly branched. Stored mainly in liver and muscle.
    - **Cellulose:** plant cell wall structure. Made of β-glucose. Indigestible to most animals (humans lack cellulase).
    - **Chitin:** arthropod exoskeletons, fungal cell walls. Modified glucose.
- **Lipids** (NOT polymers in true sense — they're not made of repeating monomers):
  - **Triglycerides (fats and oils):** 3 fatty acids + glycerol. Energy storage, insulation. Saturated (no double bonds, solid at room temp, animal fats) vs unsaturated (double bonds, liquid at room temp, oils).
  - **Phospholipids:** 2 fatty acids + glycerol + phosphate group. AMPHIPATHIC — hydrophilic head + hydrophobic tail. Form bilayers in water → cell membranes.
  - **Steroids:** 4 fused carbon rings. Cholesterol (membrane component, hormone precursor), testosterone, estrogen.
- **Proteins:**
  - **Monomers:** amino acids — central carbon (alpha carbon) bonded to: amino group (-NH₂), carboxyl group (-COOH), hydrogen, and an R group (variable side chain that defines the amino acid).
  - **20 amino acids** with different R groups → different chemical properties (polar, nonpolar, charged, hydrophobic, etc.).
  - **Peptide bond:** formed between carboxyl of one amino acid and amino of another, releasing water (dehydration synthesis).
  - **Four levels of protein structure:**
    - **Primary:** linear sequence of amino acids. Determined by gene.
    - **Secondary:** local folding. Alpha helix or beta pleated sheet. Stabilized by HYDROGEN BONDS in the backbone.
    - **Tertiary:** overall 3D shape. Stabilized by R-group interactions (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, disulfide bridges between cysteines).
    - **Quaternary:** multiple polypeptide subunits joined together (e.g., hemoglobin has 4 subunits).
- **Denaturation:** loss of protein structure (and function) due to disrupted bonds. Caused by high temperature, extreme pH, certain chemicals. Often irreversible.
- **Nucleic acids:**
  - **DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid):** stores genetic information. Double-stranded helix.
  - **RNA (ribonucleic acid):** functions in protein synthesis. Usually single-stranded.
  - **Nucleotide structure:** 5-carbon sugar + phosphate group + nitrogenous base.
  - **Bases:**
    - **Purines:** adenine (A), guanine (G). Two-ring structure.
    - **Pyrimidines:** cytosine (C), thymine (T, in DNA), uracil (U, in RNA). One-ring structure.
  - **Base pairing (in DNA):** A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds), G pairs with C (3 hydrogen bonds).
  - **In RNA:** A pairs with U.
  - **Sugar-phosphate backbone:** alternating sugars and phosphates form the strands.
  - **Direction:** 5' end (free phosphate) and 3' end (free hydroxyl). DNA strands are antiparallel.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why ice floats matters biologically.** When water freezes, hydrogen bonds form a rigid open lattice that's about 10% less dense than liquid water. Result: ice floats. This insulates water below, allowing fish and other aquatic organisms to survive winter. If ice sank, lakes and oceans would freeze from the bottom up, eliminating most aquatic life.
- **Hydrophilic vs hydrophobic:**
  - **Hydrophilic ("water-loving"):** polar or charged. Dissolve in water. Examples: salts, sugars, amino acids.
  - **Hydrophobic ("water-fearing"):** nonpolar. Don't dissolve in water. Examples: fats, oils, hydrocarbons.
  - **Amphipathic:** both hydrophilic AND hydrophobic regions. Phospholipids are the key example — explains why they spontaneously form bilayers in water.
- **Why hydrophobic interactions form.** Hydrophobic molecules don't repel water; rather, they cluster together to MINIMIZE contact with water (which prefers to hydrogen-bond with itself rather than surround nonpolar molecules). This "hydrophobic effect" drives membrane formation, protein folding, and many other biological structures.
- **Saturated vs unsaturated fats:**
  - **Saturated:** no double bonds in fatty acid tails. Tails pack tightly together → solid at room temperature (butter, lard).
  - **Unsaturated:** one or more double bonds (creating "kinks" in tails). Don't pack as tightly → liquid at room temperature (olive oil, fish oil).
  - **Trans fats:** unsaturated fats with hydrogen atoms on opposite sides of double bond (instead of natural cis configuration). Behave more like saturated fats.
- **Why cell membranes need cholesterol.** Cholesterol embedded in phospholipid bilayer:
  - At HIGH temperatures: stabilizes membrane, prevents excessive fluidity.
  - At LOW temperatures: disrupts tight packing, prevents membrane from becoming too rigid.
  - Acts as a fluidity buffer.
- **Protein structure stability and bonds:**
  - **Primary structure:** held by COVALENT peptide bonds (very strong).
  - **Secondary, tertiary, quaternary:** held by NONCOVALENT interactions (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals) and DISULFIDE bridges (covalent, between cysteines).
  - When proteins denature, primary structure is preserved but higher levels are disrupted.
- **Enzymes** (foreshadowing Unit 3):
  - Globular proteins that catalyze biochemical reactions.
  - Active site has specific shape complementary to substrate.
  - Lock-and-key model (older) → induced fit model (more accurate — substrate and enzyme shape adjust slightly upon binding).
- **DNA structure details:**
  - **Sugar:** deoxyribose (no oxygen at 2' position).
  - **Bases:** A, T, G, C.
  - **Strands:** antiparallel (one runs 5' → 3', the other 3' → 5').
  - **Width of double helix:** ~2 nm.
  - **Stacked bases:** held together by van der Waals forces.
  - **Replication:** semiconservative — each new molecule has one old and one new strand (Unit 6).
- **RNA structure details:**
  - **Sugar:** ribose (oxygen at 2' position).
  - **Bases:** A, U, G, C (uracil instead of thymine).
  - **Usually single-stranded** (but can fold back on itself with intramolecular base pairing).
  - **Types:** mRNA (messenger), tRNA (transfer), rRNA (ribosomal).

### Adds for [5]

- **The "hydrophobic effect" as entropy-driven.** Counterintuitively, hydrophobic clustering is driven not by attraction between nonpolar molecules but by water's preference for hydrogen bonding with itself. When hydrophobic molecules disperse in water, water must form ordered cages around them (low entropy). When hydrophobic molecules cluster, water can form a single shell around the cluster (higher entropy). The system maximizes entropy, driving hydrophobic interactions.
- **Why proteins fold spontaneously.** Levinthal paradox: a protein could in principle explore an astronomical number of conformations, but folds in milliseconds to seconds. The energy landscape funnels folding — small changes in the right direction lead to thermodynamically favorable states. Misfolding causes diseases (Alzheimer's, prion diseases like BSE).
- **The "central dogma" and its extensions.** Crick (1958): information flow is DNA → RNA → protein. Modern biology recognizes:
  - **Reverse transcription** (RNA → DNA) in retroviruses (HIV).
  - **Epigenetic modifications** affecting gene expression without changing DNA sequence.
  - **Non-coding RNAs** that regulate gene expression without being translated.
  - The dogma remains useful but has been significantly expanded.
- **Why ATP is the universal energy currency.** ATP (adenosine triphosphate) has three phosphate groups. The bonds between phosphates contain stored energy. Hydrolysis of the terminal phosphate releases energy that can drive other reactions. ATP is rapidly recycled — humans use ~70 kg of ATP per day but maintain only ~250 g at any moment. The phosphate groups are repeatedly added (during cellular respiration) and removed (when energy is needed).
- **Why complementary base pairing matters beyond DNA structure.** A-T and G-C pairing enable:
  - **DNA replication** (template strand directs new strand synthesis).
  - **Transcription** (DNA template → mRNA).
  - **Translation** (mRNA codons pair with tRNA anticodons).
  - **Gene editing tools** (CRISPR uses RNA guides that base-pair with target DNA).
  - The same simple chemistry (hydrogen bonds between bases) underlies all the information processing of life.
- **Membrane fluidity and the "fluid mosaic" model.** Singer-Nicolson (1972): cell membrane is a 2D fluid with proteins floating in a phospholipid bilayer. Fluidity depends on:
  - Temperature (warmer = more fluid).
  - Cholesterol (buffers fluidity).
  - Saturated vs unsaturated fatty acids (saturated = less fluid).
  - This model replaced earlier "sandwich" models showing rigid protein layers.
- **Carbon as the "Goldilocks" element for life.** Why carbon? Forms 4 bonds (more than most elements), bonds are moderately strong (stable but breakable), can form chains and rings of varying complexity, and forms multiple bond types (single, double, triple). Silicon is the closest alternative but its bonds are too strong, limiting molecular diversity. The chemical foundation of carbon-based life isn't accidental — it reflects carbon's unique bonding properties.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Identifying macromolecule from monomer

For each monomer, identify the macromolecule family it belongs to:
(a) Glucose (b) Amino acid with side chain -CH(CH₃)₂ (c) Adenine attached to ribose and phosphate (d) Glycerol attached to three long carbon chains.

- **(a)** Glucose: a monosaccharide → CARBOHYDRATE.
- **(b)** Amino acid (with isopropyl side chain — this is leucine) → builds PROTEINS.
- **(c)** Adenine + ribose + phosphate = nucleotide → builds NUCLEIC ACIDS (could be DNA or RNA depending on whether ribose is deoxyribose).
- **(d)** Glycerol + 3 fatty acids = triglyceride → LIPID.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Predicting protein function from structure

A protein has:
- Many CYSTEINE amino acids in close proximity (forming disulfide bridges).
- Hydrophobic R groups in the interior; hydrophilic R groups on the exterior.

Predict its function and stability.

- **Hydrophobic interior + hydrophilic exterior** = typical for water-soluble proteins. The protein can dissolve in cytoplasm or blood. Likely function: enzyme, transport protein, or other soluble role.
- **Many disulfide bridges** = highly stable structure. Disulfide bridges are covalent bonds that resist denaturation. Likely a protein needing structural rigidity. Examples: keratin in hair, antibodies (immunoglobulins), insulin.

### Example 3 [4] — Why water's properties matter for life

A scientist studying alien biochemistry hypothesizes that life on a hot planet might use ammonia (NH₃) as its solvent instead of water.

Compare ammonia and water based on properties relevant to life:

- **Polarity:** Both polar (N is electronegative).
- **Hydrogen bonding:** Both can hydrogen bond, but water can form 4 H-bonds per molecule while ammonia can form 3. Water has stronger H-bond network.
- **Specific heat:** Water has very high specific heat (4.18 J/g·°C). Ammonia much lower. Ammonia would temperature-fluctuate more rapidly, making temperature-stable cellular environments harder.
- **Liquid range:** Water is liquid 0–100°C. Ammonia is liquid −78 to −33°C — only at very cold temperatures.
- **Density anomaly:** Water expands when freezing. Ammonia doesn't behave the same way.
- **Conclusion:** ammonia could in principle support life but only in narrow temperature ranges and with different biochemistry. Water's broader liquid range, higher heat capacity, and stronger H-bond network make it the more versatile life-supporting solvent.

### Example 4 [4] — Predicting effect of high temperature on protein

A protein function is essential for cell survival. The cell experiences fever (43°C, well above normal 37°C body temperature).

Predict the effect on the protein and the cell:

- **High temperature** disrupts the noncovalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions) that maintain protein secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structure.
- **Result:** DENATURATION — protein loses its 3D structure.
- **Functional consequence:** denatured protein cannot perform its function (substrate doesn't fit denatured active site, transport function lost, etc.).
- **Cellular consequence:** if many proteins denature, the cell dies. This is why prolonged fever above 41-42°C is dangerous.
- **Note:** primary structure (peptide bonds) is preserved — denaturation doesn't break the polypeptide chain, just disrupts folding. Some proteins can renature if conditions return to normal, but many denatured proteins cannot recover function.

### Example 5 [5] — Connecting structure to membrane function

Phospholipids spontaneously form bilayers in water. Explain WHY this occurs and how it relates to membrane function.

- **Step 1.** Phospholipid structure: hydrophilic phosphate head + hydrophobic fatty acid tails (amphipathic).
- **Step 2.** In water:
  - Hydrophilic heads orient TOWARD water (favorable interactions).
  - Hydrophobic tails orient AWAY from water (water excludes them).
  - Result: heads on outside, tails on inside → BILAYER forms spontaneously.
- **Step 3.** Why this matters for membrane function:
  - **Selective permeability:** hydrophobic interior allows small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂) to diffuse through but blocks polar/charged molecules (ions, sugars, large proteins) from passing freely.
  - **Stability:** the same hydrophobic effect that drives formation also keeps the bilayer intact in water.
  - **Membrane proteins:** can be embedded in or attached to the bilayer (integral vs peripheral).
  - **Self-sealing:** small punctures spontaneously close because the hydrophobic tails reseal away from water exposure.
- **Generalization:** phospholipid bilayer membranes appear in EVERY cell on Earth (and in cellular organelles). The same molecular logic that makes them spontaneous in water makes them universal to life.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing types of bonds.** Hydrogen bonds = WEAK noncovalent attractions between molecules. Covalent bonds = strong, share electrons. Don't conflate.
2. **Treating water as nonpolar.** Water is HIGHLY polar (oxygen is much more electronegative than hydrogen).
3. **Saying water has 2 hydrogen bonds.** Each water molecule can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds (2 as donor through its H atoms, 2 as acceptor through its lone pairs on O).
4. **Confusing carbohydrate types.** Starch (plant storage) vs glycogen (animal storage) vs cellulose (plant structure) vs chitin (arthropod exoskeleton).
5. **Calling lipids "polymers."** Lipids are NOT true polymers (no repeating monomers in the same way as carbohydrates, proteins, or nucleic acids). Triglycerides are closer to assemblies than polymers.
6. **Misidentifying amino acid components.** All 20 amino acids share: amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen, central carbon. The R group VARIES.
7. **Confusing levels of protein structure.** Primary = linear sequence. Secondary = local folding (helix, sheet). Tertiary = overall 3D shape. Quaternary = multiple subunits.
8. **Forgetting protein structure depends on R groups.** Tertiary structure is determined by interactions among R groups (which depends on amino acid sequence).
9. **Using "DNA" for both DNA and RNA.** DNA = deoxyribose, double-stranded, A-T-G-C. RNA = ribose, usually single-stranded, A-U-G-C.
10. **Confusing purines and pyrimidines.** Purines (A, G) = TWO-ring. Pyrimidines (C, T, U) = ONE-ring.
11. **Wrong base pairing.** DNA: A-T (2 H-bonds), G-C (3 H-bonds). RNA: A-U (instead of A-T).
12. **Treating hydrogen bonds as part of primary structure.** Primary structure is held by COVALENT peptide bonds. Hydrogen bonds stabilize secondary, tertiary, quaternary.
13. **Forgetting that denaturation doesn't break primary structure.** Denatured protein still has peptide bonds intact — only secondary, tertiary, quaternary disrupted.
14. **Confusing saturated and unsaturated.** Saturated = no C=C double bonds, solid at room temp. Unsaturated = has double bonds, liquid at room temp.
15. **Overlooking the directionality of nucleic acids.** DNA strands are ANTIPARALLEL (5'→3' on one strand, 3'→5' on the other). Critical for replication and transcription.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For macromolecule identification:**
- Identify monomer first.
- Connect to macromolecule family.
- Note function class (structural, enzymatic, storage, informational).

**For water properties questions:**
- Trace the property back to hydrogen bonding.
- Connect to a biological consequence.

**For protein structure questions:**
- Identify the level being discussed.
- Identify the bonds maintaining that level.
- Connect to function.

**For nucleic acid questions:**
- Distinguish DNA from RNA (sugar, bases, strands).
- Use correct base pairing.
- Note antiparallel strand orientation.

**For relating structure to function:**
- Always state the structural feature.
- State the chemical/physical property it produces.
- State the biological function it enables.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Water's polarity arises from oxygen's higher electronegativity, allowing each water molecule to form up to 4 hydrogen bonds. This hydrogen bonding network produces water's high specific heat, cohesion, and universal solvent properties — all essential for life."
2. "Phospholipids are amphipathic — having both hydrophilic phosphate heads and hydrophobic fatty acid tails. In water, they spontaneously form bilayers with heads facing outward and tails facing inward, providing the foundation for cell membranes."
3. "Protein function depends on its 3D structure (tertiary). The amino acid sequence (primary structure) determines folding through interactions among R groups: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bridges. Disrupting these interactions through heat or pH changes denatures the protein and destroys function."
4. "DNA stores genetic information through a sequence of nucleotide bases. Complementary base pairing (A-T, G-C) enables the precise replication needed for genetic inheritance and the transcription of information to mRNA for protein synthesis."
5. "All four biological macromolecules are built by DEHYDRATION SYNTHESIS (loss of water as monomers join) and broken down by HYDROLYSIS (addition of water to break bonds). This shared chemistry unifies the diverse macromolecule families."
6. "The hydrophobic effect drives the spontaneous assembly of biological structures: membranes (phospholipid bilayers), proteins (hydrophobic core, hydrophilic surface for water-soluble proteins), and DNA (stacked bases shielded from water in the interior of the helix)."
7. "Cellulose and starch are both polysaccharides of glucose, but their structural differences reflect functional differences: cellulose's β-glucose linkages create rigid, indigestible plant cell walls; starch's α-glucose linkages allow easy enzymatic breakdown for energy storage."
