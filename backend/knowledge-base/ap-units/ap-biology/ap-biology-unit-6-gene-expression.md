- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Unit 6 is the central molecular biology unit. The CENTRAL DOGMA (DNA → RNA → protein) is referenced throughout. Replication, transcription, translation are all essential and frequently tested. Mutation types and consequences appear regularly. Gene regulation (especially operons in prokaryotes; epigenetics and transcription factors in eukaryotes) is a key focus. Biotechnology techniques (PCR, gel electrophoresis, CRISPR) are perennial.

## Big Ideas

1. **DNA → RNA → Protein is the central dogma of molecular biology.** Information flows: DNA replicated → DNA transcribed to RNA → RNA translated to protein. Each step has specific machinery and follows specific rules.
2. **DNA replication is semiconservative.** Each daughter molecule contains one OLD strand (template) and one NEW strand (synthesized). DNA polymerase adds nucleotides 5' to 3' only.
3. **Transcription produces RNA from DNA template.** RNA polymerase reads template DNA strand 3' to 5' and synthesizes mRNA 5' to 3'. Eukaryotic mRNA undergoes processing (5' cap, poly-A tail, splicing) before leaving nucleus.
4. **Translation produces proteins from mRNA.** Ribosomes read mRNA codons (3 bases each); tRNAs deliver amino acids matching the codon-anticodon pairing. Protein synthesis is universal — same code in all life.
5. **Gene expression is highly regulated.** Cells with the same genome can express different genes, producing different cell types. Regulation can occur at transcription (most common), RNA processing, translation, or post-translation. Mutations change DNA; epigenetics change expression without changing DNA sequence.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **DNA structure (review from Unit 1):**
  - Double helix; antiparallel strands.
  - Sugar-phosphate backbone.
  - A-T (2 H-bonds), G-C (3 H-bonds).
  - Each strand runs 5' → 3' direction.
- **DNA replication (semiconservative):**
  - Each daughter molecule has one OLD strand (template) and one NEW strand.
  - **Steps:**
    - **Initiation:** helicase unwinds DNA at origins of replication.
    - **Elongation:**
      - Primase makes RNA primer.
      - DNA polymerase adds DNA nucleotides 5' to 3' from the primer.
      - One strand (LEADING) synthesized continuously.
      - Other strand (LAGGING) synthesized in fragments (Okazaki fragments) because polymerase only works 5' → 3'.
    - **Ligase** joins Okazaki fragments.
    - **Topoisomerase** relieves torsional stress ahead of replication fork.
- **Why DNA polymerase only synthesizes 5' to 3'.** It can only add new nucleotides to a free 3' OH end. Result: leading strand made smoothly; lagging strand made in fragments going AWAY from replication fork.
- **Transcription:**
  - Occurs in nucleus (in eukaryotes).
  - **RNA polymerase** reads DNA template strand (3' → 5') and synthesizes mRNA (5' → 3').
  - **In RNA, U replaces T** (so DNA's A pairs with RNA's U).
  - **Promoter:** DNA region where RNA polymerase binds (e.g., TATA box in eukaryotes).
  - **Termination:** RNA polymerase stops at terminator sequence.
- **mRNA processing (eukaryotes only — prokaryotic mRNA goes directly to translation):**
  - **5' cap** added (modified guanine — protects mRNA, signals to ribosome).
  - **Poly-A tail** added (long string of adenines at 3' end — protects mRNA from degradation).
  - **Splicing:** introns (non-coding) are removed; exons (coding) are joined together by spliceosome.
  - **Result:** mature mRNA exits nucleus to cytoplasm for translation.
- **Translation:**
  - Occurs at ribosomes in cytoplasm (or rough ER for membrane/secreted proteins).
  - **Ribosome** has small and large subunits.
  - Reads mRNA codons (groups of 3 bases) from 5' end.
  - **tRNA** has anticodon (3 bases) and carries one specific amino acid.
  - tRNA anticodon pairs with mRNA codon → delivers amino acid to ribosome.
  - Ribosome forms peptide bonds between amino acids.
  - Process continues until STOP codon (UAA, UAG, UGA) signals termination.
- **Genetic code:**
  - 4 bases (A, U, G, C) in groups of 3 → 64 possible codons.
  - 61 codons specify amino acids; 3 are STOP codons (UAA, UAG, UGA).
  - **AUG** is START codon AND codes for methionine.
  - **Code is REDUNDANT (degenerate)** — most amino acids have multiple codons (often differing in the third position).
  - **Code is UNIVERSAL** — same in essentially all life (with rare exceptions).
- **Mutations:**
  - **Point mutations** (single nucleotide change):
    - **Substitution:** one base replaced with another. Effects depend on resulting amino acid change.
      - **Silent:** codes for same amino acid (no effect on protein).
      - **Missense:** codes for different amino acid (may or may not affect protein function).
      - **Nonsense:** creates STOP codon (truncated protein, often nonfunctional).
    - **Insertion or deletion:** adds or removes a base, causing FRAMESHIFT — changes reading frame from that point onward, usually destroying protein function.
  - **Chromosomal mutations:** larger-scale changes:
    - **Deletion** of a chromosome segment.
    - **Duplication** of a segment.
    - **Inversion** (segment reversed).
    - **Translocation** (segment moved to another chromosome).
- **Gene regulation in prokaryotes — operon model:**
  - Operon = group of genes regulated together (with shared promoter).
  - **lac operon (in E. coli):** genes for lactose digestion. 
    - **Repressor protein** binds operator → blocks transcription.
    - When LACTOSE present: lactose binds repressor → repressor releases operator → transcription occurs.
    - This is INDUCIBLE OPERON.
  - **trp operon:** genes for tryptophan synthesis.
    - **Repressor protein** is INACTIVE alone.
    - When TRYPTOPHAN abundant: tryptophan binds repressor → activates it → blocks transcription.
    - This is REPRESSIBLE OPERON.
- **Gene regulation in eukaryotes (more complex):**
  - **Transcription factors** bind DNA regions (promoters, enhancers) to regulate transcription.
  - **Enhancers:** can act at long distances; regulate WHEN and WHERE genes are expressed.
  - **Repressors:** decrease transcription.
  - **Activators:** increase transcription.
  - **Chromatin structure:** DNA wrapped around histones. Tightly packed (heterochromatin) = inaccessible. Loosely packed (euchromatin) = accessible.
  - **Epigenetics:** modifications to DNA (methylation) or histones that affect gene expression WITHOUT changing DNA sequence. Heritable across cell divisions.
  - **Alternative splicing:** different exon combinations from same pre-mRNA produce different proteins from the same gene.
- **Cell differentiation:** different cells in a multicellular organism have the SAME genome but express DIFFERENT genes, producing different cell types. Result: skin cells, neurons, muscle cells all have same DNA but very different proteins.
- **Biotechnology techniques:**
  - **PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction):** amplifies specific DNA sequences using primers and DNA polymerase. Cycle of denaturation (95°C), annealing (50-65°C), extension (72°C).
  - **Gel electrophoresis:** separates DNA fragments by size. Smaller fragments migrate further. Used to analyze PCR products, identify individuals (DNA fingerprinting), detect mutations.
  - **CRISPR-Cas9:** gene editing technique. Cas9 protein (cuts DNA) guided by RNA to specific DNA sequence. Allows precise gene editing.
  - **Recombinant DNA:** combining DNA from different sources. Used to produce insulin in bacteria, GMO crops.
  - **Cloning:** asexual production of genetically identical organisms (Dolly the sheep, 1996).

### Adds for [4]

- **Why DNA replication needs primers.** DNA polymerase can ONLY ADD to an existing 3' OH end — it can't start from scratch. RNA primase synthesizes a short RNA primer that DNA polymerase can extend. Primers are later replaced with DNA.
- **Leading vs lagging strand details:**
  - **Leading strand:** the new strand synthesized continuously in the 5' → 3' direction TOWARD the replication fork. Smooth synthesis.
  - **Lagging strand:** the new strand synthesized in DISCONTINUOUS fragments (Okazaki fragments) going AWAY from the replication fork. Each fragment requires its own primer. Ligase joins fragments.
  - **Why this matters:** the requirement for new primers on the lagging strand explains telomere shortening — the very end of chromosomes can't be fully copied without an existing primer beyond. Telomerase compensates in some cell types.
- **Detailed replication enzymes:**
  - **Helicase:** unwinds DNA double helix at replication fork.
  - **Single-strand binding proteins:** keep separated strands apart.
  - **Topoisomerase:** relieves torsional stress ahead of fork.
  - **Primase:** synthesizes RNA primers.
  - **DNA polymerase III** (in bacteria): adds DNA nucleotides 5' → 3'.
  - **DNA polymerase I:** removes RNA primers and replaces with DNA.
  - **DNA ligase:** joins Okazaki fragments.
- **mRNA processing details:**
  - **5' cap (modified guanine):** protects mRNA from degradation; required for ribosome recognition during translation.
  - **Poly-A tail (long string of adenines):** protects mRNA, helps with translation efficiency.
  - **Splicing:** intron-exon recognition by spliceosome (RNA-protein complex). Introns are removed; exons joined.
  - **Alternative splicing:** different exons can be combined differently → multiple proteins from one gene. Increases proteome diversity.
- **Genetic code redundancy explained:**
  - **64 possible codons** (4³).
  - **20 amino acids** + 3 stop codons.
  - Each amino acid has 1-6 codons.
  - Most amino acids have synonymous codons that differ in the THIRD POSITION (wobble effect).
  - **Implication:** point mutations in the third position are often SILENT (don't change amino acid).
- **Codon-anticodon pairing in translation:**
  - mRNA codon read 5' → 3'.
  - tRNA anticodon pairs antiparallel (3' → 5').
  - Each tRNA has a specific amino acid at the other end.
  - Multiple tRNAs (for different codons of same amino acid) can deliver same amino acid.
- **Mutation effects on protein function:**
  - **Silent mutation** (third position usually): no protein change.
  - **Missense:** one amino acid changed. Effects depend on which amino acid (e.g., R group similarity, location in protein).
  - **Nonsense:** STOP codon → truncated protein (usually nonfunctional). Often severe.
  - **Insertion/deletion (frameshift):** changes reading frame for all subsequent codons. Almost always destroys protein function.
- **Operon regulation comparison:**
  - **Inducible operon (lac):** OFF by default; turned ON by inducer (lactose). Adapted for using sugar when present.
  - **Repressible operon (trp):** ON by default; turned OFF by corepressor (tryptophan). Adapted for making amino acid only when needed.
  - Both use repressor proteins, but with opposite logic.
- **Transcription factors as gene-expression regulators:**
  - Bind DNA at promoters or enhancers.
  - **General transcription factors** assemble at promoter to start transcription.
  - **Specific transcription factors** activate or repress particular genes in response to cellular signals.
  - **Enhancers** can be far from the gene (thousands of bases) but loop to bring transcription factors near the promoter.
- **Epigenetic mechanisms:**
  - **DNA methylation:** addition of methyl groups to cytosine (typically at CpG sites). Usually represses transcription. Heritable.
  - **Histone modification:** acetylation typically loosens chromatin (allows transcription). Methylation can either activate or repress depending on the residue.
  - **Examples of epigenetic effects:** identical twins develop different patterns of gene expression; environmental factors (smoking, stress, nutrition) can affect epigenetics; some epigenetic changes pass to offspring.
- **PCR worked through:**
  - **Step 1 (denaturation, ~95°C):** DNA strands separate.
  - **Step 2 (annealing, ~50-65°C):** primers bind to specific sequences on each strand.
  - **Step 3 (extension, ~72°C):** Taq polymerase extends primers using free nucleotides.
  - **Each cycle DOUBLES DNA amount.** 30 cycles → ~10⁹ copies from a single original.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why DNA replication evolution required complex machinery.** Faithful DNA replication requires:
  - Unwinding the helix (helicase).
  - Maintaining single-stranded segments (SSBs).
  - Relieving topological stress (topoisomerase).
  - Initiating synthesis (primase makes primers).
  - Faithful copying with proofreading (DNA polymerase).
  - Joining fragments (ligase).
  - Removing primers and filling gaps.
  - Each function is essential; the evolution of each component is itself a remarkable story.
- **The "central dogma" extended.** Modern molecular biology recognizes:
  - **Reverse transcription** (RNA → DNA) in retroviruses (HIV) and in eukaryotic genome integration.
  - **Non-coding RNAs** (miRNA, lncRNA, tRNA, rRNA, etc.) that regulate without being translated.
  - **Epigenetic regulation** affecting which genes are expressed.
  - **Post-translational modifications** affecting protein function.
  - The original DNA → RNA → protein dogma remains useful but has been substantially expanded.
- **Why alternative splicing dramatically increases proteome diversity.** Humans have ~20,000 protein-coding genes but ~100,000 different proteins. The difference comes largely from alternative splicing — different exon combinations produce different proteins. One gene can encode dozens of different proteins through alternative splicing, vastly expanding the proteome from a fixed genome.
- **The "lac operon" as classic example of gene regulation.** Discovered by Jacob and Monod (1961, Nobel Prize). The lac operon was the first genetic regulatory mechanism understood at the molecular level. Its principles (inducible operon, repressor protein, operator, structural genes) became the foundation for understanding all subsequent gene regulation. The simplicity of the lac operon (just a few genes) made it tractable; its mechanism turned out to be applicable broadly.
- **Why eukaryotic gene regulation is much more complex than prokaryotic.** Eukaryotes have:
  - Larger genomes (more genes to regulate).
  - Cellular differentiation (different cell types from same genome).
  - Spatial regulation (cells in different positions express different genes).
  - Temporal regulation (development changes which genes are active).
  - This requires multilayered regulation: chromatin structure, transcription factors, alternative splicing, post-transcriptional regulation, post-translational modification — a regulatory complexity vastly exceeding prokaryotes.
- **Why mutations are essential to evolution despite usually being harmful.** Most mutations are neutral (third codon position) or harmful (missense, nonsense, frameshift). But some mutations are beneficial — providing the genetic variation natural selection acts on. Without mutations, evolution would have no raw material. The MUTATION RATE (errors per base per replication) is balanced by selection — too many mutations would kill organisms; too few wouldn't allow adaptation. Cells have evolved repair machinery (proofreading by DNA polymerase, mismatch repair, etc.) to keep mutations at the optimal rate.
- **CRISPR-Cas9 and the gene editing revolution.** Discovered as bacterial immune system (cuts viral DNA). Repurposed (2012) as gene editing tool. Allows precise modification of DNA sequences. Has revolutionized:
  - Basic research (creating disease models in cells and animals).
  - Agriculture (improving crops without traditional GMO concerns).
  - Medicine (potential gene therapy for inherited diseases like sickle cell anemia, beta-thalassemia).
  - Ethical concerns: germline editing (changes that pass to offspring) is contested; embryo editing has been done illegally in some cases.
- **The "junk DNA" controversy.** Originally, ~98% of human genome was called "junk DNA" (non-coding, no apparent function). Modern research (ENCODE project) has shown much of this DNA has regulatory function: enhancers, promoters, non-coding RNA genes, control elements. The "junk DNA" terminology is now controversial — much of it has biological roles, even if not encoding proteins.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — DNA Replication step-by-step

A double-stranded DNA molecule is undergoing replication.

(a) Identify the enzymes needed at each step.
(b) Explain WHY DNA polymerase needs a primer.
(c) Compare leading and lagging strands.

- **(a) Enzymes:**
  - **Helicase:** unwinds DNA double helix.
  - **Single-strand binding proteins (SSBs):** keep strands apart.
  - **Topoisomerase:** relieves torsional stress ahead.
  - **Primase:** makes RNA primers.
  - **DNA polymerase III:** synthesizes DNA 5' → 3' from primers.
  - **DNA polymerase I:** replaces primers with DNA.
  - **Ligase:** joins Okazaki fragments.
- **(b)** DNA polymerase can ONLY ADD to existing 3' OH end; cannot start new strand. RNA primase synthesizes short RNA primer that DNA polymerase extends.
- **(c)** **Leading strand:** synthesized continuously, in 5' → 3' direction TOWARD replication fork. **Lagging strand:** synthesized in fragments (Okazaki fragments) AWAY from fork. Each fragment requires its own primer; ligase joins them.

### Example 2 [3] — Translation worked through

An mRNA has the sequence: 5' AUG GCC CAU CCU UAA 3'

(a) Identify the codons.
(b) Determine amino acids using the genetic code.
(c) Identify the protein sequence.

- **(a) Codons:** AUG | GCC | CAU | CCU | UAA
- **(b) Amino acids:**
  - AUG = Methionine (Met) — also the START codon.
  - GCC = Alanine (Ala).
  - CAU = Histidine (His).
  - CCU = Proline (Pro).
  - UAA = STOP codon. Translation terminates.
- **(c) Protein:** Met-Ala-His-Pro (4 amino acids). The STOP codon doesn't add an amino acid; it just signals termination.

### Example 3 [4] — Mutation effects

A protein has the original mRNA: 5' AUG CGU UAC GAU UAA 3' (Met-Arg-Tyr-Asp-STOP).

For each mutation, predict the effect:
(a) AUG CGU UAU GAU UAA (3rd codon UAC → UAU)
(b) AUG CGU UAA GAU UAA (3rd codon UAC → UAA)
(c) AUG CGU UCC GAU UAA (3rd codon UAC → UCC)
(d) AUG CG UAC GAU UAA (deletion of one nucleotide)

- **(a) Silent mutation.** UAU also codes for Tyrosine (Tyr) — same amino acid. NO PROTEIN CHANGE.
- **(b) Nonsense mutation.** UAA is a STOP codon. Translation stops EARLY (after Arg). Truncated protein (likely nonfunctional).
- **(c) Missense mutation.** UCC codes for Serine (Ser). Amino acid changed (Tyr → Ser). Effect depends on importance of that amino acid for protein function.
- **(d) Frameshift mutation.** Deletion shifts reading frame. From the deletion point, all subsequent codons are different. Protein sequence completely changed. Almost always nonfunctional protein.

### Example 4 [4] — lac operon regulation

A culture of E. coli is grown in different conditions.

(a) Glucose only (no lactose): predict lac operon state.
(b) Lactose only (no glucose): predict lac operon state.

- **(a) Glucose only, no lactose:** lac operon is OFF.
  - Without lactose, repressor protein binds the operator.
  - RNA polymerase blocked.
  - Lactose-digesting enzymes NOT produced.
  - Cell is metabolizing glucose via standard glycolysis.
- **(b) Lactose only, no glucose:** lac operon is ON.
  - Lactose binds the repressor → repressor changes shape and releases operator.
  - RNA polymerase can transcribe.
  - Lactose-digesting enzymes are produced.
  - Cell can metabolize lactose for energy.
- **Why this is "inducible":** the operon is OFF unless an inducer (lactose) is present. This is logical — making lactose-digesting enzymes when there's no lactose would waste energy.

### Example 5 [5] — CRISPR-Cas9 mechanism

A researcher wants to edit a specific gene to correct a disease-causing mutation.

(a) Describe the CRISPR-Cas9 mechanism.
(b) What are the ethical considerations?

- **(a) Mechanism:**
  - **Step 1:** Design a guide RNA (gRNA) complementary to the target DNA sequence.
  - **Step 2:** Cas9 protein + gRNA enter cell.
  - **Step 3:** Cas9 + gRNA find target DNA via complementary base pairing.
  - **Step 4:** Cas9 cuts both DNA strands at target site.
  - **Step 5:** Cell repairs the cut, often using a provided template DNA — the corrected sequence.
  - **Result:** specific DNA edit at chosen location.
- **(b) Ethical considerations:**
  - **Somatic cell editing:** changes stay in the patient; widely accepted for therapy.
  - **Germline editing:** changes pass to offspring; very controversial (consent issues across generations, unintended consequences, "designer babies").
  - **Off-target effects:** Cas9 may cut at unintended sites, causing unintended mutations.
  - **Equitable access:** therapies costly; benefits may be limited to wealthy patients.
  - **Scientific community has called for caution** (e.g., 2018 controversy when Chinese scientist edited human embryos using CRISPR — widely condemned).

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing replication, transcription, and translation.** Replication: DNA → DNA (semiconservative). Transcription: DNA → RNA. Translation: RNA → protein.
2. **Forgetting DNA polymerase only synthesizes 5' → 3'.** This is why lagging strand requires Okazaki fragments.
3. **Misnaming components of mRNA processing.** 5' cap, poly-A tail, splicing (introns out, exons in).
4. **Confusing introns and exons.** **Exons EXIT** the nucleus (they're in the final mature mRNA). **Introns INTERVENE** but are spliced out.
5. **Wrong direction of strand reading.** RNA polymerase reads template DNA 3' → 5'; synthesizes mRNA 5' → 3'. Ribosome reads mRNA 5' → 3'.
6. **Confusing codon and anticodon.** **Codon** is on mRNA. **Anticodon** is on tRNA. They pair antiparallel and complementary.
7. **Wrong number of stop codons.** Three stop codons: UAA, UAG, UGA.
8. **Misnaming mutation types.** Silent (no change), missense (different AA), nonsense (stop codon), frameshift (insertion/deletion shifts reading frame).
9. **Confusing inducible and repressible operons.** Inducible (lac): OFF by default, ON when inducer present. Repressible (trp): ON by default, OFF when corepressor present.
10. **Treating prokaryotic and eukaryotic gene regulation as similar.** Eukaryotic gene regulation is much more complex (multi-step, transcription factors, epigenetics, alternative splicing).
11. **Missing the universal nature of the genetic code.** Same code in essentially all life (with a few rare exceptions). This evidence supports common ancestry.
12. **Forgetting alternative splicing.** Different exon combinations produce different proteins from one gene. Greatly increases proteome diversity.
13. **Confusing methylation effects.** DNA methylation typically REPRESSES gene expression. Histone acetylation typically ACTIVATES.
14. **Missing the central dogma's nuances.** Reverse transcription exists (retroviruses). Non-coding RNAs are functional. Epigenetics affects expression without changing DNA.
15. **Treating biotechnology as just one technique.** PCR, gel electrophoresis, CRISPR-Cas9, recombinant DNA all have specific purposes and mechanisms.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For replication questions:**
- Identify the enzymes and their roles.
- Explain semiconservative nature.
- Distinguish leading from lagging strand.

**For transcription questions:**
- Identify location (nucleus in eukaryotes).
- Identify enzyme (RNA polymerase).
- Note RNA processing in eukaryotes (5' cap, poly-A tail, splicing).

**For translation questions:**
- Identify codons (3 bases each).
- Identify start (AUG) and stop (UAA, UAG, UGA) codons.
- Use genetic code chart for amino acids.
- Trace tRNA-mRNA pairing.

**For mutation questions:**
- Identify mutation type (substitution: silent, missense, nonsense; insertion/deletion: frameshift).
- Predict protein effect.
- Connect to functional consequences.

**For operon questions:**
- Identify type (inducible vs repressible).
- Trace regulation logic.
- Note when the operon is ON vs OFF.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "DNA replication is SEMICONSERVATIVE: each daughter molecule contains one OLD strand (template) and one NEW strand (synthesized). DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides 5' → 3', requiring an RNA primer to begin synthesis."
2. "The leading strand is synthesized continuously in the direction of the replication fork. The lagging strand is synthesized in fragments (Okazaki fragments) away from the fork because DNA polymerase only works 5' → 3'. Ligase joins the fragments."
3. "Eukaryotic mRNA undergoes processing before translation: a 5' cap (modified guanine) is added for stability and ribosome recognition; a poly-A tail (long string of adenines) is added at the 3' end for stability; introns (non-coding regions) are removed by the spliceosome, and exons (coding regions) are joined together."
4. "Translation reads mRNA codons (3 bases each) from 5' to 3'. tRNAs deliver amino acids matching their anticodon to the codon. The genetic code is REDUNDANT (multiple codons per amino acid, often differing in the third position) and UNIVERSAL (same code in essentially all life)."
5. "A nonsense mutation creates a STOP codon (UAA, UAG, or UGA) where one didn't exist before, terminating translation early and producing a truncated, often nonfunctional protein. Frameshift mutations (insertions or deletions) change the reading frame for all subsequent codons, almost always destroying protein function."
6. "The lac operon is INDUCIBLE: when lactose is absent, the repressor protein binds the operator, blocking transcription. When lactose is present, lactose binds the repressor, releasing the operator, and the lac operon is transcribed. This regulation conserves cellular resources by producing lactose-digesting enzymes only when lactose is available."
7. "Eukaryotic gene regulation is multilayered: chromatin structure (DNA methylation and histone modifications determine accessibility), transcription factors (activators and repressors at promoters and enhancers), alternative splicing (different exon combinations from same gene), and post-translational modifications. This complexity allows the same genome to produce many different cell types."
