## Big Ideas

1. **Evolution = change in allele frequencies in a population over time.** This is the population-level definition. Individuals don't evolve; populations do. Mutations create variation; selection (and other forces) change frequencies.
2. **Natural selection acts on phenotypes, not directly on genotypes.** Individuals with phenotypes better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more. Their alleles become more common in the next generation.
3. **Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium describes a population with NO evolution occurring.** Five conditions: no mutation, no migration, large population, random mating, no selection. The equation p² + 2pq + q² = 1 lets us calculate genotype frequencies from allele frequencies.
4. **Multiple lines of evidence support evolution.** Fossils, comparative anatomy (homologous structures), embryology, biogeography, molecular biology (DNA/protein similarities) all converge on common descent.
5. **Speciation creates new species through reproductive isolation.** Allopatric (geographic separation) is most common. Sympatric (without geographic separation) is rarer. Reproductive isolation can be prezygotic (before fertilization) or postzygotic (after fertilization).

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Evolution:** change in allele frequencies in a population over time.
- **Natural selection** (Darwin and Wallace, 1858):
  - **Variation:** individuals in a population differ.
  - **Heritability:** some variations are inherited.
  - **Differential reproduction:** individuals with favorable variations leave more offspring.
  - **Result:** favorable variations become more common over generations.
- **Fitness:** reproductive success. NOT physical strength. The "fittest" produces the most offspring that survive to reproduce themselves.
- **Adaptations:** inherited traits that increase fitness in a particular environment.
- **Types of natural selection** (based on which phenotypes are favored):
  - **Directional selection:** favors one extreme. Population shifts toward that extreme. Example: industrial melanism in peppered moths (dark moths favored when trees darkened by pollution).
  - **Stabilizing selection:** favors intermediate phenotype. Reduces variation. Example: human birth weight (very small AND very large babies have lower survival).
  - **Disruptive selection:** favors both extremes; eliminates intermediates. Can lead to speciation. Example: bird beaks where both small and large work but medium doesn't (different food sources).
- **Sexual selection:** form of natural selection where mate choice drives evolution.
  - **Intersexual selection:** one sex chooses mates (often females choose males with elaborate traits).
  - **Intrasexual selection:** competition within one sex (often males competing with each other).
  - **Result:** can produce traits that LOWER survival but INCREASE mating success (peacock tails, deer antlers).
- **Artificial selection:** humans select traits in domesticated species. Same principle as natural selection but with human-chosen criteria. Examples: dog breeds, agricultural crops, livestock breeds.
- **Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium:**
  - **Equation 1:** p + q = 1 (allele frequencies sum to 1).
  - **Equation 2:** p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (genotype frequencies).
    - p² = frequency of homozygous dominant.
    - 2pq = frequency of heterozygous.
    - q² = frequency of homozygous recessive.
- **Five conditions for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (population NOT evolving):**
  - **(1) No mutation.**
  - **(2) No migration (gene flow).**
  - **(3) Very large population (no genetic drift).**
  - **(4) Random mating.**
  - **(5) No natural selection.**
  - **In reality:** these conditions are rarely all met. Hardy-Weinberg serves as a NULL HYPOTHESIS — if observed allele frequencies differ from H-W expectations, evolution is occurring.
- **Evidence for evolution:**
  - **Fossil record:** transitional fossils (whale ancestors, Tiktaalik between fish and tetrapods, Archaeopteryx between reptiles and birds).
  - **Comparative anatomy:**
    - **Homologous structures:** same evolutionary origin, different function (human arm, bat wing, whale flipper — all have same bone structure).
    - **Analogous structures:** different evolutionary origins, similar function (insect wing vs bird wing — both fly but evolved separately).
    - **Vestigial structures:** reduced/non-functional remnants from ancestors (whale pelvic bones, human appendix).
  - **Embryology:** similar embryonic development in related species (vertebrate embryos look similar early).
  - **Biogeography:** geographic distribution of species reflects evolutionary history (Australian marsupials, Galapagos finches).
  - **Molecular biology:** DNA and protein similarities reveal evolutionary relationships. The MORE SIMILAR the molecules, the more recent the common ancestor.
- **Phylogeny:** the evolutionary history of a species or group.
- **Phylogenetic trees:**
  - Show evolutionary relationships.
  - Branches represent lineages.
  - Nodes represent common ancestors.
  - **Reading trees:** organisms sharing more recent common ancestors are MORE closely related.
  - **Common ancestor:** organisms at the closest node before divergence.
- **Speciation:** formation of new species.
- **Modes of speciation:**
  - **Allopatric (most common):** geographic separation prevents gene flow → populations diverge → eventually become reproductively isolated. Example: Galapagos finches.
  - **Sympatric:** speciation WITHOUT geographic separation. Through ecological niches, sexual selection, or polyploidy (especially in plants).
- **Reproductive isolation mechanisms:**
  - **Prezygotic (before fertilization):**
    - **Habitat isolation:** different ecological niches.
    - **Temporal isolation:** different breeding times.
    - **Behavioral isolation:** different courtship behaviors.
    - **Mechanical isolation:** anatomically incompatible.
    - **Gametic isolation:** sperm and egg incompatible.
  - **Postzygotic (after fertilization):**
    - **Reduced hybrid viability:** hybrids don't survive.
    - **Reduced hybrid fertility:** hybrids are sterile (e.g., mules from horse × donkey).
    - **Hybrid breakdown:** F2 generations are weak or sterile.
- **Genetic drift:** random changes in allele frequencies, especially in small populations.
- **Bottleneck effect:** drastic reduction in population size → loss of genetic diversity. Example: cheetahs (extreme bottleneck reduced their genetic diversity).
- **Founder effect:** small group leaves to start new population → new population's gene pool reflects founders' alleles, not original population. Example: Pingelap atoll inhabitants (high frequency of color blindness).
- **Gene flow:** migration of alleles between populations through breeding.

### Adds for [4]

- **Hardy-Weinberg worked example:**
  - Question: 36% of population shows recessive phenotype. Calculate p, q, frequency of heterozygotes, frequency of homozygous dominants.
  - Step 1: q² = 0.36 → q = 0.6.
  - Step 2: p = 1 − q = 0.4.
  - Step 3: 2pq = 2(0.4)(0.6) = 0.48 → 48% heterozygous.
  - Step 4: p² = (0.4)² = 0.16 → 16% homozygous dominant.
  - Verify: p² + 2pq + q² = 0.16 + 0.48 + 0.36 = 1.00 ✓.
- **Why Hardy-Weinberg matters as a NULL hypothesis:**
  - Real populations rarely meet all 5 conditions.
  - When observed frequencies differ from H-W expectation, evolution is occurring.
  - Quantifying the difference reveals which evolutionary force is at work.
- **Why molecular evidence is so powerful for phylogeny.** DNA sequences, protein sequences, and shared genetic features (like having mitochondria) provide MOLECULAR clocks. Mutations accumulate at relatively constant rates over time. Comparing DNA between species reveals approximate divergence dates. Modern phylogenetic trees use molecular data extensively, often confirming older trees built from morphology but sometimes revealing surprising relationships (e.g., whales' close relationship to hippos rather than to other "fish-like" creatures).
- **Endosymbiotic theory revisited (Unit 2 + Unit 7).** Mitochondria and chloroplasts originated from free-living bacteria. Evidence: own DNA, own ribosomes, double membranes, binary fission. This is itself a major evolutionary event — the origin of eukaryotic cells via symbiosis.
- **Punctuated equilibrium vs gradualism:**
  - **Gradualism:** evolution happens slowly and steadily. Original Darwinian view.
  - **Punctuated equilibrium (Eldredge and Gould, 1972):** long periods of stasis interrupted by rapid bursts of speciation. Better fits some fossil records (transitions appear sudden in geological time).
  - Modern view: both patterns occur in different lineages and at different times.
- **Speciation worked through (allopatric example):**
  - **Step 1:** Single population isolated by geographic barrier (mountain range, river).
  - **Step 2:** Two populations evolve independently (different selection pressures, different mutations, different drift).
  - **Step 3:** Over time, populations become so different that even if they meet again, they cannot interbreed (reproductive isolation).
  - **Result:** two species from one ancestral species.
- **Coevolution:** species evolve in response to each other. Examples:
  - Predators and prey (lions and antelope).
  - Parasites and hosts (immune system arms race).
  - Plants and pollinators (flower shapes match pollinator anatomy).
  - Mutualistic relationships (bees and flowers).
- **Convergent evolution:** unrelated species evolve similar features for similar environments. Examples:
  - Wings of birds, bats, and insects (different lineages, similar function).
  - Streamlined body shapes of fish, dolphins, and ichthyosaurs.
  - Eye-like structures in mollusks, vertebrates, and arthropods.
- **Adaptive radiation:** rapid diversification from a common ancestor into many forms adapted to different niches. Classic examples:
  - **Galapagos finches:** beak shape adapted to different food sources.
  - **Hawaiian honeycreepers:** bill shapes adapted to different ecological roles.
  - **African cichlids:** species diversified into different ecological niches in lakes.
- **Mass extinctions and their evolutionary impact:** five major mass extinctions in Earth's history have wiped out many species, opening niches for new species. The K-Pg extinction (66 mya, asteroid + volcanism) eliminated dinosaurs, allowing mammals to diversify.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why the gene pool is the level of evolution.** A single individual's genome is fixed at conception (with rare exceptions). Individuals don't evolve. POPULATIONS evolve because allele frequencies in the gene pool change. This is why population genetics is the mathematical foundation of evolution.
- **Why deviations from Hardy-Weinberg reveal evolution.** Hardy-Weinberg gives expected frequencies under no evolution. Real frequencies differ from expectations because:
  - **Mutation** introduces new alleles.
  - **Migration** changes allele frequencies via gene flow.
  - **Genetic drift** in small populations randomizes frequencies.
  - **Non-random mating** changes genotype distributions.
  - **Natural selection** changes allele frequencies based on fitness.
- **The Modern Synthesis (1930s-40s).** Combining Darwin's natural selection with Mendelian genetics produced modern evolutionary theory. Key contributors: R.A. Fisher (statistical methods), J.B.S. Haldane (mathematical models), Sewall Wright (genetic drift), Theodosius Dobzhansky (population genetics in the wild). The synthesis showed that natural selection acts on the genetic variation produced by mutation and recombination, with measurable effects on allele frequencies.
- **Evo-Devo (Evolutionary Developmental Biology).** Studies how changes in development produce evolutionary change. Hox genes (master regulatory genes for body plan) are remarkably conserved across all bilateral animals. Mutations in regulatory genes can produce dramatic morphological change with relatively little molecular change. This explains how whales evolved from land mammals through changes in regulatory gene expression rather than wholesale genome rewriting.
- **Why "race" is not a biologically meaningful category.** Modern genetics has shown:
  - Genetic variation WITHIN socially-defined races is greater than variation BETWEEN races.
  - Most genetic variation occurred in Africa (humans evolved there); subsequent migration carried subsets of this variation to other continents.
  - "Racial" categories don't correspond to discrete genetic clusters; they're social constructs that humans apply to continuous genetic variation.
  - This doesn't mean genetic variation isn't real — it just means socially-constructed racial categories don't track biological reality.
- **The "tree of life" concept and its complications.** Traditional phylogenetic trees show a branching pattern. But:
  - **Horizontal gene transfer** (especially in prokaryotes) means genes can move between lineages, complicating the tree pattern.
  - **Hybridization** between species (especially plants and some animals) creates reticulated patterns.
  - **Polyploidy** (genome doubling) creates new species in single events.
  - Modern phylogenetics uses both tree and network representations.
- **Why mass extinctions accelerate evolution.** When many species go extinct simultaneously, ecological niches become vacant. Surviving species rapidly diversify to fill these niches. The K-Pg extinction (asteroid impact 66 mya) eliminated all non-avian dinosaurs but left mammals to fill their niches. Modern mammalian diversity (including humans) is partly the result of this opportunity.
- **Origin of life hypotheses:**
  - **Primordial soup (Miller-Urey 1953):** simple molecules in early Earth's atmosphere could form complex organic molecules with energy input (lightning).
  - **RNA world hypothesis:** RNA can both store information AND catalyze reactions, suggesting it could be the basis of early life before DNA and proteins evolved.
  - **Hydrothermal vents:** chemical and thermal gradients at deep-sea vents could provide energy and surfaces for early biochemistry.
  - **Panspermia:** life originated elsewhere and was delivered to Earth (largely speculative).
  - All hypotheses agree that life on Earth originated ~3.5-4 billion years ago and shares common ancestry (universal genetic code, common molecular machinery).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Hardy-Weinberg calculation

In a population, 64% of individuals show the recessive phenotype.

(a) Calculate q (recessive allele frequency).
(b) Calculate p (dominant allele frequency).
(c) Calculate frequencies of homozygous dominant, heterozygous, and homozygous recessive genotypes.

- **(a)** q² = 0.64 → q = √0.64 = 0.8.
- **(b)** p = 1 − q = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2.
- **(c)** Genotype frequencies:
  - p² = (0.2)² = 0.04 → 4% homozygous dominant.
  - 2pq = 2(0.2)(0.8) = 0.32 → 32% heterozygous.
  - q² = (0.8)² = 0.64 → 64% homozygous recessive (matches given).
  - Verify: 0.04 + 0.32 + 0.64 = 1.00 ✓.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Natural selection types

For each scenario, identify the type of natural selection (directional, stabilizing, or disruptive):

(a) Average human birth weight (~7 lbs) has highest survival; very small and very large babies have lower survival.
(b) After industrial pollution darkens trees, dark-colored peppered moths increase in frequency.
(c) On an island, only birds with very small or very large beaks can crack the available seeds (medium beaks struggle).

- **(a) Stabilizing selection.** Favors intermediate phenotype; reduces variation.
- **(b) Directional selection.** Favors one extreme (dark phenotype); population shifts in that direction.
- **(c) Disruptive selection.** Favors both extremes; eliminates intermediates. Can lead to speciation if the two extremes become reproductively isolated.

### Example 3 [4] — Phylogenetic tree analysis

A phylogenetic tree shows: A and B share a recent common ancestor; C diverged earlier; D diverged before that.

(a) Which two species are most closely related?
(b) Which species is most distantly related to A?
(c) What does the order of branching tell us?

- **(a)** A and B (share most recent common ancestor).
- **(b)** D (diverged earliest from the lineage leading to A).
- **(c)** The order of branching shows the order of divergence in time. Species that diverged more recently share more recent common ancestors. The branching pattern reflects the evolutionary history.

### Example 4 [4] — Speciation worked through

A population of mice in a forest. A river forms, dividing the population into two groups (one north, one south).

(a) Identify the type of speciation possible.
(b) Predict what could happen.
(c) When would the two populations be considered separate species?

- **(a) Allopatric speciation** — geographic separation prevents gene flow.
- **(b)** Each population:
  - Experiences different selection pressures.
  - Accumulates different mutations.
  - Different alleles spread or are lost (genetic drift, especially if populations are small).
  - Diverges genetically over time.
- **(c)** Two populations are separate species when they are REPRODUCTIVELY ISOLATED — even if they meet again (e.g., river dries up), they cannot produce viable, fertile offspring. Reproductive isolation can be prezygotic (different mating times, behaviors) or postzygotic (hybrids inviable or sterile).

### Example 5 [5] — Multiple lines of evidence for evolution

A student questions whether evolution actually occurred. Provide multiple lines of evidence.

- **1. Fossil record:** transitional fossils show gradual change over time. Examples: Tiktaalik (between fish and tetrapod), Archaeopteryx (between reptile and bird), whale ancestors with leg bones (suggesting whales evolved from land mammals).
- **2. Comparative anatomy — Homologous structures:** human arm, bat wing, whale flipper, cat foreleg all have the same underlying bone structure (humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, etc.) despite different functions. This suggests common ancestry.
- **3. Vestigial structures:** human appendix, whale pelvic bones (no longer needed but inherited from ancestors).
- **4. Embryology:** vertebrate embryos look very similar in early development, then diverge. Suggests common developmental origin.
- **5. Biogeography:** geographic distribution reflects evolutionary history. Australia's marsupial mammals (kangaroos, koalas) evolved from a common ancestor isolated when Australia separated from other continents. Galapagos finches show adaptive radiation from a common ancestor.
- **6. Molecular biology:** DNA and protein similarities reflect evolutionary distance. Humans and chimpanzees share ~98.8% of DNA. Universal genetic code in all life suggests common ancestor.
- **7. Direct observation:** evolution can be observed in real time:
  - Antibiotic resistance in bacteria.
  - Pesticide resistance in insects.
  - Industrial melanism in peppered moths.
  - Beak shape changes in Galapagos finches in response to drought.
- **Conclusion:** these multiple lines of evidence, from independent disciplines, all converge on the same conclusion — evolution occurred and continues to occur.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Saying individuals evolve.** Individuals don't evolve; POPULATIONS do. Evolution is change in allele frequencies over generations.
2. **Equating fitness with strength.** Fitness = reproductive success. The "fittest" produces the most offspring that survive to reproduce.
3. **Treating natural selection as goal-directed.** Selection has no foresight or purpose. It "selects" only in the sense that successful variants leave more descendants.
4. **Confusing types of selection.** Directional (one extreme), stabilizing (intermediate), disruptive (both extremes).
5. **Misnaming Hardy-Weinberg conditions.** Five required conditions: no mutation, no migration, large population, random mating, no selection.
6. **Wrong Hardy-Weinberg formula.** p + q = 1 (alleles). p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (genotypes).
7. **Confusing homologous and analogous structures.** Homologous: same origin, different function. Analogous: different origin, similar function (convergent evolution).
8. **Treating common ancestry as equivalent to direct ancestry.** Two species shared a common ancestor; they're not directly descended from each other (humans didn't evolve from chimps; both share a common ancestor).
9. **Misnaming reproductive isolation mechanisms.** Prezygotic (before fertilization): habitat, temporal, behavioral, mechanical, gametic. Postzygotic (after): hybrid inviability, hybrid sterility, hybrid breakdown.
10. **Confusing genetic drift with natural selection.** Drift is RANDOM change in allele frequencies (especially in small populations). Selection is NON-RANDOM change based on fitness differences.
11. **Misapplying bottleneck effect.** Bottleneck reduces genetic diversity through population reduction, not through migration.
12. **Treating speciation as fast.** Speciation usually takes thousands to millions of years.
13. **Ignoring molecular evidence.** Modern phylogeny relies heavily on DNA and protein sequence comparisons.
14. **Treating extinction as failure.** Most species that ever lived are extinct (~99%). Extinction is a normal part of evolution. Mass extinctions can accelerate evolution by clearing niches.
15. **Treating Hardy-Weinberg as describing reality.** It's a NULL HYPOTHESIS — populations rarely meet all 5 conditions. Real populations evolve.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For Hardy-Weinberg problems:**
- Identify q from recessive phenotype frequency (q²).
- Calculate p (1 − q).
- Use p² + 2pq + q² = 1 for genotype frequencies.
- Verify total equals 1.

**For natural selection identification:**
- Determine which phenotypes are favored.
- Identify directional, stabilizing, or disruptive.
- Predict population shift.

**For speciation questions:**
- Identify mode (allopatric vs sympatric).
- Identify reproductive isolation (prezygotic or postzygotic mechanisms).
- Trace process from initial isolation to reproductive incompatibility.

**For phylogenetic tree analysis:**
- Identify common ancestors at nodes.
- Determine relative timing of divergence.
- Identify which species share more recent common ancestors.

**For evidence of evolution:**
- Cite multiple independent lines (fossils, anatomy, embryology, molecular).
- Connect evidence to evolutionary mechanism.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Evolution is change in allele frequencies in a population over time. Individuals do not evolve; populations evolve as the gene pool changes through mutation, migration, drift, non-random mating, and natural selection."
2. "Natural selection acts on phenotypic variation: individuals with favorable phenotypes survive and reproduce more, increasing their alleles' frequency in the next generation. Selection has no goal or foresight; it produces adaptive change as a byproduct of differential reproductive success."
3. "Using Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, allele frequencies p and q are calculated from genotype frequencies p² + 2pq + q² = 1. The five conditions for H-W equilibrium are: no mutation, no migration, large population, random mating, and no natural selection."
4. "Multiple independent lines of evidence support evolution: fossils show transitional forms over time; comparative anatomy reveals homologous structures (same evolutionary origin, different function); vestigial structures reflect evolutionary history; molecular similarities (DNA, proteins) confirm common ancestry."
5. "Allopatric speciation occurs when geographic separation prevents gene flow between populations. Each population evolves independently, accumulating different mutations and experiencing different selective pressures, until they become reproductively isolated and cannot interbreed even if they encounter each other again."
6. "Genetic drift is RANDOM change in allele frequencies, particularly in small populations. The bottleneck effect (drastic population reduction) and founder effect (small group establishing new population) are forms of genetic drift that can dramatically reduce genetic diversity."
7. "Phylogenetic trees represent evolutionary relationships: branches show lineages, nodes show common ancestors. Species sharing more recent common ancestors are more closely related. Modern phylogenetic analyses use molecular data (DNA, proteins) to refine and sometimes revise relationships originally determined from morphology."
