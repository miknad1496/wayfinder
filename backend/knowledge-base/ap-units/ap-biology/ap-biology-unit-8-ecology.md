- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Unit 8 covers the biology of populations, communities, and ecosystems. Population growth models (exponential and logistic) are perennial. Energy flow through trophic levels (10% rule) appears regularly. Species interactions (predation, competition, symbiosis) are common. Human impacts on ecosystems (climate change, biodiversity loss) are increasingly tested. The unit connects evolution (Unit 7) to real-world biological systems.

## Big Ideas

1. **Energy flows ONE WAY through ecosystems; matter cycles.** Energy enters as sunlight, flows through trophic levels, and exits as heat. Matter (carbon, nitrogen, water) cycles repeatedly through living and non-living components.
2. **Population growth follows two basic models.** EXPONENTIAL growth (J-curve) when resources unlimited. LOGISTIC growth (S-curve) when carrying capacity reached. Real populations approximate these patterns under different conditions.
3. **Species interactions shape communities.** Competition (negative for both), predation (negative for prey, positive for predator), and three types of symbiosis (mutualism, commensalism, parasitism) all influence species distribution and abundance.
4. **Biodiversity is essential for ecosystem stability and services.** Higher diversity → more resilience to disturbances and more ecosystem services (pollination, water filtration, climate regulation).
5. **Human activity is causing rapid ecosystem change.** Climate change, habitat loss, invasive species, pollution, and overharvesting are driving the current "sixth mass extinction." Understanding ecological principles is essential for managing these impacts.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Levels of ecological organization:**
  - **Individual** — one organism.
  - **Population** — group of individuals of the same species in the same area.
  - **Community** — all populations in the same area (interactions among species).
  - **Ecosystem** — community + abiotic factors (sunlight, water, nutrients).
  - **Biome** — major ecological region (rainforest, tundra, savanna).
  - **Biosphere** — all ecosystems on Earth.
- **Energy flow through ecosystems:**
  - **Producers (autotrophs):** make their own food. Plants (photosynthesis), some bacteria. Form the base of the food chain.
  - **Consumers (heterotrophs):** eat other organisms.
    - **Primary consumers:** herbivores (eat producers).
    - **Secondary consumers:** carnivores (eat herbivores).
    - **Tertiary consumers:** carnivores (eat secondary consumers).
    - **Decomposers:** bacteria, fungi (break down dead material).
- **10% rule (energy transfer between trophic levels):**
  - Approximately 10% of energy is passed from one trophic level to the next.
  - 90% lost as heat (cellular respiration), uneaten parts, undigested material.
  - **Implication:** food chains rarely exceed 4-5 trophic levels because energy runs out.
- **Food chain:** linear sequence (producer → primary consumer → secondary → etc.).
- **Food web:** complex network of feeding relationships.
- **Population ecology — growth models:**
  - **Exponential growth:** dN/dt = rN, where r = per capita growth rate. Produces J-shaped curve. Occurs when resources unlimited.
  - **Logistic growth:** dN/dt = rN((K-N)/K), where K = carrying capacity. Produces S-shaped curve. As N approaches K, growth slows; reaches stable equilibrium at N = K.
- **Carrying capacity (K):** the maximum population size an environment can sustain.
- **Density-dependent factors:** factors whose effect depends on population density. Examples: competition, predation, disease (more density → more transmission).
- **Density-independent factors:** affect populations regardless of density. Examples: weather, natural disasters, climate events.
- **r-selected vs K-selected species:**
  - **r-selected:** rapid reproduction, many offspring, low parental care, short lifespan, populations fluctuate widely (mosquitoes, weeds).
  - **K-selected:** slow reproduction, few offspring, high parental care, long lifespan, populations stable near carrying capacity (elephants, humans).
- **Species interactions:**
  - **Competition (- / -):** both species harmed. Can be intraspecific (within species) or interspecific (between species).
  - **Predation (+ / -):** predator benefits, prey harmed. Predator-prey populations often oscillate.
  - **Symbiosis** (close, long-term interactions):
    - **Mutualism (+ / +):** both benefit. Examples: bees and flowers (pollination), gut bacteria.
    - **Commensalism (+ / 0):** one benefits, other unaffected. Examples: barnacles on whales.
    - **Parasitism (+ / -):** parasite benefits, host harmed. Examples: tapeworms, fleas.
- **Niche:** the role of a species in its ecosystem.
  - **Fundamental niche:** the full range of conditions the species could occupy.
  - **Realized niche:** the actual range it occupies, often limited by competition or other factors.
- **Competitive exclusion principle (Gause):** two species competing for the same resources cannot coexist indefinitely; one will outcompete the other.
- **Resource partitioning:** species evolve to use different resources or niches, allowing coexistence. Example: warblers feeding in different parts of the same tree.
- **Biodiversity:**
  - **Genetic diversity:** variation within a species.
  - **Species diversity:** number and variety of species in an area.
  - **Ecosystem diversity:** variety of ecosystems in a region.
- **Ecosystem services:** benefits humans receive from ecosystems:
  - **Provisioning:** food, water, timber, fiber.
  - **Regulating:** climate regulation, water purification, pollination, disease control.
  - **Cultural:** recreation, spiritual values, aesthetic.
  - **Supporting:** soil formation, nutrient cycling, primary production.
- **Disruptions to ecosystems:**
  - **Climate change:** rising temperatures, ocean acidification, sea level rise.
  - **Habitat loss:** deforestation, urbanization.
  - **Invasive species:** non-native species outcompeting natives.
  - **Pollution:** air, water, soil contamination.
  - **Overharvesting:** fisheries collapse, deforestation.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why energy flow is one-way but matter cycles:**
  - Energy: enters as sunlight (mostly), used by producers, transferred up trophic levels, lost as heat at each step (entropy). Eventually all converted to heat and radiated away. ENERGY DOES NOT RECYCLE.
  - Matter: cycles between living and non-living. Carbon in CO₂ → plant tissue → animal tissue → decomposers → back to soil/atmosphere. Same atoms recycled repeatedly.
- **Trophic level dynamics:**
  - **Pyramid of energy:** energy decreases dramatically up the pyramid (10% rule).
  - **Pyramid of biomass:** total mass at each level usually decreases up the pyramid.
  - **Pyramid of numbers:** number of individuals at each level may increase OR decrease (huge prey individuals support fewer predators; small prey individuals support more).
- **Why food chains rarely exceed 4-5 levels.** With 10% energy transfer:
  - Producers: 100,000 calories.
  - Primary consumer: 10,000.
  - Secondary: 1,000.
  - Tertiary: 100.
  - Quaternary: 10.
  - Insufficient energy to support a 5th-level consumer.
- **Predator-prey oscillations:**
  - Classic example: lynx and snowshoe hare (Hudson Bay records show ~10-year cycles).
  - Mechanism: prey abundant → predators reproduce more → predators decrease prey → prey decrease → predators starve → predators decrease → prey rebound.
  - **Lotka-Volterra equations** model this mathematically.
- **Logistic growth model worked through:**
  - dN/dt = rN((K-N)/K).
  - Initial growth: N is small, (K-N)/K is close to 1, so dN/dt ≈ rN (exponential).
  - As N approaches K: (K-N)/K approaches 0, so dN/dt approaches 0 (growth slows).
  - At N = K: dN/dt = 0 (growth stops; population stable).
  - **Real populations** can exceed K temporarily then crash, or can have density-dependent factors that prevent reaching K.
- **Species richness and species diversity:**
  - **Species richness:** number of species.
  - **Species diversity:** combines richness and EVENNESS (how evenly individuals are distributed among species). High evenness = no species dominates; low evenness = one or two species dominate.
- **Keystone species:** species with disproportionately large effects on community structure relative to their abundance. Removing them dramatically changes the ecosystem. Examples:
  - **Sea otters** in kelp forests (eat sea urchins; without otters, urchins overgraze kelp).
  - **Beavers** (create wetlands; affect entire watershed).
  - **Wolves** in Yellowstone (control deer populations; affect plant growth, river dynamics).
- **Ecological succession:**
  - **Primary succession:** starts with bare rock or new substrate (volcanic eruption, retreating glacier). Pioneer species (lichens) → mosses → grasses → shrubs → trees.
  - **Secondary succession:** starts after disturbance to existing community (fire, abandoned farm). Faster than primary because soil already exists.
  - **Climax community:** end stage of succession (relatively stable community).
- **Biogeochemical cycles:**
  - **Water cycle:** evaporation → precipitation → runoff → groundwater.
  - **Carbon cycle:** atmosphere ↔ photosynthesis ↔ respiration ↔ ocean ↔ rocks (slow).
  - **Nitrogen cycle:** nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert N₂ to ammonia → nitrification → assimilation → decomposition → denitrification.
  - **Phosphorus cycle:** rocks → soil → plants → animals → back to rocks. NO atmospheric component.
- **Climate change ecological impacts:**
  - Range shifts (species moving toward poles or higher elevations).
  - Phenology shifts (timing of flowering, migration changes).
  - Coral bleaching (rising temperatures + ocean acidification).
  - Permafrost melting (releases trapped methane, accelerating warming).
  - Extreme weather events.
- **Invasive species impacts:**
  - Often have no natural predators in new environment.
  - Can outcompete natives for resources.
  - Examples: zebra mussels (Great Lakes), kudzu (US South), cane toads (Australia), Burmese pythons (Florida Everglades).

### Adds for [5]

- **Why energy "flows" through ecosystems but matter "cycles."** This distinction is fundamental. Energy enters as concentrated solar energy and exits as dispersed heat (second law of thermodynamics — entropy increases). Matter is conserved (first law of thermodynamics — atoms not created or destroyed). Result: ecosystems require continuous energy input but can recycle matter indefinitely.
- **Why ecosystem services have monetary value worth measuring.** Costanza et al. (1997) estimated global ecosystem services at $33 trillion/year (vs $18 trillion global GDP at the time). This included pollination (~$200 billion/year for crop pollination), water filtration, climate regulation, etc. Valuation argues for protecting natural systems on economic grounds.
- **The "intermediate disturbance hypothesis":** Diversity is highest at INTERMEDIATE levels of disturbance:
  - Low disturbance: dominant species outcompete others; diversity low.
  - Intermediate disturbance: variety of species can coexist; diversity high.
  - High disturbance: only stress-tolerant species survive; diversity low.
  - Insight: completely "protected" ecosystems can lose diversity through dominance; some disturbance is healthy.
- **The "tragedy of the commons" (Garrett Hardin, 1968).** Common-pool resources tend to be overexploited because individual incentive is to take more (private benefit) while costs are distributed. Examples: overfishing (fish are "commons"), deforestation, atmospheric carbon pollution. Solutions: regulation, privatization, community management. Climate change is the largest tragedy of the commons in human history.
- **Why mass extinctions matter.** The current rate of species extinction is 100-1,000 times background rates (the natural baseline). Some scientists call this the "Sixth Mass Extinction." Causes: habitat loss, climate change, invasive species, pollution, overharvesting. Implications: loss of ecosystem services, loss of evolutionary heritage, accelerating ecological cascade effects.
- **Carbon cycle and climate change:**
  - Burning fossil fuels releases carbon stored over millions of years.
  - Atmospheric CO₂ has risen from ~280 ppm (pre-industrial) to over 420 ppm (2024).
  - This is the highest CO₂ in at least 800,000 years (and probably much longer).
  - Effects: warming, ocean acidification, climate disruption, sea level rise.
  - Mitigation requires both reducing emissions AND increasing carbon sequestration (forests, oceans, technological solutions).
- **The "edge effect" in fragmented habitats.** When habitat is fragmented (broken into smaller pieces by roads, agriculture), the EDGES of patches have different conditions than the interior. Edge effects:
  - More predation on edge species (e.g., birds nesting near edges face more predators).
  - Different microclimate (more wind, light, temperature variation).
  - More invasive species.
  - **Implication:** large continuous habitats are much more valuable for biodiversity than equivalent total area in small fragments.
- **Trophic cascades:** when changes at one trophic level cascade through multiple levels. Yellowstone wolves' return (1995) reduced elk → reduced browsing → restored riparian vegetation → returning beaver populations → improved water systems. Demonstrates the connectedness of ecological systems.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Energy flow calculation

A producer captures 100,000 J of energy.

(a) Predict energy available at primary, secondary, and tertiary consumers.
(b) Explain why food chains have limited length.

- **(a)** Using 10% rule:
  - Primary consumer: 100,000 × 0.10 = 10,000 J.
  - Secondary consumer: 10,000 × 0.10 = 1,000 J.
  - Tertiary consumer: 1,000 × 0.10 = 100 J.
  - Quaternary consumer: 100 × 0.10 = 10 J.
- **(b)** Energy is rapidly lost as heat (cellular respiration), uneaten parts, undigested material. After 4-5 trophic levels, insufficient energy remains to support additional levels. This is why apex predators (high trophic levels) are typically less common than lower trophic levels.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Logistic vs exponential growth

A bacterial culture is started with 100 cells. The growth rate r = 0.5 per hour. The carrying capacity K = 10,000 cells.

(a) Sketch exponential growth (no K limitation).
(b) Sketch logistic growth (with K).
(c) When would each model apply?

- **(a) Exponential:** N = N₀e^(rt). At hour 1: 100·e^0.5 ≈ 165. At hour 5: 100·e^2.5 ≈ 1,218. At hour 10: 100·e^5 ≈ 14,841.
- **(b) Logistic:** initial growth approximately exponential, slows as approaches K, stabilizes at K = 10,000.
- **(c) Exponential applies when resources are unlimited:** early colonization, lab conditions, after disturbance.
- **Logistic applies when carrying capacity is real:** stable populations, limited resources, density-dependent factors.

### Example 3 [3] — Species interaction identification

Identify the species interaction:
(a) Bees pollinate flowers; bees get nectar; flowers reproduce.
(b) Tapeworm lives in human intestine; tapeworm gets food; human suffers.
(c) Two species of warblers feed on different parts of the same tree.
(d) Lions hunt zebras.
(e) Barnacles attach to whale shells; barnacles get free transport; whales unaffected.

- **(a) Mutualism (+/+).** Both benefit.
- **(b) Parasitism (+/-).** Tapeworm benefits; human harmed.
- **(c) Resource partitioning** (avoiding competition). Both species coexist by using different resources (different parts of the tree). Reduces direct competition.
- **(d) Predation (+/-).** Lions benefit; zebras harmed.
- **(e) Commensalism (+/0).** Barnacles benefit; whales (mostly) unaffected.

### Example 4 [4] — Keystone species impact

In a coral reef ecosystem, a population of sea urchins is removed.

(a) Predict the effect on kelp populations.
(b) What does this reveal about sea urchins' role?

- **(a)** Without sea urchins, kelp populations would INCREASE dramatically (sea urchins are major kelp grazers). This illustrates the keystone species concept.
- **(b)** Sea urchins are a KEYSTONE SPECIES — relatively few in number but with huge effect on community structure. Removing them dramatically reshapes the ecosystem.
- **Note:** in kelp forests, SEA OTTERS are the more famous keystone species (they eat sea urchins, which controls urchin populations and protects kelp). When sea otters were nearly hunted to extinction, urchin populations exploded and overgrazed kelp. Sea otter recovery has restored kelp forests.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Climate change ecological cascade

Average ocean temperatures have risen 0.5°C in a region. Predict ecological consequences.

- **Direct effects:**
  - **Coral bleaching:** corals expel symbiotic algae when stressed by heat. Bleached corals die if temperature stays elevated.
  - **Range shifts:** species move toward poles or deeper water.
  - **Phenology disruption:** breeding cycles, migration timing changes.
- **Indirect effects (cascades):**
  - Loss of corals removes habitat for fish.
  - Fish loss affects fishing communities and human food supply.
  - Range shifts disrupt established food webs.
  - Decomposing dead corals release CO₂, accelerating warming.
- **Long-term:**
  - Loss of biodiversity.
  - Loss of ecosystem services (fisheries, coastal protection from coral reefs).
  - Tipping points (corals, polar ice, Amazon rainforest) where small changes cause irreversible system shifts.
- **Implication:** small temperature changes have large ecosystem effects through interconnected systems. Climate change is one of the most significant disruptions to global ecosystems.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Saying matter cycles AND energy cycles.** Energy flows ONE WAY (in as light, out as heat). Matter cycles.
2. **Misnaming trophic levels.** Producers (1st level), primary consumers (2nd), secondary (3rd), tertiary (4th).
3. **Forgetting decomposers.** Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) recycle matter; not part of the energy chain in the usual sense, but essential to ecosystem function.
4. **Wrong energy transfer percentage.** 10% rule = ~10% of energy passes between trophic levels (90% lost).
5. **Treating exponential growth as the norm.** Most populations are limited by carrying capacity; logistic growth is more typical of established populations.
6. **Confusing exponential and logistic.** Exponential = J-curve (no limit). Logistic = S-curve (reaches carrying capacity).
7. **Misidentifying species interactions.** Mutualism (+/+), Commensalism (+/0), Parasitism (+/-), Competition (-/-), Predation (+/-).
8. **Confusing fundamental and realized niche.** Fundamental = full range possible. Realized = actual range due to competition/limitations.
9. **Treating biodiversity as just species count.** Biodiversity includes genetic diversity (within species), species diversity (richness + evenness), and ecosystem diversity.
10. **Misnaming biogeochemical cycles.** Carbon cycle (atmosphere, photosynthesis, respiration, fossil fuels). Nitrogen cycle (nitrogen-fixing bacteria, nitrification, denitrification). Water cycle. Phosphorus cycle (NO atmospheric component).
11. **Forgetting that humans cause many disruptions.** Climate change, habitat loss, invasive species, pollution, overharvesting are all anthropogenic.
12. **Saying invasive species are always bad.** They cause ecological damage, but the "bad" judgment is from human perspective. Ecologically they often outcompete natives because of release from natural predators.
13. **Treating succession as goal-directed.** Succession isn't goal-directed; it reflects who can colonize and persist under given conditions.
14. **Forgetting density-dependent vs density-independent factors.** Density-dependent (competition, disease) increase with population. Density-independent (weather, disasters) act regardless of density.
15. **Treating ecosystem services as free.** They have economic value. Valuing them is essential for environmental policy.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For energy flow questions:**
- Apply 10% rule.
- Identify trophic level.
- Distinguish energy flow (one-way) from matter cycling.

**For population growth questions:**
- Identify exponential vs logistic.
- Apply formulas correctly.
- Identify carrying capacity (K).
- Distinguish density-dependent from density-independent factors.

**For species interactions:**
- Use the +/-/0 notation correctly.
- Identify the type (mutualism, commensalism, parasitism, predation, competition).
- Predict effects on each species.

**For biodiversity questions:**
- Distinguish levels (genetic, species, ecosystem).
- Note both richness and evenness.
- Connect to ecosystem services.

**For human impact questions:**
- Identify the type (climate, habitat, invasive, pollution, overharvesting).
- Trace cascading effects.
- Note ecosystem service implications.
