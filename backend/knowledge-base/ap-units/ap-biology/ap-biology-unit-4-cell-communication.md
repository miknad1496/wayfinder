- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Unit 4 covers two interconnected topics — how cells communicate with each other (signal transduction) and how cells divide (cell cycle). Both are perennial FRQ topics. Signal transduction pathways (with their reception, transduction, response stages) and the cell cycle's checkpoints are commonly tested. The connections to disease (cancer = dysregulated cell cycle; many disorders involve disrupted signaling) are also tested.

## Big Ideas

1. **Cells communicate through signal transduction pathways — a process with three stages: reception, transduction, response.** A signal molecule binds to a receptor (reception); a cascade of events transmits and amplifies the signal (transduction); the cell responds (response).
2. **Receptors come in two main types based on signal molecule character.** Membrane receptors (for water-soluble signals that can't cross the membrane) and intracellular receptors (for lipid-soluble signals that diffuse through membrane).
3. **Feedback loops regulate biological systems.** Negative feedback maintains stable conditions (homeostasis). Positive feedback amplifies a process to a defined endpoint.
4. **The cell cycle is highly regulated through checkpoints.** G1, G2, and M-phase checkpoints monitor cell readiness to proceed. CDK-cyclin complexes control progression. Cancer = checkpoint failure → uncontrolled division.
5. **Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells.** PMAT (Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase) followed by cytokinesis. Critical for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Cell communication:** the process by which cells receive, interpret, and respond to signals from other cells or the environment.
- **Three stages of signal transduction:**
  - **(1) Reception:** signal molecule (LIGAND) binds to a RECEPTOR.
  - **(2) Transduction:** signal is converted to a form that can produce a cellular response. Often involves a cascade of molecular events (often phosphorylation cascades).
  - **(3) Response:** the cell responds — change in gene expression, enzyme activity, cell movement, etc.
- **Two types of receptors:**
  - **Membrane (cell-surface) receptors:** for hydrophilic (water-soluble) signal molecules that can't cross the lipid bilayer. Examples:
    - **G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs):** activate G proteins inside cell.
    - **Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs):** receptor itself is enzyme; phosphorylates tyrosines.
    - **Ligand-gated ion channels:** open/close in response to ligand binding.
  - **Intracellular receptors:** for hydrophobic (lipid-soluble) signal molecules (steroids, thyroid hormones) that diffuse through the membrane. Receptors located in cytoplasm or nucleus. Often act as transcription factors.
- **Signal types by distance:**
  - **Endocrine signaling:** signal molecules travel long distances through bloodstream (hormones).
  - **Paracrine signaling:** signal acts on nearby cells (e.g., growth factors).
  - **Synaptic signaling:** signal acts across a small gap between cells (neurotransmitters at synapses).
  - **Direct contact:** signal molecules on cell surface bind to receptors on adjacent cells (immune system, juxtacrine signaling).
- **Phosphorylation cascade:** a common transduction mechanism. Protein kinase activates another protein by adding phosphate group; that activated protein activates another; etc. Amplifies the signal at each step.
- **Second messengers:** small molecules that spread the signal throughout the cell (e.g., cyclic AMP, calcium ions). Made/released after first signal binds receptor.
- **Feedback regulation:**
  - **Negative feedback:** the response REDUCES the original stimulus. Maintains homeostasis. Example: insulin secretion in response to high blood glucose lowers blood glucose, reducing the stimulus for insulin.
  - **Positive feedback:** the response INTENSIFIES the original stimulus. Drives processes to completion. Example: childbirth — uterine contractions stimulate oxytocin release, which causes more contractions, until baby is born.
- **Cell cycle:** the sequence of events from one cell division to the next.
- **Cell cycle stages:**
  - **Interphase (~90% of cycle):**
    - **G1 (Gap 1):** cell grows, performs normal functions, prepares for DNA synthesis.
    - **S (Synthesis):** DNA is replicated. Each chromosome now has two sister chromatids.
    - **G2 (Gap 2):** cell continues growth, prepares for mitosis.
  - **M phase (Mitosis + Cytokinesis):**
    - **Mitosis:** division of nucleus.
    - **Cytokinesis:** division of cytoplasm.
- **Mitosis stages (PMAT):**
  - **Prophase:** chromosomes condense; spindle fibers form; nuclear envelope breaks down.
  - **Metaphase:** chromosomes align at the equator (metaphase plate).
  - **Anaphase:** sister chromatids separate; spindle fibers pull them to opposite poles.
  - **Telophase:** nuclear envelope reforms around each set of chromosomes; chromosomes decondense.
- **Cytokinesis:** physical division of the cytoplasm.
  - **Animal cells:** cleavage furrow (actin ring contracts, pinching cell in two).
  - **Plant cells:** cell plate forms (vesicles fuse to form new cell wall between daughter cells).
- **Cell cycle checkpoints:**
  - **G1 checkpoint:** "restriction point." Most important. Determines whether cell will proceed with division.
  - **G2 checkpoint:** verifies DNA has been correctly replicated; checks for damage.
  - **M checkpoint (spindle assembly checkpoint):** ensures chromosomes are properly attached to spindle before anaphase.
- **Cyclins and CDKs (cyclin-dependent kinases):**
  - Cyclins are regulatory proteins that bind to CDKs.
  - CDKs are enzymes that phosphorylate other proteins to drive the cell cycle.
  - Cyclin-CDK complexes drive cell cycle progression at each checkpoint.
  - Cyclin levels rise and fall through the cycle.
- **Cancer:**
  - Caused by mutations that disrupt cell cycle regulation.
  - Loss of cell cycle control → uncontrolled division.
  - **Two main mutation types:**
    - **Oncogenes:** mutated proto-oncogenes (normally promote division). Mutation makes them too active. Examples: Ras, Myc.
    - **Tumor suppressor genes:** normally inhibit cell division. Mutation makes them inactive. Examples: p53, RB.
  - p53 is the "guardian of the genome." Damaged in over 50% of human cancers.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why receptor-ligand binding is specific.** The receptor's binding site has a specific shape complementary to the ligand. Like enzyme-substrate binding (and antibody-antigen binding), receptor-ligand interactions depend on shape and chemistry — hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals forces.
- **Why signal cascades amplify the signal.** A single ligand molecule binding can activate many downstream proteins. Each activated kinase can phosphorylate multiple targets. After several steps of a cascade, a single initial signal can produce thousands or millions of downstream effects.
- **G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) detailed mechanism:**
  - Ligand binds to receptor.
  - Receptor conformation changes; activates associated G protein.
  - G protein dissociates into α and βγ subunits; α subunit activates downstream enzyme.
  - Enzyme creates second messenger (e.g., cAMP).
  - Second messenger activates protein kinases.
  - GPCRs are the most common receptor type — ~30% of all approved drugs target GPCRs.
- **Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs):**
  - Ligand binds; receptors dimerize.
  - Receptors phosphorylate each other on tyrosine residues.
  - Phosphorylated tyrosines act as docking sites for downstream proteins.
  - Initiates multiple parallel cascades.
  - Important in growth factors, insulin signaling.
- **The cell cycle's "all or nothing" decision.** Once past the G1 checkpoint (restriction point), cells are committed to dividing. The decision depends on multiple signals: growth factors present, sufficient nutrients, undamaged DNA, sufficient cell size.
- **Why DNA damage triggers cell cycle arrest.** When DNA is damaged, p53 is activated. p53 turns on genes that:
  - Halt the cell cycle (arrest at G1 or G2).
  - Activate DNA repair enzymes.
  - If damage is too severe, trigger apoptosis (programmed cell death).
- **Why p53 is "guardian of the genome."** p53 mutations are the most common single mutation in human cancers. When p53 fails:
  - Damaged cells continue dividing.
  - Mutations accumulate.
  - More mutations → more aggressive cancer.
  - p53 mutation is often the FIRST step in cancer development.
- **Apoptosis (programmed cell death):**
  - Controlled, orderly cell death.
  - Different from necrosis (uncontrolled cell death from injury).
  - Triggered by damaged or unneeded cells.
  - Important in development (sculpting structures), immune system (eliminating infected cells), cancer prevention.
  - Caspases (proteases) execute the death program.
- **Negative feedback examples in human physiology:**
  - **Blood glucose regulation:** high glucose → insulin → cells take up glucose → glucose drops.
  - **Body temperature:** high temp → sweating + vasodilation → temperature drops.
  - **Thyroid hormone:** high T3/T4 → inhibits TSH → less thyroid stimulation.
- **Positive feedback examples:**
  - **Childbirth:** uterine contractions → oxytocin → more contractions.
  - **Blood clotting:** platelets activate other platelets → cascade until clot complete.
  - **Action potential:** depolarization opens Na+ channels → more depolarization.
  - Note: positive feedback is RARE in physiology because it can spiral out of control. Almost always has a defined endpoint.
- **Disease examples of disrupted signaling:**
  - **Cholera:** cholera toxin permanently activates G protein → uncontrolled fluid secretion in intestine → severe diarrhea.
  - **Diabetes type 2:** insulin signaling pathway becomes resistant.
  - **Cancer:** mutations in receptors (HER2 in breast cancer), G proteins (Ras), or downstream kinases lead to uncontrolled division.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why signaling cascades use kinases specifically.** Phosphorylation is reversible (phosphatases remove the phosphate group), so signaling can be turned ON and OFF. Phosphorylation also dramatically changes a protein's shape and activity. The combination of fast, reversible, and dramatic effect makes kinase cascades ideal for cell communication.
- **The "molecular memory" of cell signaling.** Cells can remember past signals through:
  - Sustained changes in gene expression.
  - Modifications to chromatin (epigenetic marks).
  - Modifications to existing proteins.
  - Changes in receptor expression on the cell surface.
  - This memory enables learning, immunity, and developmental specialization.
- **Why cell cycle regulation is so complex.** The cell cycle must be:
  - Driven forward by positive signals (growth factors, nutrients).
  - Halted by negative signals (DNA damage, missing nutrients).
  - Coordinated across all cells in a tissue.
  - Tightly regulated to prevent inappropriate division (cancer) or insufficient division (failure to grow/heal).
  - The result is a complex regulatory network with multiple checkpoints, many proteins involved, and extensive feedback.
- **Why cancer is so hard to treat.** Cancer cells:
  - Have multiple mutations affecting different pathways.
  - Often have defective p53 (no longer trigger apoptosis when damaged by chemotherapy).
  - Can develop resistance to single-target drugs.
  - Hide from immune surveillance.
  - Can metastasize to other tissues.
  - Modern targeted therapies (e.g., HER2 inhibitors for breast cancer, EGFR inhibitors for some lung cancers) work for specific mutations, but cancer often evolves resistance.
- **The connection between cell cycle and stem cells.** Stem cells can divide indefinitely while maintaining the ability to differentiate into specialized cell types. Their cell cycles are tightly regulated. Cancer cells share some features with stem cells (immortal, can self-renew), and cancer stem cells may be the source of treatment-resistant cancers.
- **Why apoptosis is essential to development.** During fetal development:
  - Apoptosis sculpts hands and feet (separating fingers and toes from initial webbing).
  - Eliminates excess neurons that don't make functional connections.
  - Removes structures that aren't needed (tail in human embryos, body symmetry adjustments).
  - Without controlled cell death, development would produce malformed organisms.
- **The "Hayflick limit"** — most normal human cells can divide only ~50 times before they stop. This is partly due to telomere shortening (DNA at chromosome ends gets shorter with each division). Cancer cells often reactivate telomerase (an enzyme that lengthens telomeres), enabling unlimited division. Stem cells also use telomerase.
- **Why feedback loops are universal in biology.** From single cells to ecosystems, feedback regulation maintains stability and enables adaptation:
  - **Cellular:** negative feedback in metabolism (ATP inhibits glycolysis enzyme).
  - **Organismal:** homeostasis (body temperature, blood pressure, blood glucose).
  - **Population:** density-dependent regulation (Unit 8).
  - **Ecosystem:** climate feedback loops.
  - **Evolution:** natural selection is itself a feedback process.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Identifying signaling components

A signaling pathway:
1. Insulin (a peptide hormone) released from pancreas.
2. Insulin binds to receptor on muscle cell surface.
3. Receptor phosphorylates internal proteins.
4. Cascade of phosphorylations.
5. GLUT4 transporters move to cell membrane.
6. Glucose enters cell.

(a) Identify the ligand, receptor type, and stages of signal transduction.

- **(a)** **Ligand:** insulin (hydrophilic peptide hormone — can't cross membrane).
- **Receptor type:** receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK), located on cell surface. Receptor's intracellular domain phosphorylates tyrosines.
- **Stages:**
  - **Reception:** Step 2 (insulin binds receptor).
  - **Transduction:** Steps 3-4 (receptor phosphorylation, downstream cascade).
  - **Response:** Step 5-6 (GLUT4 movement, glucose uptake).
- **Why insulin can't enter the cell:** insulin is hydrophilic (peptide hormone), so it can't cross the lipid bilayer. Hence it must use a membrane receptor.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Negative feedback example

In humans, body temperature regulation involves the following:
- Body temp rises above 37°C.
- Hypothalamus detects.
- Triggers sweating and vasodilation.
- Body temp drops back to ~37°C.

(a) Identify this as positive or negative feedback.
(b) Explain mechanism.

- **(a)** NEGATIVE feedback. The response (sweating, vasodilation) REDUCES the original stimulus (high body temp).
- **(b) Mechanism:**
  - Stimulus (high temp) → sensor (hypothalamus) → effector (sweat glands, blood vessels).
  - Effector reduces the stimulus.
  - System returns to homeostatic set point.
  - Negative feedback maintains stable conditions.
- **Generalization:** virtually all physiological homeostasis (blood glucose, blood pressure, blood pH, hormone levels) uses negative feedback. Positive feedback is rare and reserved for processes that should drive to a defined endpoint (childbirth, blood clotting).

### Example 3 [3] — Mitosis stages

A microscopist observes the following in a cell:
(a) Sister chromatids being pulled to opposite poles.
(b) Chromosomes lined up at the equator.
(c) Chromosomes condensed; nuclear envelope breaking down.
(d) Two new nuclei forming; chromosomes decondensing.

Identify each stage of mitosis.

- **(a) Anaphase** (sister chromatid separation).
- **(b) Metaphase** (chromosomes at metaphase plate / equator).
- **(c) Prophase** (chromatin condensation, nuclear envelope breakdown).
- **(d) Telophase** (nuclear envelope reformation, chromosome decondensation).
- **Memory:** PMAT (Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase).

### Example 4 [4] — Cell cycle checkpoint failure

A cell has a mutation in p53 that makes it nonfunctional. Predict the consequences.

- **Normal p53 function:** detects DNA damage → halts cell cycle at G1 → activates repair OR triggers apoptosis if damage severe.
- **With p53 nonfunctional:**
  - Damaged cells DON'T arrest at G1.
  - Damaged cells continue to divide.
  - Mutations accumulate (each division can introduce more errors).
  - More mutations → more dysregulated growth.
  - Apoptosis pathway is impaired → damaged cells survive when they should die.
- **Result:** uncontrolled cell division → tumor formation. p53 mutations are found in over 50% of human cancers.
- **Why p53 is "guardian of the genome":** by halting damaged cells, it prevents mutations from being passed to daughter cells.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Comparing positive and negative feedback

A researcher discovers a new signaling pathway that increases its own activity over time. The signal protein A activates production of more protein A.

(a) Identify as positive or negative feedback.
(b) When might this be biologically appropriate?
(c) When might this be dangerous?

- **(a) Positive feedback** (response amplifies original stimulus).
- **(b) Biologically appropriate:**
  - Childbirth (uterine contractions → oxytocin → more contractions until birth).
  - Blood clotting (platelet activation → more platelet activation until clot complete).
  - Action potentials (depolarization → Na+ channel opening → more depolarization until peak).
  - In each case, there's a defined endpoint (birth, clot, threshold) that terminates the cycle.
- **(c) Dangerous if no endpoint:**
  - Without a way to stop, positive feedback can spiral out of control.
  - In disease: autoimmune flare-ups can self-perpetuate.
  - In cancer: dysregulated growth signals reinforcing more growth.
- **Why positive feedback is RARE in biology:** most physiological regulation requires stability (homeostasis), which is achieved through negative feedback. Positive feedback is reserved for processes where you want to drive forward to a defined completion point.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing receptor location based on signal molecule type.** Hydrophilic signals → MEMBRANE receptor (can't cross bilayer). Hydrophobic signals → INTRACELLULAR receptor (diffuse through bilayer).
2. **Misordering signal transduction stages.** Reception → Transduction → Response.
3. **Treating positive feedback as rare disease.** Positive feedback exists in normal biology (childbirth, clotting, action potentials) but is rare and always has defined endpoint.
4. **Confusing positive and negative feedback direction.** Negative = response REDUCES stimulus. Positive = response AMPLIFIES stimulus.
5. **Misnaming the cell cycle stages.** Interphase (G1, S, G2) → M phase (mitosis + cytokinesis).
6. **Misordering mitosis (PMAT).** Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase.
7. **Confusing mitosis with cytokinesis.** Mitosis = nuclear division. Cytokinesis = cytoplasmic division.
8. **Saying mitosis produces 4 daughter cells.** That's MEIOSIS. Mitosis produces 2 genetically identical daughter cells.
9. **Treating cell cycle checkpoints as optional.** Checkpoints are essential; bypassing them leads to cancer.
10. **Confusing oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes.** Oncogenes: normally promote division; mutation makes them OVERACTIVE. Tumor suppressors: normally inhibit division; mutation makes them INACTIVE.
11. **Treating cancer as one disease.** Cancer is many diseases sharing the common feature of dysregulated cell cycle.
12. **Saying chemotherapy kills only cancer cells.** Chemotherapy targets dividing cells generally — also affects normal rapidly-dividing cells (hair, gut lining, bone marrow), causing side effects.
13. **Forgetting that signal cascades amplify.** A single ligand binding can produce thousands of downstream effects.
14. **Confusing GPCRs and RTKs.** GPCRs activate G proteins; RTKs are themselves enzymes (kinases). Different mechanisms.
15. **Treating apoptosis as random cell death.** Apoptosis is HIGHLY REGULATED programmed cell death — distinct from uncontrolled necrosis.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For signal transduction questions:**
- Identify the three stages.
- Identify ligand and receptor type.
- Trace the cascade.
- Identify the response.

**For receptor questions:**
- Determine receptor location based on ligand chemistry.
- Hydrophilic → membrane receptor.
- Hydrophobic → intracellular.

**For feedback questions:**
- Identify positive vs negative.
- Trace stimulus → response → effect on stimulus.

**For cell cycle questions:**
- Identify the stage by features.
- Note checkpoint role.
- Connect to cancer if relevant.

**For mitosis questions:**
- Use PMAT to identify stages.
- Distinguish mitosis (nuclear) from cytokinesis (cytoplasmic).
- Note products: 2 genetically identical daughter cells.
