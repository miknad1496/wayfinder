- **Sub-topics covered:**

  - 3.2 Enzyme Catalysis
  - 3.3 Environmental Impacts on Enzyme Function
  - 3.4 Cellular Energy
  - 3.5 Photosynthesis
  - 3.6 Cellular Respiration

- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Unit 3 is heavily tested. Enzyme function (especially the effects of temperature, pH, inhibitors) is in nearly every exam. Cellular respiration's stages (glycolysis → pyruvate oxidation → Krebs → oxidative phosphorylation) and photosynthesis's stages (light reactions + Calvin cycle) are perennial FRQs. Chemiosmosis (proton gradient + ATP synthase) is the most-tested mechanism — appearing in both photosynthesis and respiration contexts. Comparing aerobic vs anaerobic respiration recurs.

## Big Ideas

1. **Enzymes lower activation energy without being consumed.** They speed up reactions by stabilizing the transition state. Each enzyme is specific for its substrate(s); shape determines function.
2. **ATP is the universal energy currency of cells.** Energy released from breaking down food (cellular respiration) is captured in ATP, which can then be hydrolyzed to drive endergonic reactions (active transport, biosynthesis, movement).
3. **Cellular respiration converts glucose to ATP through four stages.** Glycolysis (cytoplasm) → Pyruvate Oxidation (matrix) → Krebs Cycle (matrix) → Oxidative Phosphorylation (inner membrane). Each stage produces ATP and electron carriers (NADH, FADH₂) that feed the electron transport chain.
4. **Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy.** Light reactions (thylakoid membrane) produce ATP and NADPH; Calvin cycle (stroma) uses these to fix CO₂ into glucose. The reverse of cellular respiration in many ways.
5. **Chemiosmosis is the central energy-transduction mechanism in BOTH respiration and photosynthesis.** A proton gradient is built across a membrane; ATP synthase uses the gradient to make ATP. The same fundamental mechanism in both processes.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Enzymes:** biological catalysts (almost always proteins; some are RNA — ribozymes).
- **Active site:** the specific region where substrate binds. Shape complementary to substrate.
- **Substrate:** the molecule(s) on which the enzyme acts.
- **Enzyme-substrate complex:** the temporary bound state.
- **Product:** the molecules formed by the reaction.
- **Lock-and-key model (older):** substrate fits perfectly into pre-formed active site.
- **Induced fit model (current):** substrate binding causes conformational change in enzyme; active site molds slightly around substrate.
- **Activation energy (Eₐ):** the energy required to start a reaction (reach the transition state). Enzymes LOWER activation energy.
- **Enzymes don't change ΔG.** They don't change WHETHER a reaction is favorable, only how FAST it proceeds.
- **Factors affecting enzyme activity:**
  - **Temperature:** increases activity up to optimum, then DENATURES at high temperatures.
  - **pH:** each enzyme has an optimum pH. Extreme pH denatures.
  - **Substrate concentration:** as [S] increases, rate increases until enzyme is SATURATED (all active sites occupied). Plateaus at Vmax.
  - **Enzyme concentration:** as [E] increases, rate increases proportionally (assuming substrate is in excess).
  - **Inhibitors:**
    - **Competitive:** binds to active site, competes with substrate. Effects can be overcome by adding more substrate.
    - **Noncompetitive (allosteric):** binds to a different site, changes enzyme shape. Adding more substrate doesn't help.
- **Cofactors:** non-protein molecules required for enzyme function. Inorganic cofactors (Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺) or organic coenzymes (vitamins like NAD⁺, FAD).
- **Energy and metabolism:**
  - **Exergonic reaction:** releases free energy (negative ΔG). Spontaneous.
  - **Endergonic reaction:** requires free energy input (positive ΔG). Non-spontaneous.
  - **Coupled reactions:** exergonic reactions drive endergonic reactions. ATP hydrolysis (exergonic) provides energy for cellular processes (endergonic).
- **ATP (adenosine triphosphate):**
  - Adenine + ribose + 3 phosphate groups.
  - **High-energy bonds:** especially the bond between the second and third phosphate.
  - **ATP → ADP + Pi + ENERGY:** hydrolysis releases energy that drives cellular work.
  - **ADP + Pi + ENERGY → ATP:** synthesis stores energy from cellular respiration.
- **Cellular respiration overview:**
  - **C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + ENERGY (ATP + heat)**
  - Glucose is fully oxidized; oxygen is fully reduced.
  - Total ATP yield: ~30-32 ATP per glucose (in eukaryotes; some textbooks use 36 ATP).
- **Four stages of cellular respiration:**
  - **(1) Glycolysis** (cytoplasm):
    - Glucose (6C) → 2 Pyruvate (3C each).
    - Net: 2 ATP, 2 NADH per glucose.
    - Anaerobic (doesn't require oxygen).
  - **(2) Pyruvate oxidation** (matrix of mitochondria):
    - Each pyruvate → acetyl-CoA + CO₂.
    - Per glucose: 2 NADH, 2 CO₂.
  - **(3) Krebs Cycle / Citric Acid Cycle** (matrix):
    - Each acetyl-CoA → 2 CO₂ + 1 ATP + 3 NADH + 1 FADH₂.
    - Per glucose (2 acetyl-CoA): 2 ATP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, 4 CO₂.
  - **(4) Oxidative Phosphorylation** (inner membrane of mitochondria):
    - Electron Transport Chain (ETC): NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons.
    - Electrons pass through protein complexes, releasing energy.
    - Energy pumps protons (H⁺) from matrix to intermembrane space.
    - Creates proton gradient.
    - **Chemiosmosis:** protons flow back through ATP SYNTHASE, generating ATP.
    - Final electron acceptor: O₂ → forms H₂O.
    - Per glucose: ~26-28 ATP from oxidative phosphorylation.
- **Photosynthesis overview:**
  - **6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂**
  - Solar energy converted to chemical energy in glucose.
  - Two stages: light reactions and Calvin Cycle.
- **Light Reactions** (thylakoid membrane):
  - Light hits chlorophyll → energizes electrons.
  - Photosystem II (PSII) splits water: H₂O → 2H⁺ + ½ O₂ + 2e⁻ (photolysis). O₂ released as byproduct.
  - Energized electrons pass through ETC, pumping H⁺ into thylakoid lumen → proton gradient.
  - **Chemiosmosis:** H⁺ flows through ATP SYNTHASE → ATP.
  - PSI re-energizes electrons → NADPH formed (reduces NADP⁺ to NADPH).
- **Calvin Cycle** (stroma):
  - Uses ATP and NADPH from light reactions.
  - **Carbon fixation:** RuBisCO catalyzes addition of CO₂ to RuBP (5C compound) → unstable 6C → 2 PGA (3C each).
  - Reduction phase: PGA → G3P (uses ATP and NADPH).
  - Some G3P leaves to make glucose; rest regenerates RuBP.
  - 6 CO₂ → 1 glucose (requires 18 ATP and 12 NADPH).

### Adds for [4]

- **The role of NAD⁺/NADH and FAD/FADH₂.** These are ELECTRON CARRIERS — they pick up electrons (and become reduced — gain electrons = reduction) and deliver them to the electron transport chain. Each NADH delivers 2 electrons; each FADH₂ delivers 2 electrons.
- **Why each NADH yields ~3 ATP and each FADH₂ yields ~2 ATP** (in older textbook calculations): NADH enters ETC at Complex I (more proton pumping). FADH₂ enters at Complex II (less proton pumping). Modern measurements give ~2.5 ATP per NADH and ~1.5 ATP per FADH₂.
- **Chemiosmosis worked through:**
  - **Step 1:** ETC pumps H⁺ across membrane, creating gradient (more H⁺ on one side).
  - **Step 2:** H⁺ has potential energy due to gradient — wants to flow back to lower concentration.
  - **Step 3:** ATP synthase is a transmembrane protein with a "rotor" that turns as H⁺ flows through.
  - **Step 4:** Rotation drives the binding of ADP + Pi → ATP.
  - **Same mechanism in mitochondria (respiration) and chloroplasts (photosynthesis), and even in some prokaryotes.**
- **Aerobic vs anaerobic respiration:**
  - **Aerobic:** requires oxygen as final electron acceptor. Yields ~30-32 ATP per glucose. Process: glycolysis + pyruvate oxidation + Krebs + oxidative phosphorylation.
  - **Anaerobic (fermentation):** glycolysis only + regeneration of NAD⁺. Yields only 2 ATP per glucose. Two main types:
    - **Alcoholic fermentation (yeast, some bacteria):** pyruvate → ethanol + CO₂.
    - **Lactic acid fermentation (animal muscle when O₂ low, some bacteria):** pyruvate → lactic acid.
  - Why fermentation regenerates NAD⁺: glycolysis needs NAD⁺ to continue; without oxygen to regenerate NAD⁺ via the ETC, fermentation pathway provides this function.
- **C3, C4, and CAM photosynthesis** — adaptations to different environments:
  - **C3 (most plants):** standard Calvin cycle. RuBisCO fixes CO₂ to RuBP. Photorespiration (RuBisCO accidentally fixes O₂ instead) is a problem in hot/dry conditions because stomata close and CO₂ runs low.
  - **C4 (corn, sugarcane):** spatial separation. CO₂ first fixed in mesophyll cells (4C compound), then transferred to bundle-sheath cells where Calvin cycle occurs. Concentrates CO₂ near RuBisCO, reducing photorespiration.
  - **CAM (cacti, succulents):** temporal separation. Stomata open at NIGHT to take in CO₂ (stored as 4C compound), close during DAY when Calvin cycle uses stored CO₂. Conserves water in dry environments.
- **Oxidation-reduction (REDOX) in metabolism:**
  - **Oxidation:** loss of electrons (or hydrogens, since H carries electrons in living systems).
  - **Reduction:** gain of electrons.
  - Memory: OIL RIG (Oxidation Is Loss; Reduction Is Gain).
  - Cellular respiration: glucose is OXIDIZED (loses H atoms); oxygen is REDUCED (gains H atoms to form H₂O).
  - Photosynthesis: CO₂ is REDUCED to glucose; H₂O is OXIDIZED to O₂.
  - The reactions are mirror images of each other in many ways.
- **Where ATP is made in respiration:**
  - **Substrate-level phosphorylation** (direct enzyme-mediated transfer of phosphate to ADP): glycolysis (2 ATP), Krebs (2 ATP).
  - **Oxidative phosphorylation** (chemiosmosis using gradient from ETC): ~26-28 ATP.
  - Total: ~30-32 ATP per glucose.
- **The "energy investment" stage of glycolysis.** Glycolysis uses 2 ATP at the start to phosphorylate glucose, producing 4 ATP later. Net: 4 − 2 = 2 ATP per glucose. Investment of ATP enables the subsequent payoff.
- **Why the proton gradient is essential to ATP production.** Without the gradient, ATP synthase has no driving force to rotate. The gradient stores energy in the form of an electrochemical potential. The same logic applies to bacterial flagellar rotation, which also uses proton gradients.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why aerobic respiration is so much more efficient than fermentation.** Glycolysis alone yields 2 ATP per glucose. Aerobic respiration yields ~30-32. The difference (~15-fold) comes from oxidative phosphorylation, which requires oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor. Without oxygen, the ETC backs up — electrons can't flow → no proton gradient → no ATP synthesis → cells can only use glycolysis. This is why oxygen is essential to most complex life.
- **The endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts (from Unit 2) explains why they have separate genomes and proton gradients.** They were once free-living bacteria. Their ancestors used the same chemiosmotic mechanism. When engulfed by ancestral eukaryotes, they brought this capability with them. Modern eukaryotes inherited bacterial energy-production machinery.
- **Why oxidative phosphorylation makes much more ATP than substrate-level phosphorylation.** Each NADH from glycolysis/Krebs delivers 2 electrons through 3 sites of proton pumping (Complexes I, III, IV) → ~2.5 ATP. Each FADH₂ delivers 2 electrons through 2 sites of proton pumping (Complexes III, IV) → ~1.5 ATP. The 10 NADH and 2 FADH₂ produced per glucose generate ~28 ATP through oxidative phosphorylation.
- **Why C4 and CAM are evolutionary adaptations to specific environments.** Photorespiration (RuBisCO fixing O₂ instead of CO₂) wastes energy and reduces glucose production. Hot, dry conditions force plants to close stomata, reducing CO₂ and increasing O₂ inside the leaf. C4 and CAM evolved separately in different plant lineages, both addressing this problem through different strategies (spatial vs temporal CO₂ concentration).
- **The "Z-scheme" of photosynthesis** — energy diagram showing electron flow from PSII through PSI:
  - Light excites PSII electrons.
  - Excited electrons pass through ETC (releasing energy → proton gradient).
  - Electrons reach PSI; light excites them again.
  - Excited electrons reduce NADP⁺ → NADPH.
  - Replacement electrons for PSII come from photolysis of water (releasing O₂).
  - The "Z" shape comes from the energy levels going up (excitation) → down (electron transport) → up (excitation) → down (NADP+ reduction).
- **Why ATP synthase is an evolutionarily ancient molecular machine.** ATP synthase is found in essentially all life — bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes. The structure (a rotating motor that converts proton flow into mechanical rotation that produces ATP) is one of the most conserved structures in biology. It evolved very early in life's history, before the divergence of major lineages.
- **The "induced fit" model and modern enzyme thinking.** The lock-and-key model suggests rigid complementarity. Induced fit recognizes that BOTH enzyme and substrate undergo conformational changes upon binding. This explains:
  - **Allosteric regulation:** binding at one site affects shape at another site.
  - **Cooperativity:** binding of one substrate can change the enzyme's affinity for additional substrates.
  - **Specificity:** enzymes can be specific even when substrates are similar (subtle shape adjustments distinguish).
- **Why metabolic pathways are regulated by feedback inhibition.** A pathway's end product often inhibits an early enzyme in the pathway. This prevents waste — when product accumulates, synthesis slows. Example: ATP inhibits phosphofructokinase (regulating glycolysis); when ATP is plentiful, glycolysis slows. This negative feedback is the major regulatory mechanism in metabolism.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Predicting effect of factors on enzyme

A particular enzyme has optimum activity at 37°C and pH 7. Predict effect on activity of:
(a) Heating to 60°C.
(b) Cooling to 10°C.
(c) Adding HCl to lower pH to 4.
(d) Increasing substrate concentration.

- **(a)** 60°C far above optimum → likely DENATURED. Activity drops dramatically (perhaps to zero) and is irreversible.
- **(b)** 10°C below optimum → activity REDUCED but not denatured. Cooling slows molecular motion. Activity could be restored by warming.
- **(c)** pH 4 far from optimum → activity reduced; potentially denatured if extreme pH. Affects ionization of R groups, disrupts noncovalent interactions.
- **(d)** Increasing [substrate] → increases activity until enzyme is SATURATED. After all active sites are occupied, adding more substrate has no effect (rate plateaus at Vmax).

### Example 2 [3][4] — Identifying inhibitor type

In an experiment, adding more substrate restores enzyme activity to nearly normal in Inhibitor A but doesn't help with Inhibitor B.

(a) Identify inhibitor type.
(b) Explain mechanism.

- **(a)** Inhibitor A = competitive inhibitor. Inhibitor B = noncompetitive (allosteric).
- **(b) Mechanism:** Competitive inhibitor BINDS TO ACTIVE SITE, competing with substrate. Adding more substrate increases the chance that substrate (not inhibitor) binds → activity restored. Noncompetitive inhibitor binds at a DIFFERENT site (allosteric site), changing the enzyme's shape so substrate can no longer bind effectively. Adding more substrate doesn't help because the enzyme's shape is already changed.

### Example 3 [4] — Aerobic vs anaerobic ATP yields

A muscle cell metabolizes one molecule of glucose during intense exercise. Initially aerobic (oxygen abundant), but oxygen depletes → switches to fermentation.

(a) Compare ATP yield in each phase.
(b) Explain WHY fermentation produces so much less ATP.

- **(a) Aerobic:** glycolysis (2 ATP) + Krebs cycle substrate-level (2 ATP) + oxidative phosphorylation (~26-28 ATP) = ~30-32 ATP per glucose. **Anaerobic fermentation:** glycolysis only (2 ATP). NO Krebs, NO oxidative phosphorylation.
- **(b)** Without oxygen, the ETC can't function (no terminal electron acceptor). NADH cannot dump its electrons via the ETC. NAD+ is not regenerated. Glycolysis stalls. Fermentation regenerates NAD+ by reducing pyruvate (to lactate or ethanol) without producing additional ATP. The ~28 ATP from oxidative phosphorylation is unavailable.
- **Implication:** muscle fatigue during intense exercise reflects this energy deficit. Lactic acid buildup is a side effect of fermentation in animal muscle.

### Example 4 [4] — Photosynthesis stages worked through

For each step, identify the stage (light reactions or Calvin cycle) and the location:

(a) Water is split, releasing O₂ as byproduct.
(b) ATP is generated by ATP synthase as protons flow across membrane.
(c) CO₂ is fixed to RuBP by RuBisCO.
(d) NADP+ is reduced to NADPH.
(e) Glucose precursor (G3P) is produced.

- **(a) Light reactions, thylakoid membrane.** Photolysis of water provides electrons for PSII.
- **(b) Light reactions, thylakoid membrane.** Chemiosmosis through ATP synthase generates ATP.
- **(c) Calvin cycle, stroma.** RuBisCO catalyzes carbon fixation.
- **(d) Light reactions, thylakoid membrane.** PSI's energized electrons reduce NADP+ to NADPH.
- **(e) Calvin cycle, stroma.** G3P is produced through reduction (using ATP and NADPH from light reactions).

### Example 5 [4][5] — Chemiosmosis comparison

Compare chemiosmosis in mitochondria (cellular respiration) and chloroplasts (photosynthesis).

- **Similarities:**
  - Same fundamental mechanism: proton gradient + ATP synthase.
  - Both use electron transport chains to pump protons.
  - Both produce ATP from the gradient.
  - Both involve protons moving from high concentration to low through ATP synthase.
- **Differences:**
  - **Energy source:** mitochondria use chemical energy from food (NADH from glucose breakdown). Chloroplasts use light energy.
  - **Direction of proton movement:** in mitochondria, protons pumped INTO intermembrane space, flow back into matrix. In chloroplasts, protons pumped INTO thylakoid lumen, flow back into stroma.
  - **Final electron acceptor:** mitochondria use O₂ (forms H₂O). Chloroplasts pass electrons to NADP+ (forms NADPH).
  - **What it produces:** mitochondria's ATP fuels cellular work. Chloroplasts' ATP and NADPH fuel Calvin cycle to make glucose.
- **The deep insight:** chemiosmosis is the SAME mechanism transporting protons and using the gradient to make ATP. The differences in source, direction, and outcome reflect the different roles of these organelles, but the underlying energy transduction is identical. Both mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from prokaryotes (endosymbiotic theory), inheriting this ancient energy-production machinery.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Saying enzymes are "used up" in reactions.** Enzymes are CATALYSTS — not consumed; can catalyze reaction repeatedly.
2. **Saying enzymes change ΔG.** Enzymes lower activation energy (Eₐ), making reactions FASTER. They don't change WHETHER the reaction is favorable.
3. **Confusing competitive and noncompetitive inhibition.** Competitive: binds active site. Noncompetitive: binds elsewhere. Adding substrate helps competitive but not noncompetitive.
4. **Misnaming the stages of cellular respiration.** Glycolysis (cytoplasm) → Pyruvate Oxidation (matrix) → Krebs Cycle (matrix) → Oxidative Phosphorylation (inner membrane).
5. **Wrong location for cellular respiration stages.** Glycolysis is CYTOPLASM. The other three are in MITOCHONDRIA.
6. **Forgetting the energy investment in glycolysis.** Glycolysis NETS 2 ATP per glucose (4 ATP made minus 2 ATP invested initially).
7. **Misordering oxidative phosphorylation steps.** ETC pumps protons → proton gradient → chemiosmosis through ATP synthase → ATP made.
8. **Treating fermentation as "making ATP."** Fermentation regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can continue. The 2 ATP comes from glycolysis, not fermentation itself.
9. **Confusing photosynthesis stages.** Light reactions: in thylakoid membrane; produce ATP and NADPH. Calvin cycle: in stroma; uses ATP and NADPH; fixes CO₂.
10. **Misnaming the products of light reactions.** ATP, NADPH, AND O₂ (released as byproduct from water splitting).
11. **Misnaming RuBisCO.** It's the enzyme that catalyzes carbon fixation in Calvin cycle. Most abundant protein on Earth.
12. **Treating photosynthesis and respiration as opposites.** They're related but not exact opposites. Both use chemiosmosis with proton gradients. Both have ETCs. The substrate and end products differ.
13. **Forgetting C4 and CAM exist.** Most plants are C3, but C4 (corn, sugarcane) and CAM (cacti) are important adaptations.
14. **Saying chemiosmosis only happens in mitochondria.** It happens in BOTH mitochondria (respiration) AND chloroplasts (photosynthesis), and in many prokaryotes.
15. **Forgetting redox reactions in metabolism.** Glucose is OXIDIZED in respiration; CO₂ is REDUCED in photosynthesis. Tracking electrons (and their carriers NADH, NADPH, FADH₂) is critical.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For enzyme questions:**
- Identify factor (temp, pH, [substrate], inhibitor type).
- Explain effect on enzyme activity.
- Connect to mechanism (denaturation, ionization, saturation).

**For respiration questions:**
- Identify the stage by location and reactants/products.
- Track ATP, NADH, FADH₂, CO₂.
- Connect to chemiosmosis for ATP synthesis explanation.

**For photosynthesis questions:**
- Distinguish light reactions (thylakoid membrane) from Calvin cycle (stroma).
- Identify substrates and products of each.
- Show how light reactions feed Calvin cycle.

**For comparing aerobic and anaerobic:**
- Identify each pathway's stages.
- Compare ATP yield.
- Explain WHY (oxygen availability, ETC function, NAD+ regeneration).

**For chemiosmosis:**
- Describe proton gradient formation.
- Connect to ATP synthase rotation.
- Note the same mechanism in both respiration and photosynthesis.
