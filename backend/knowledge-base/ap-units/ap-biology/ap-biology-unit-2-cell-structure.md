## Big Ideas

1. **Cells are the fundamental units of life — and their compartmentalization enables complex function.** All cells share basic features (membrane, DNA, ribosomes, cytoplasm). Eukaryotic cells add membrane-bound organelles that COMPARTMENTALIZE incompatible reactions, allowing complex multicellular life.
2. **Surface area to volume ratio fundamentally limits cell size.** As cells grow, volume increases faster than surface area. Larger cells have less membrane per unit volume → can't exchange materials fast enough. This is why cells are small.
3. **Cell membranes are SELECTIVELY PERMEABLE.** Phospholipid bilayer + embedded proteins control what enters and exits. Small nonpolar molecules diffuse freely; polar/charged molecules need protein channels; large molecules require active transport or bulk transport (endocytosis/exocytosis).
4. **Active transport requires energy; passive transport doesn't.** Passive (diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis): goes WITH concentration gradient, no ATP. Active: goes AGAINST gradient, requires ATP. Membrane proteins enable both.
5. **Endosymbiotic theory explains the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts.** They were once free-living prokaryotes engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells. Evidence: own DNA, own ribosomes (bacterial-type), double membranes, can divide independently. Foundational for understanding eukaryotic evolution.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Cell theory:** all living things are made of cells; cells are the basic unit of life; new cells come from existing cells.
- **Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic cells:**

| Feature | Prokaryote | Eukaryote |
|---------|-----------|------------|
| Examples | Bacteria, Archaea | Animals, Plants, Fungi, Protists |
| Nucleus | NO (DNA in nucleoid region) | YES (DNA in nucleus) |
| Membrane-bound organelles | NO | YES |
| Cell size | Small (1–10 μm) | Larger (10–100 μm) |
| Ribosomes | Smaller (70S) | Larger (80S in cytoplasm) |
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan (bacteria) | Cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), none (animals) |
| Genetic material | Single circular DNA + plasmids | Multiple linear chromosomes |

- **Key eukaryotic organelles** and their functions:
  - **Nucleus:** stores DNA; controls cell activities. Bounded by nuclear envelope (double membrane with pores).
  - **Nucleolus:** within nucleus; site of ribosome assembly.
  - **Ribosomes:** site of protein synthesis. Found in cytoplasm (free) and on rough ER (membrane-bound).
  - **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER):**
    - **Rough ER (with ribosomes):** synthesizes proteins for export or membrane.
    - **Smooth ER (no ribosomes):** synthesizes lipids, detoxifies drugs (especially in liver), stores calcium.
  - **Golgi apparatus:** modifies, sorts, packages proteins and lipids from ER. Sends them to their destinations via vesicles.
  - **Mitochondria:** site of cellular respiration (ATP production). Have own DNA. Double membrane (inner highly folded into cristae).
  - **Chloroplasts (plants only):** site of photosynthesis. Own DNA. Double membrane. Internal thylakoid membranes stacked into grana.
  - **Lysosomes (animals):** contain digestive enzymes; break down old organelles, food, debris.
  - **Vacuoles:** storage. Plants have one large central vacuole providing turgor pressure.
  - **Peroxisomes:** detoxify hydrogen peroxide.
  - **Cytoskeleton:** internal protein network. Microfilaments (actin), intermediate filaments, microtubules.
  - **Centrosomes / centrioles** (animals): organize microtubules; play role in cell division.
  - **Cilia and flagella:** movement. Different structures (cilia shorter, more numerous; flagella longer, fewer).
- **Plant cells specifically have:**
  - Cell wall (cellulose).
  - Central vacuole.
  - Chloroplasts.
- **Animal cells specifically have:**
  - Centrioles (in some).
  - Lysosomes.
- **Endosymbiotic Theory** — explains the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts:
  - Both were once free-living prokaryotes.
  - Ancestral eukaryotic cell engulfed them; they became endosymbionts.
  - **Evidence:**
    - **Double membranes** (outer = engulfing cell's; inner = original prokaryote's).
    - **Own circular DNA** (like bacteria).
    - **Bacterial-type ribosomes** (70S, like prokaryotes).
    - **Reproduce by binary fission** (independent of cell division).
    - **Size similar to bacteria.**
- **Surface area to volume (SA:V) ratio:**
  - As cells grow, volume increases by cube of length, surface area by square.
  - Result: SA:V DECREASES with size.
  - **Implication:** small cells have HIGH SA:V → efficient material exchange. Large cells have LOW SA:V → can't exchange materials fast enough.
  - **Adaptations to overcome SA:V limit:**
    - Stay small (most cells are 10-100 μm).
    - Folded membranes (mitochondria's cristae, intestinal microvilli).
    - Multiple smaller cells rather than one large.
- **Plasma membrane structure (Fluid Mosaic Model):**
  - Phospholipid bilayer (hydrophilic heads outside, hydrophobic tails inside).
  - Embedded proteins (integral or peripheral).
  - Cholesterol (regulates fluidity).
  - Carbohydrates attached to proteins (glycoproteins) or lipids (glycolipids) on outer surface — function in cell recognition.
  - "Mosaic": diverse proteins scattered throughout. "Fluid": components can move laterally.
- **Membrane permeability:**
  - **Easily crosses (small, nonpolar):** O₂, CO₂, H₂O (slowly), small lipids.
  - **Crosses slowly:** glucose (uses transport protein), amino acids.
  - **Cannot easily cross (large, polar, charged):** ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺), large proteins, DNA.
- **Types of membrane transport:**
  - **Passive transport (no ATP):**
    - **Simple diffusion:** small nonpolar molecules move down concentration gradient through lipid bilayer.
    - **Facilitated diffusion:** polar/charged molecules move down gradient through transport proteins (channel or carrier).
    - **Osmosis:** diffusion of WATER through selectively permeable membrane.
  - **Active transport (requires ATP):**
    - Moves substances AGAINST concentration gradient.
    - Uses transport proteins.
    - Example: sodium-potassium pump (3 Na⁺ out + 2 K⁺ in for each ATP).
  - **Bulk transport:**
    - **Endocytosis:** cell engulfs material into vesicles. Phagocytosis ("cell eating"), pinocytosis ("cell drinking"), receptor-mediated endocytosis.
    - **Exocytosis:** cell expels material via vesicles fusing with plasma membrane.
- **Tonicity** (relative solute concentration of solution compared to cell):
  - **Hypertonic** (more solutes outside than inside): water flows OUT of cell. Cell shrinks (animal cells crenate; plant cells plasmolysis).
  - **Hypotonic** (fewer solutes outside than inside): water flows INTO cell. Cell swells (animal cells lyse; plant cells become turgid — supported by cell wall).
  - **Isotonic** (equal solute concentrations): no net water movement. Cell maintains shape.
- **Water potential** (more advanced concept):
  - Water moves from HIGH water potential to LOW water potential.
  - Pure water has water potential = 0.
  - Adding solutes DECREASES water potential.
  - Water moves to where solutes are MORE concentrated (toward lower water potential).
- **Compartmentalization:** organelles separate incompatible reactions. Examples:
  - Lysosomal hydrolytic enzymes contained in lysosomes (don't digest cytoplasm).
  - Mitochondrial proton gradient maintained across inner membrane.
  - Nuclear envelope separates DNA processing from cytoplasm.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why the SA:V ratio matters in cell biology and biology generally.** A cube with 1 cm sides has SA = 6 cm², V = 1 cm³, SA:V = 6:1. A cube with 2 cm sides has SA = 24 cm², V = 8 cm³, SA:V = 3:1. Doubling size halves SA:V. This geometric reality limits:
  - **Cell size:** explains why cells are usually 10-100 μm.
  - **Tissue organization:** flat sheets (epithelium), branched structures (lung alveoli, kidney tubules, plant root hairs, intestinal microvilli) maximize SA:V.
  - **Animal body shape:** larger animals have proportionally smaller surface area, requiring specialized exchange surfaces (lungs, kidneys, intestines).
- **Membrane fluidity factors:**
  - **Temperature:** higher T → more fluid.
  - **Cholesterol:** at high T, restricts movement (less fluid). At low T, prevents tight packing (more fluid). Buffers against extremes.
  - **Saturated vs unsaturated fatty acids:** unsaturated (kinked tails) → less tightly packed → more fluid. Saturated → more tightly packed → less fluid.
- **Why active transport exists:** sometimes cells need substances at higher concentrations than the surrounding environment (e.g., neurons maintain high K⁺ inside, low Na⁺ inside; nutrients need to be concentrated inside cells). Without active transport, equilibrium would equalize concentrations inside and outside, which would prevent many essential cellular processes.
- **Sodium-potassium pump details:**
  - Uses ATP (hydrolyzed to ADP + Pi).
  - Moves 3 Na⁺ OUT and 2 K⁺ IN per ATP.
  - Net charge change: 1 positive charge moved out per cycle → contributes to membrane potential.
  - Critical for nerve impulse conduction, kidney function, cellular volume regulation.
- **Cotransport (secondary active transport):**
  - Uses energy stored in concentration gradients (created by primary active transport like Na/K pump) to move other substances.
  - Example: Na+/glucose cotransporter in intestinal cells uses Na+ gradient to import glucose against its gradient.
- **The endosymbiotic theory in detail:**
  - **Mitochondria** were once aerobic bacteria. The ancestral cell that engulfed them gained the ability to perform oxidative phosphorylation (much more ATP from glucose).
  - **Chloroplasts** were once cyanobacteria (oxygenic photosynthesizers). Engulfed by an ancestral cell with mitochondria, creating the lineage leading to modern plants and algae.
  - **Sequence:** mitochondria arose first (~2 billion years ago), chloroplasts later (~1.5 billion years ago) in a separate event.
  - **Genetic legacy:** mitochondria and chloroplasts have DNA, but most of their genes have transferred to the nuclear genome over evolutionary time. They're not fully autonomous; they depend on nuclear-encoded proteins.
- **Compartmentalization advantages:**
  - **Separation of incompatible reactions** (hydrolysis in lysosomes vs synthesis in cytoplasm).
  - **Specialized environments** (acidic pH in lysosomes, low Ca²⁺ in cytoplasm but high in ER).
  - **Concentrated reactants** (substrates kept in proximity to enzymes).
  - **Controlled access to genetic material** (nucleus regulates RNA export).
- **Connecting compartmentalization to cell complexity:**
  - Prokaryotes: limited specialization, all reactions in cytoplasm.
  - Eukaryotes: extensive specialization, diverse organelles enabling complex multicellular life.
  - Multicellular life with tissue specialization (Unit 8) requires the cell-level complexity that compartmentalization enables.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why cells didn't evolve to be larger** (despite the theoretical advantages of complexity): cellular reactions need rapid material exchange, which requires high SA:V. Cells could in principle grow larger if they had compensatory adaptations (folded membranes, internal transport systems), but most cells stay in the 10-100 μm range because that's the size at which all the biochemistry works efficiently. Large single-celled organisms (paramecia, amoebas) have extensive folded membranes and complex internal transport.
- **The evolutionary significance of mitochondria.** The endosymbiotic acquisition of mitochondria (~2 Ga) was arguably the single most important event in eukaryotic evolution. Aerobic respiration produces ~16x more ATP than anaerobic processes. This energy bonanza made possible:
  - Larger cell sizes.
  - Multicellular complexity.
  - Energy-expensive features like nervous systems and locomotion.
  - Without mitochondria, the energy budget for complex life wouldn't exist.
- **Why plants need both mitochondria and chloroplasts.** Chloroplasts capture solar energy (photosynthesis); mitochondria release energy from glucose for cell use (cellular respiration). Plant cells DO BOTH — they create glucose AND consume it for energy. At night (no light), plants rely on mitochondria. During day, plants do both simultaneously, but photosynthesis usually exceeds respiration, producing net oxygen.
- **The "selective permeability" concept rethought.** Membranes aren't selective in the sense of "knowing" what to let through. Rather, the chemistry of the lipid bilayer + membrane proteins creates ASYMMETRIES in transport rates that effectively select. Small nonpolar molecules cross 10-100 million times faster than large polar molecules. The result is "selective" but the mechanism is purely physical.
- **The "cellular volume" problem.** Cells must maintain stable volume despite osmotic pressures. Without active regulation, cells would either burst (in hypotonic environments) or shrivel (in hypertonic). Adaptations:
  - **Cell wall** in plants, bacteria, fungi: prevents bursting.
  - **Contractile vacuoles** in protists: actively pump out water.
  - **Active solute regulation** in animal cells: pumps maintain osmotic balance.
  - **Different osmolarity requirements** in different organisms (kangaroo rats produce concentrated urine to conserve water; freshwater fish constantly excrete water).
- **Endosymbiotic theory's broader implications.** The theory implies that life's complexity arose through SYMBIOSIS, not just direct selection. Multiple lineages have undergone secondary endosymbiosis (algae engulfing other algae). Modern research (especially from Lynn Margulis) shows that symbiotic origins are more common in evolution than previously recognized.
- **The "energy crisis" of cell size.** As cells grow, SA:V drops, but METABOLIC NEEDS scale with volume. Eventually a large cell cannot get enough nutrients across its membrane to support its metabolism. This is why mitochondria themselves are small (1-2 μm) — even within a cell, the energy-producing organelles need high SA:V.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Identifying organelles

For each function, identify the organelle:
(a) Site of cellular respiration (ATP production).
(b) Stores hydrolytic enzymes; digests food and old organelles.
(c) Modifies and packages proteins from ER for export.
(d) Synthesizes lipids and detoxifies drugs.
(e) Synthesizes most cellular proteins.

- **(a) Mitochondria** (cellular respiration, ATP production)
- **(b) Lysosomes** (digestive enzymes)
- **(c) Golgi apparatus** (modification, packaging, sorting)
- **(d) Smooth ER** (lipid synthesis, drug detoxification)
- **(e) Ribosomes** (protein synthesis)

### Example 2 [3][4] — Cell SA:V calculation

Calculate SA:V for two cubic cells: side length 1 μm and side length 2 μm.

- **Cell A (1 μm side):** SA = 6 × 1² = 6 μm². V = 1³ = 1 μm³. SA:V = 6:1.
- **Cell B (2 μm side):** SA = 6 × 2² = 24 μm². V = 2³ = 8 μm³. SA:V = 24:8 = 3:1.
- **Conclusion:** Cell A has TWICE the SA:V of Cell B. As cells grow, SA:V decreases.
- **Implication:** the larger cell has proportionally LESS membrane per unit of volume, making material exchange harder. This is why cells stay small.

### Example 3 [3] — Tonicity prediction

Three solutions are placed in a beaker of red blood cells: pure water (Solution A), 0.9% saline (Solution B, same as RBC interior), and 5% saline (Solution C).

(a) Identify the tonicity of each solution relative to RBC.
(b) Predict what happens to RBC in each.

- **(a) Solution A (pure water):** HYPOTONIC (less solute than RBC). **Solution B (0.9% saline):** ISOTONIC (same as RBC interior — this is why IV fluids are 0.9% saline). **Solution C (5% saline):** HYPERTONIC (more solute than RBC).
- **(b) RBC reactions:**
  - **In Solution A:** water flows IN → cell SWELLS → eventually LYSES (bursts).
  - **In Solution B:** no net water movement → cell maintains shape.
  - **In Solution C:** water flows OUT → cell SHRINKS → CRENATES (becomes shriveled).

### Example 4 [4] — Active vs passive transport

A cell has [glucose] = 5 mM inside, while the surrounding environment has [glucose] = 50 mM. Glucose enters via facilitated diffusion. Later, a different molecule X has [X] = 10 mM inside while environment has [X] = 1 mM. X enters the cell.

For each:
(a) Identify the type of transport.
(b) Explain why ATP is or isn't needed.

- **(a) Glucose:** facilitated diffusion (passive — moves DOWN gradient from 50 mM outside to 5 mM inside; uses transport protein).
- **(a) X:** active transport (against gradient — moving from 1 mM outside to 10 mM inside; requires energy input).
- **(b) Glucose:** no ATP needed. Concentration gradient provides the driving force for spontaneous movement.
- **(b) X:** ATP required. The cell must input energy to move X against its gradient, just as you must input energy to push a ball uphill.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Endosymbiotic theory evidence

A student claims mitochondria are just regular eukaryotic organelles. List the evidence that mitochondria evolved through endosymbiosis from a free-living bacterium.

- **1. Double membrane:** outer = derived from engulfing cell's plasma membrane; inner = original bacterium's plasma membrane.
- **2. Own DNA:** mitochondria contain a small circular DNA (similar to bacterial DNA, distinct from nuclear DNA).
- **3. Own ribosomes:** mitochondrial ribosomes are 70S (bacterial-type), distinct from cytoplasmic 80S (eukaryotic).
- **4. Reproduce by binary fission:** mitochondria divide independently of the cell, similar to bacteria.
- **5. Similar size to bacteria:** mitochondria are 1-2 μm — similar to many bacteria.
- **6. Genetic similarity:** mitochondrial DNA is more similar to certain bacterial DNA (alpha-proteobacteria) than to nuclear DNA.
- **7. Sensitivity to bacterial-targeting antibiotics:** drugs that target bacterial protein synthesis (e.g., chloramphenicol) also affect mitochondrial protein synthesis.
- **Conclusion:** the consistent pattern of bacterial-like features in mitochondria strongly supports the endosymbiotic origin. The same evidence pattern applies to chloroplasts (which evolved from cyanobacteria).

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing prokaryotic and eukaryotic features.** Prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles AND nuclei. Eukaryotes have both.
2. **Misnaming organelles.** Mitochondria (NOT mitochondrion when plural). Chloroplast vs chlorophyll (chloroplast = organelle; chlorophyll = pigment within).
3. **Saying lysosomes exist in plants.** Most plants don't have lysosomes per se; they have similar functions in vacuoles.
4. **Confusing cilia and flagella.** Cilia: shorter, more numerous, like rowing. Flagella: longer, fewer, propeller-like.
5. **Treating membrane as static.** Membrane is FLUID — components move laterally.
6. **Confusing diffusion and osmosis.** Diffusion: solutes move down their concentration gradient. Osmosis: WATER moves down its (water) concentration gradient.
7. **Wrong direction of water flow in tonicity.** Water moves FROM hypotonic (more water) TO hypertonic (less water). Or equivalently: TOWARD higher solute concentration.
8. **Forgetting that osmosis follows water potential.** Water moves from high water potential to low water potential. Solutes lower water potential.
9. **Treating active transport as occasional.** Active transport runs CONTINUOUSLY in cells; it's a constant energy expenditure (sodium-potassium pump uses ~25% of cellular ATP in some cells).
10. **Misordering protein synthesis location.** Proteins are synthesized AT ribosomes (free in cytoplasm OR bound to rough ER). Then modified through ER and Golgi.
11. **Saying ribosomes are organelles.** Ribosomes are technically NOT membrane-bound, so some sources don't count them as organelles. They're cellular structures.
12. **Treating SA:V as something special about cells.** SA:V is a geometric reality of all 3D objects — applies to cells, organisms, organs, anything.
13. **Forgetting that water potential ≠ pure water.** Pure water has water potential 0. Adding solutes makes water potential more negative.
14. **Confusing types of endocytosis.** Phagocytosis = solid particles. Pinocytosis = liquid uptake. Receptor-mediated endocytosis = specific molecules via receptors.
15. **Missing endosymbiotic theory evidence.** Don't just say "they have DNA." Be specific: double membrane, own ribosomes, binary fission, etc.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For organelle identification:**
- Match organelle to function precisely.
- Use technical vocabulary (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosome).

**For cell type comparisons:**
- Use specific features (presence/absence of nucleus, organelles, cell wall composition).
- Distinguish bacteria from eukaryotic prokaryote-like features (no organelles).

**For SA:V questions:**
- Calculate explicitly for given dimensions.
- Compare ratios.
- Connect to biological function (efficient material exchange).

**For tonicity questions:**
- Identify solute concentrations on both sides.
- Determine relative tonicity (hypo/hyper/iso).
- Predict water movement direction.
- Predict cell consequences (lyse, crenate, maintain shape).

**For membrane transport questions:**
- Identify whether transport is WITH or AGAINST gradient.
- Identify whether ATP is needed.
- Identify the protein involved (channel vs carrier vs pump).

**For endosymbiotic theory:**
- Cite multiple lines of evidence.
- Explain WHY each line supports the theory.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Eukaryotic cells differ from prokaryotic cells in containing a true nucleus enclosed by a nuclear envelope and other membrane-bound organelles. This compartmentalization allows specialization and separation of incompatible reactions, enabling greater complexity."
2. "As cells grow larger, surface area increases by the SQUARE of length while volume increases by the CUBE of length. The resulting decrease in surface-area-to-volume ratio limits how efficiently larger cells can exchange materials with their environment, which is why most cells remain small (10-100 μm)."
3. "The plasma membrane is selectively permeable due to its phospholipid bilayer structure: hydrophobic interior excludes most polar and charged molecules, while small nonpolar molecules diffuse freely. Membrane proteins enable transport of polar molecules through facilitated diffusion (passive) or active transport (requires ATP)."
4. "In a hypertonic solution, water flows OUT of the cell down its concentration gradient, causing the cell to shrink (animal cells crenate; plant cells undergo plasmolysis as the cytoplasm pulls away from the cell wall)."
5. "Active transport requires ATP because it moves substances AGAINST their concentration gradient. Energy from ATP hydrolysis is used by transport proteins (such as the sodium-potassium pump) to overcome the diffusion gradient."
6. "Multiple lines of evidence support the endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts: double membranes, own circular DNA, bacterial-type 70S ribosomes, binary fission for replication, and bacterial-like size — together suggesting these organelles were once free-living prokaryotes engulfed by ancestral eukaryotic cells."
7. "Compartmentalization in eukaryotic cells allows incompatible reactions to occur simultaneously: digestive enzymes are contained in lysosomes (preventing self-digestion); proton gradients are maintained across mitochondrial inner membrane (enabling ATP production); DNA is sequestered in the nucleus (allowing controlled regulation of transcription)."
