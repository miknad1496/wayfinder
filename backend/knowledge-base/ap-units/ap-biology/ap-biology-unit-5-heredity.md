## Big Ideas

1. **Meiosis produces 4 genetically diverse haploid gametes.** The two key sources of variation: independent assortment of chromosomes during metaphase I, and crossing over (recombination) during prophase I.
2. **Mendelian genetics describes single-gene inheritance with discrete alleles.** Law of segregation: alleles separate during gamete formation. Law of independent assortment: genes on different chromosomes assort independently. Punnett squares predict offspring ratios.
3. **Many traits don't follow simple Mendelian patterns.** Incomplete dominance (blending), codominance (both expressed), multiple alleles (more than 2 alleles for one gene), polygenic inheritance (multiple genes for one trait), pleiotropy (one gene affects multiple traits), epistasis (one gene affects expression of another).
4. **Sex-linked traits are inherited differently in males and females.** X-linked recessive traits more common in males (one X, one allele determines phenotype). Examples: hemophilia, color blindness.
5. **Environment affects phenotype.** Even with identical genes (identical twins), environmental factors influence trait expression.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Chromosomes:**
  - **Homologous chromosomes:** matching pairs (one from each parent). Same gene loci but possibly different alleles.
  - **Sister chromatids:** two identical copies of a chromosome connected at the centromere (after DNA replication).
  - **Autosomes:** non-sex chromosomes (chromosomes 1-22 in humans).
  - **Sex chromosomes:** X and Y in mammals. XX = female, XY = male.
- **Diploid (2n)** = full set of chromosomes (humans: 46, in 23 pairs).
- **Haploid (n)** = half set (humans: 23). Gametes (sperm and egg).
- **Mitosis vs Meiosis:**

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Purpose | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Gamete formation |
| Number of divisions | 1 | 2 |
| Daughter cells | 2 | 4 |
| Chromosomes per daughter | 2n (diploid, same as parent) | n (haploid) |
| Genetic identity | Identical to parent | Different from parent (diversity) |

- **Meiosis stages:**
  - **Meiosis I:** reductional division (separates HOMOLOGOUS pairs).
    - **Prophase I:** chromosomes condense; homologous pairs synapse (form tetrads/bivalents); CROSSING OVER occurs.
    - **Metaphase I:** homologous pairs align at equator; INDEPENDENT ASSORTMENT.
    - **Anaphase I:** homologous chromosomes separate (NOT sister chromatids).
    - **Telophase I + cytokinesis:** two haploid cells with sister chromatids still attached.
  - **Meiosis II:** equational division (separates SISTER CHROMATIDS — like mitosis on haploid cells).
    - **Prophase II, Metaphase II, Anaphase II (sister chromatids separate), Telophase II + cytokinesis:** four haploid cells (gametes).
- **Sources of genetic variation in meiosis:**
  - **Independent assortment:** during metaphase I, homologous pairs orient randomly. With 23 pairs in humans, 2²³ = ~8 million possible combinations.
  - **Crossing over (recombination):** during prophase I, homologous chromosomes exchange segments. Adds further variation by creating new allele combinations.
  - **Random fertilization:** any egg can be fertilized by any sperm. Adds another factor of ~64 trillion combinations.
- **Mendelian genetics — vocabulary:**
  - **Gene:** segment of DNA encoding a trait.
  - **Allele:** alternative form of a gene (e.g., for flower color, alleles for purple or white).
  - **Genotype:** allelic combination (e.g., PP, Pp, pp).
  - **Phenotype:** observable trait (purple or white flower).
  - **Homozygous:** two same alleles (PP or pp).
  - **Heterozygous:** two different alleles (Pp).
  - **Dominant allele:** masks recessive allele in heterozygous condition. Conventionally CAPITALIZED.
  - **Recessive allele:** only expressed when homozygous. Conventionally lowercase.
- **Mendel's Laws:**
  - **Law of Segregation:** during gamete formation, alleles separate so each gamete receives one allele per gene.
  - **Law of Independent Assortment:** alleles of different genes assort independently during gamete formation (true for genes on different chromosomes; partially true for genes far apart on same chromosome).
- **Punnett squares:**
  - Use to predict offspring genotypes and phenotypes.
  - **Monohybrid cross:** Pp × Pp → 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp (genotype) → 3 purple : 1 white (phenotype).
  - **Dihybrid cross:** PpYy × PpYy → 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio.
- **Test cross:** breeding an unknown individual with a homozygous recessive to determine the unknown's genotype. If unknown is Pp, half the offspring will be recessive. If unknown is PP, all will be dominant.
- **Non-Mendelian inheritance patterns:**
  - **Incomplete dominance:** heterozygote has intermediate phenotype (e.g., red × white snapdragons → pink heterozygotes).
  - **Codominance:** both alleles expressed in heterozygote (e.g., AB blood type — both A and B antigens present).
  - **Multiple alleles:** more than 2 alleles for a gene (e.g., human ABO blood: alleles I^A, I^B, i).
  - **Polygenic inheritance:** multiple genes contribute to one trait (e.g., human height, skin color).
  - **Pleiotropy:** one gene affects multiple traits (e.g., sickle cell allele affects both hemoglobin and malaria resistance).
  - **Epistasis:** one gene's expression depends on another gene (e.g., coat color in mice — one gene determines whether color is expressed at all, another determines what color).
- **Sex-linked inheritance:**
  - **X-linked recessive:** allele on X chromosome. Females need two copies to express (XX); males need only one (XY).
  - **Result:** X-linked recessive traits more common in males. Females are usually carriers (heterozygous, unaffected) but can transmit to sons.
  - **Examples:** hemophilia, color blindness, Duchenne muscular dystrophy.
- **Pedigrees:** family trees showing inheritance patterns.
  - **Filled = affected; empty = unaffected.**
  - **Circle = female; square = male.**
  - **Use to determine inheritance pattern (autosomal dominant/recessive, sex-linked dominant/recessive).**
- **Chi-square (χ²) test for genetics:**
  - Tests if observed phenotype ratios match expected (e.g., 3:1 ratio).
  - Formula: χ² = Σ ((O − E)² / E).
  - Compare to critical value at appropriate degrees of freedom.
  - If χ² < critical value: observed matches expected (don't reject null).
  - If χ² > critical value: observed differs significantly (reject null).

### Adds for [4]

- **Why crossing over generates variation.** Without crossing over, each chromosome would pass intact to gametes. Genes on the same chromosome would be inherited together (LINKED). Crossing over breaks linkage by exchanging segments between homologous chromosomes, creating new allele combinations.
- **Linkage and recombination frequency:**
  - Genes close together on the same chromosome are LINKED — inherited together.
  - The closer two genes are, the LESS frequently they're separated by crossing over.
  - **Recombination frequency** = (recombinant offspring / total offspring) × 100. Measures distance between linked genes.
  - 1% recombination = 1 map unit (centiMorgan).
- **Independent assortment limits:**
  - Independent assortment is TRUE for genes on different chromosomes.
  - Genes on the SAME chromosome don't assort independently — they're linked.
  - Crossing over can break linkage, but for genes very close together, linkage dominates.
- **Probability and genetics:**
  - **Multiplication rule:** probability of independent events both occurring = p(A) × p(B). Used for "what's the probability of having a child with TWO specific traits?"
  - **Addition rule:** probability of either of two mutually exclusive events occurring = p(A) + p(B). Used for "what's the probability of either AABB or aabb?"
  - **Worked example:** AaBb × AaBb. Probability of offspring being aabb = (1/4) × (1/4) = 1/16.
- **ABO blood type genetics (multiple alleles + codominance):**
  - Alleles: I^A (codominant), I^B (codominant), i (recessive).
  - Genotypes: I^A I^A or I^A i = blood type A; I^B I^B or I^B i = blood type B; I^A I^B = blood type AB (codominance — both expressed); ii = blood type O.
  - Three alleles in population, but each individual has only 2.
- **Pedigree analysis flowchart:**
  - **If trait skips generations:** likely recessive.
  - **If trait appears in every generation:** likely dominant.
  - **If trait affects males more than females:** likely X-linked recessive.
  - **If father affected → daughter always carrier:** could be X-linked dominant or autosomal.
  - **If two unaffected parents have affected child:** trait is recessive.
- **Environmental effects on phenotype:**
  - **Temperature affects coat color in Siamese cats and Himalayan rabbits** (cooler areas → darker fur).
  - **Hydrangea flower color depends on soil pH** (acidic soil → blue; basic → pink).
  - **Identical twins (same DNA) have different fingerprints.**
  - **Phenotype = genotype + environment.**
- **Chi-square example:**
  - Predicted: 3:1 ratio in 80 offspring → expected 60:20.
  - Observed: 65 dominant, 15 recessive.
  - χ² = (65−60)²/60 + (15−20)²/20 = 25/60 + 25/20 = 0.42 + 1.25 = 1.67.
  - df = 1 (n − 1 categories). Critical value at p = 0.05 = 3.84.
  - 1.67 < 3.84, so don't reject null. Observed matches expected.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why meiosis is so essential to evolution.** Without meiosis (and the variation it generates), all offspring would be identical to parents (like cloning). Genetic variation is the substrate on which natural selection acts. Meiosis's two main variation mechanisms — independent assortment and crossing over — combined with random fertilization and mutation, generate the genetic diversity that fuels evolutionary change.
- **The "biological cost" of sexual reproduction.** Sexual reproduction produces 50% of the offspring of asexual reproduction (males don't directly reproduce themselves). So why is sex maintained? Hypotheses:
  - **Recombination produces beneficial allele combinations** that asexual reproduction can't.
  - **Recombination separates beneficial from harmful mutations** (selection can act more efficiently).
  - **Sexual reproduction enables faster adaptation to changing environments** (especially parasites — the "Red Queen" hypothesis).
- **Why X-linked recessive traits are more common in males.** Males have one X chromosome (XY), females have two (XX). For an X-linked recessive trait, a male needs only ONE recessive allele to express it (he has no other X to mask it). A female needs TWO recessive alleles. With recessive alleles at frequency p in the population, expected frequency in males = p, in females = p². For p = 0.1, males express 10%, females only 1%.
- **Polygenic inheritance and continuous variation.** Most human traits (height, skin color, IQ) are polygenic — affected by many genes, each contributing small amounts. Result: continuous variation rather than discrete categories. The combined effect of many genes plus environment produces approximately normal distributions in populations.
- **Why pleiotropy is common.** Genes often encode proteins that participate in multiple pathways. A change in one protein affects all the pathways it's in. Sickle cell anemia is the classic example — the same hemoglobin mutation affects oxygen transport, RBC shape, malaria resistance, blood vessel function. Modern genomics has shown pleiotropy is the rule, not the exception.
- **Genomic imprinting and parent-of-origin effects.** Some genes are expressed differently depending on which parent they came from. Maternal vs paternal alleles can have different effects. Example: Prader-Willi syndrome (paternal deletion) and Angelman syndrome (maternal deletion) involve the same chromosomal region but affect different genes due to imprinting.
- **Mitochondrial inheritance.** Mitochondrial DNA is inherited from mother only (sperm contribute essentially no mitochondria). Mitochondrial diseases follow MATERNAL inheritance pattern — mother passes to all children, but only daughters can pass to grandchildren.
- **Why some traits don't follow Mendel's laws strictly.**
  - **Linkage:** genes close on the same chromosome don't assort independently.
  - **Polygenic inheritance:** continuous variation rather than discrete ratios.
  - **Pleiotropy:** can complicate single-gene analysis.
  - **Epistasis:** masking of one gene by another.
  - **Imprinting and other epigenetic effects:** can produce non-Mendelian patterns.
  - **Mitochondrial inheritance:** completely non-Mendelian (uniparental).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Monohybrid cross

In pea plants, purple flowers (P) is dominant to white (p). A heterozygous purple-flowered plant is crossed with a white-flowered plant.

(a) Set up Punnett square.
(b) Predict genotype and phenotype ratios.

- **(a)** Cross: Pp × pp.

| | p | p |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | Pp | Pp |
| **p** | pp | pp |

- **(b)** Genotype ratio: 2 Pp : 2 pp = 1:1. Phenotype ratio: 2 purple : 2 white = 1:1.

### Example 2 [4] — Dihybrid cross

In humans, brown eyes (B) is dominant to blue (b), and right-handedness (R) is dominant to left-handedness (r). A heterozygous brown-eyed, heterozygous right-handed person mates with another with the same genotype.

(a) Use Punnett square to find phenotype ratios.

- Cross: BbRr × BbRr.
- Each parent produces 4 types of gametes: BR, Br, bR, br (each at 1/4 frequency).
- 16-cell Punnett square gives 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio:
  - 9 brown-right
  - 3 brown-left
  - 3 blue-right
  - 1 blue-left
- **9:3:3:1** is the classic dihybrid cross ratio.

### Example 3 [4] — X-linked inheritance

Hemophilia is X-linked recessive. A carrier female (X^H X^h) marries an unaffected male (X^H Y).

(a) Predict genotypes and phenotypes of offspring.

- Cross: X^H X^h × X^H Y.

| | X^H | Y |
|---|---|---|
| **X^H** | X^H X^H (unaffected female) | X^H Y (unaffected male) |
| **X^h** | X^H X^h (carrier female) | X^h Y (affected male) |

- **Daughters:** 1/2 unaffected (X^H X^H), 1/2 carriers (X^H X^h). NONE affected.
- **Sons:** 1/2 unaffected (X^H Y), 1/2 affected (X^h Y).
- **Why this pattern:** males have only one X, so a single recessive allele determines their phenotype. Females need two recessive alleles to be affected. Carrier mothers transmit to half their sons (who become affected).

### Example 4 [4] — Pedigree analysis

A pedigree shows: affected individuals in every generation, both males and females affected, two affected individuals can have unaffected children.

Determine the inheritance pattern and explain.

- **Trait appears in every generation** → likely DOMINANT (recessive often skips generations).
- **Affected individuals are heterozygous** (since two affected parents can produce unaffected children — must mean they're Aa × Aa, where aa would be unaffected if recessive — but here we see Aa parents producing aa unaffected, suggesting dominant trait where heterozygotes show the trait).
- **Both males and females affected** → likely AUTOSOMAL (not sex-linked, since X-linked dominant would affect daughters of affected fathers more).
- **Conclusion:** AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT inheritance.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Chi-square test

A genetic experiment predicts a 3:1 phenotype ratio. Out of 200 offspring, 140 are dominant and 60 are recessive.

(a) Calculate chi-square.
(b) Determine if results match expected ratio (use critical value of 3.84 at α = 0.05, df = 1).

- **(a)** Expected: 200 × 3/4 = 150 dominant; 200 × 1/4 = 50 recessive.
- χ² = (140−150)²/150 + (60−50)²/50 = 100/150 + 100/50 = 0.67 + 2.00 = 2.67.
- **(b)** χ² = 2.67 < 3.84 (critical value at p = 0.05, df = 1).
- **Conclusion:** observed ratio is NOT significantly different from expected. Do not reject the null hypothesis (3:1 ratio).
- **Interpretation:** the observed numbers (140:60) are consistent with a 3:1 expected ratio. Differences are within range of chance variation.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing mitosis and meiosis.** Mitosis: 1 division, 2 daughters, identical to parent. Meiosis: 2 divisions, 4 daughters, genetically diverse, half the chromosomes.
2. **Misordering meiosis stages.** Meiosis I (PMAT I, separates HOMOLOGS), Meiosis II (PMAT II, separates SISTER CHROMATIDS).
3. **Forgetting crossing over occurs in PROPHASE I.** Not anywhere else.
4. **Forgetting independent assortment occurs in METAPHASE I.** Random orientation of homolog pairs.
5. **Confusing sister chromatids with homologous chromosomes.** Sister chromatids: identical copies attached at centromere (after DNA replication). Homologous chromosomes: matching pairs from each parent.
6. **Confusing genotype and phenotype.** Genotype = allele combination. Phenotype = observable trait.
7. **Confusing homozygous and heterozygous.** Homozygous = same alleles (BB or bb). Heterozygous = different alleles (Bb).
8. **Wrong Punnett square setup.** Each parent's gametes go on TOP and SIDE; offspring genotypes in cells.
9. **Calculating wrong probability for combined events.** Multiplication rule for AND (both events). Addition rule for OR (either event).
10. **Confusing incomplete dominance and codominance.** Incomplete: blending (red + white = pink). Codominant: both expressed (AB blood = both A and B antigens).
11. **Treating multiple alleles as multiple genes.** Multiple alleles = many alternative forms of ONE gene (each individual has only 2 alleles).
12. **Forgetting that linked genes don't assort independently.** Genes on the same chromosome are LINKED.
13. **Confusing X-linked recessive in males vs females.** Males: one X allele determines phenotype. Females: need two recessive alleles to express trait.
14. **Wrong chi-square formula.** χ² = Σ ((O − E)² / E). Sum across all categories.
15. **Ignoring environmental effects.** Phenotype depends on genotype AND environment. Identical genes don't always produce identical phenotypes.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For Punnett square problems:**
- Identify alleles and their dominance relationships.
- Set up the cross properly.
- Calculate genotype and phenotype ratios.

**For probability calculations:**
- Use multiplication rule for AND.
- Use addition rule for OR (mutually exclusive).
- Apply correctly to multi-trait crosses.

**For pedigree analysis:**
- Look at trait expression patterns (every generation? skipping? affecting which sex?).
- Determine dominant vs recessive.
- Determine autosomal vs sex-linked.

**For chi-square:**
- Calculate observed vs expected.
- Apply formula χ² = Σ ((O − E)² / E).
- Compare to critical value at appropriate df.

**For meiosis questions:**
- Identify the stage by features (homologs paired, separation, etc.).
- Distinguish Meiosis I from Meiosis II.
- Identify sources of genetic variation.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Meiosis produces 4 genetically diverse haploid gametes through TWO sources of variation: INDEPENDENT ASSORTMENT during metaphase I (random orientation of homolog pairs) and CROSSING OVER during prophase I (exchange of segments between homologous chromosomes)."
2. "In the cross [Pp × Pp], the expected genotype ratio is 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp, producing the phenotype ratio 3 dominant : 1 recessive."
3. "X-linked recessive traits are more common in males because males have only one X chromosome (XY); a single recessive allele determines their phenotype. Females (XX) need two recessive alleles to express the trait."
4. "Codominance is shown when both alleles are equally expressed in the heterozygote (such as AB blood type, where both A and B antigens are present). This differs from incomplete dominance, where the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype (such as pink flowers from red × white snapdragons)."
5. "Pedigree analysis reveals an autosomal recessive pattern when (1) trait skips generations, (2) two unaffected parents have affected children, and (3) the trait affects males and females equally."
6. "Chi-square testing compares observed phenotype ratios to expected ratios. χ² = Σ ((O − E)² / E). If χ² is less than the critical value at the appropriate degrees of freedom, observed and expected ratios are not significantly different."
7. "Phenotype results from both genotype and environmental factors. Even individuals with identical genotypes (such as identical twins) can show different phenotypes when raised in different environments. Examples include hydrangea flower color (depends on soil pH) and Siamese cat coat color (cooler areas darker)."
