## Big Ideas

1. **Progressive Era reformers responded to Gilded Age problems by expanding government — but with significant racial limits.** Muckraking journalism, regulatory legislation (Pure Food and Drug Act, Meat Inspection Act, Federal Reserve, Clayton Antitrust), women's suffrage (19th Amendment, 1920), and direct democracy (initiative, referendum, recall) all expanded democratic capacity and federal authority. But Progressivism largely accepted Jim Crow and immigration restriction; "progress" was for white Americans.
2. **The Spanish-American War (1898) launched the US as a global imperial power.** Acquisition of Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam, Cuba (as protectorate), Hawaii (1898), and informal control over the Caribbean fundamentally redefined US foreign policy. The anti-imperialist debate (Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie) lost; the imperialist consensus dominated through 1920.
3. **WWI transformed both American power and American society.** Federal government expanded dramatically (war boards, draft, propaganda, wartime restrictions on speech). The war's failed peace (Versailles Treaty, US rejection of League of Nations) led to the interwar isolationism that delayed US response to fascism.
4. **The Great Depression destroyed faith in laissez-faire and made the federal government the central economic actor.** New Deal's "alphabet agencies," Social Security, federal labor protection, and federal banking regulation transformed the American state. The size and reach of the federal government in 1945 was unrecognizable from 1929.
5. **WWII completed the transformation begun by the New Deal — the federal government became the dominant force in American life.** Total mobilization (15 million in armed services, total industrial conversion, top tax rates of 94%), centralized economic planning, and the postwar GI Bill institutionalized federal involvement in employment, education, and housing. The US emerged as the dominant global economic and military power.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Spanish-American War (1898):**
  - **Causes:** Cuban independence struggle against Spain (José Martí), yellow journalism (Hearst, Pulitzer) sensationalizing Spanish atrocities, USS *Maine* explosion in Havana Harbor (February 1898) blamed on Spain, US economic interests in Cuban sugar.
  - **War:** brief (April–August 1898). Major victories: Manila Bay (Commodore Dewey, Philippines), Battle of San Juan Hill (Theodore Roosevelt's Rough Riders, Cuba).
  - **Treaty of Paris (1898):** Spain ceded Philippines, Guam, Puerto Rico to US for $20 million; Cuba became independent (under US protectorate).
  - **Anti-Imperialist League** opposed US acquisition of Philippines. Members included Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, William Jennings Bryan, Jane Addams. Lost the political battle.
  - **Philippine-American War (1899–1902):** Filipinos under Emilio Aguinaldo fought US occupation. Brutal counterinsurgency campaign; ~4,200 US deaths, estimated 200,000+ Filipino deaths (combat, disease, famine).
- **Other turn-of-the-century imperial actions:**
  - **Annexation of Hawaii (1898)** — pineapple/sugar planters had overthrown Queen Liliuokalani in 1893 with US Marine support; US formally annexed.
  - **Open Door Notes (1899, 1900)** — Secretary of State John Hay urged European powers to maintain free trade access to China against carving China into exclusive spheres.
  - **Roosevelt Corollary (1904)** — extension of Monroe Doctrine. Asserted US right to intervene in Latin American countries to prevent European intervention. Used to justify multiple US occupations (Dominican Republic 1905–1907, Haiti 1915–1934).
  - **Panama Canal (built 1904–1914)** — TR engineered Panama's secession from Colombia, then negotiated treaty with new Panamanian government granting US sovereign control over canal zone.
  - **Big Stick / Dollar Diplomacy / Moral Diplomacy** — three early-20th-century US foreign policy doctrines:
    - Theodore Roosevelt: "Big Stick" (military force backing diplomacy).
    - Taft: "Dollar Diplomacy" (economic investment as foreign policy tool).
    - Wilson: "Moral Diplomacy" (US would only support democracy and self-determination, in theory).
- **Progressive Era (~1900–1920):**
  - **Muckrakers** — investigative journalists exposing corruption and abuse:
    - Ida Tarbell, *The History of the Standard Oil Company* (1904) — exposed Rockefeller's monopolistic practices.
    - Upton Sinclair, *The Jungle* (1906) — exposed meatpacking industry; led to Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection Act.
    - Jacob Riis, *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) — photographed New York slums; influenced housing reform.
    - Lincoln Steffens, *The Shame of the Cities* (1904) — exposed urban political corruption.
    - Jane Addams's settlement house movement also functioned as muckraking by exposing immigrant urban conditions.
  - **Theodore Roosevelt's Square Deal (1901–1909):**
    - Trust-busting: prosecuted Northern Securities Company (1904), Standard Oil (eventually broken up 1911).
    - **Pure Food and Drug Act (1906)** and **Meat Inspection Act (1906)** — federal regulation of food and drug safety (responding to *The Jungle*).
    - Conservation: established 5 national parks, 18 national monuments, 51 wildlife refuges. Worked with Gifford Pinchot (US Forest Service).
    - **Hepburn Act (1906)** — strengthened Interstate Commerce Commission's authority over railroad rates.
  - **Taft's presidency (1909–1913):**
    - Pursued more antitrust prosecutions than TR (90 vs 45).
    - Split with TR over conservation (Ballinger-Pinchot affair) and tariffs.
    - **16th Amendment (1913)** — federal income tax (proposed under Taft, ratified under Wilson).
    - **17th Amendment (1913)** — direct election of senators (proposed under Taft, ratified under Wilson).
  - **Wilson's New Freedom (1913–1921):**
    - **Underwood Tariff (1913)** — significantly reduced tariff rates; offset by income tax revenue.
    - **Federal Reserve Act (1913)** — created Federal Reserve System (12 regional banks under federal board) to provide elastic currency and banking regulation.
    - **Clayton Antitrust Act (1914)** — strengthened Sherman Act; explicitly exempted labor unions from antitrust prosecution; banned interlocking directorates.
    - **Federal Trade Commission (FTC, 1914)** — regulated business practices.
- **Constitutional Amendments of the Progressive Era:**
  - **16th Amendment (1913)** — federal income tax.
  - **17th Amendment (1913)** — direct election of senators.
  - **18th Amendment (1919)** — Prohibition (banned manufacture/sale of alcohol).
  - **19th Amendment (1920)** — women's suffrage.
- **Women's suffrage movement:**
  - **NAWSA (National American Woman Suffrage Association)** — Carrie Chapman Catt led after 1900. Pursued state-by-state strategy alongside federal amendment campaign.
  - **National Woman's Party (Alice Paul, 1916)** — more militant tactics: pickets at White House, hunger strikes when arrested.
  - **19th Amendment (1920)** — "The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex."
  - **Disenfranchisement of Black women** continued in the South under poll taxes, literacy tests (which the 19th Amendment didn't address). Black women won the right to vote in name; Southern Black women effectively gained the vote only with the Voting Rights Act (1965).
- **WWI (1914–1918, US involvement 1917–1918):**
  - **Causes of war (Europe):** alliance system, militarism, imperialism, nationalism. Triggered by assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand (June 1914).
  - **US neutrality (1914–1917):** Wilson urged Americans to be "neutral in fact as well as in name." Significant US trade with Allied powers (especially Britain) made true neutrality impossible.
  - **Causes of US entry:**
    - **German unrestricted submarine warfare** — *Lusitania* sinking (May 1915, 128 Americans killed); resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare (February 1917).
    - **Zimmerman Telegram (March 1917)** — German Foreign Minister proposed Mexican alliance against US, offering return of Texas/New Mexico/Arizona.
    - Wilson's commitment to "make the world safe for democracy."
    - US economic interest in Allied victory (huge loans to Britain/France that would be lost if Allies lost).
  - **US declared war (April 1917).** **Selective Service Act (1917)** drafted ~2.8 million men; ~2 million served in Europe.
  - **Home front:**
    - **Committee on Public Information (Creel Committee)** — federal propaganda agency, produced posters, films, "Four Minute Men" speeches.
    - **War Industries Board** — coordinated industrial production.
    - **Espionage Act (1917)** and **Sedition Act (1918)** — criminalized criticism of government, military, war effort. Eugene V. Debs sentenced to 10 years for anti-war speech (released by Harding 1921).
    - **Schenck v. United States (1919)** — Supreme Court upheld Espionage Act convictions; established "clear and present danger" test for restricting free speech.
  - **Women in workforce** filled jobs men left for military service. Helped secure ratification of 19th Amendment.
  - **Great Migration** of Black Southerners to Northern industrial cities began during WWI (continued through 1970). ~6 million Black Americans migrated north.
- **Versailles Treaty (1919) and Wilson's Fourteen Points:**
  - **Wilson's Fourteen Points** (January 1918) — included open diplomacy, freedom of seas, self-determination, League of Nations.
  - **Treaty of Versailles** harsh on Germany — territorial losses, war guilt clause (Article 231), reparations, military restrictions.
  - **League of Nations** — collective security organization. Wilson considered it the most important Fourteen Point.
  - **US Senate rejected** Treaty (and League membership) — opposition led by Henry Cabot Lodge (Republican). Reservations about Article X (collective security obligation) undermined ratification.
  - **Wilson's stroke** (October 1919) during cross-country campaign for League undermined his ability to negotiate compromises. Treaty failed.
  - US made separate peace with Germany (1921). Did not join League. Marked beginning of interwar isolationism.
- **1920s — "Roaring Twenties":**
  - **Republican presidencies** (Harding 1921–1923, Coolidge 1923–1929, Hoover 1929–1933). Pro-business, low taxes, high tariffs (Fordney-McCumber 1922).
  - **Economic boom** — automobile industry (Ford Model T, assembly line), radio, electric appliances, advertising, consumer credit. GDP grew rapidly until 1929.
  - **Cultural flowering:**
    - **Harlem Renaissance** — Black artistic and literary movement. Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Duke Ellington, Louis Armstrong. Marcus Garvey's UNIA (Universal Negro Improvement Association) advocated Black pride and "Back to Africa" movement.
    - **"Lost Generation"** — disillusioned post-WWI writers. F. Scott Fitzgerald (*The Great Gatsby*, 1925), Ernest Hemingway, Gertrude Stein.
    - **New Woman / "Flapper"** — short hair, short dresses, jazz, public smoking and drinking. Symbol of changing gender norms.
- **Cultural conflicts of the 1920s:**
  - **Prohibition (18th Amendment, 1919)** — banned alcohol. Resulted in widespread bootlegging, organized crime (Al Capone), speakeasies. **21st Amendment (1933)** repealed Prohibition — the only Constitutional amendment to repeal an earlier amendment.
  - **Immigration restriction:**
    - **Emergency Quota Act (1921)** — set immigration quotas at 3% of each nationality's 1910 US population.
    - **National Origins Act / Johnson-Reed Act (1924)** — set quotas at 2% of each nationality's 1890 US population (the year used to favor Northern/Western European immigration over Southern/Eastern). Banned all Asian immigration. Marked end of mass European immigration that had defined US since 1880.
  - **Scopes "Monkey" Trial (1925)** — John Scopes prosecuted in Tennessee for teaching evolution. William Jennings Bryan (prosecution) vs Clarence Darrow (defense). Scopes convicted but verdict overturned on technicality. Symbolized urban-rural, modernist-fundamentalist divide.
  - **Second KKK** — revived 1915, peaked at ~4 million members in mid-1920s. Anti-Black, anti-Catholic, anti-Jewish, anti-immigrant. Strong in Indiana, Oregon, other non-Southern states. Declined after 1925 corruption scandals.
  - **Sacco and Vanzetti case (1920–1927)** — two Italian anarchist immigrants convicted (controversially) of murder. Executed 1927. Symbolized nativist xenophobia and Red Scare anxieties.
- **First Red Scare (1919–1920):**
  - Bombings (mailed to government officials), strikes (Boston police strike, Seattle General Strike, steel strike), Russian Revolution (1917) inspired anti-radical hysteria.
  - **Palmer Raids (1919–1920)** — Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer organized mass arrests and deportations of suspected radicals (Emma Goldman among them).
- **Great Depression (1929–~1939):**
  - **Stock Market Crash (October 1929)** — Black Tuesday October 29. Caused by speculation, margin trading, weak banking, agricultural distress. Crash didn't directly cause Depression but signaled and accelerated it.
  - **Banking failures** — ~9,000 bank failures 1930–1933. People lost savings; lack of deposit insurance.
  - **Unemployment** peaked at ~25% (1932–1933).
  - **Hooverville** shanty-towns appeared in cities (named sardonically after President Hoover, blamed for inadequate response).
  - **Hoover's response** — believed in voluntary action, limited federal intervention. **Reconstruction Finance Corporation (1932)** — federal lending to banks and businesses. Too little, too late. Hoover viewed as out of touch.
  - **Bonus Army (1932)** — WWI veterans demanded early payment of promised bonuses; encamped in DC. Hoover ordered Army (under MacArthur) to evict them. Public relations disaster for Hoover.
- **New Deal (1933–~1938):**
  - **FDR elected (1932)** in landslide. Took office March 1933 (last March 4 inauguration; **20th Amendment** moved to January 20).
  - **First Hundred Days (March–June 1933)** — burst of legislation:
    - **Emergency Banking Act** — temporary closure of banks; reopening after federal inspection restored confidence.
    - **Glass-Steagall Act** — separated commercial and investment banking; created **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)** insuring deposits.
    - **Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC)** — employed young men in conservation work (planted billions of trees).
    - **Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA)** — federal hydroelectric and economic development for Tennessee River valley.
    - **National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA)** — created NRA (National Recovery Administration) to set industry codes; created Public Works Administration. Supreme Court struck down 1935.
    - **Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA)** — paid farmers to reduce production to raise prices. Supreme Court struck down 1936; reauthorized 1938.
  - **Second New Deal (1935–1936):**
    - **Social Security Act (1935)** — old-age pensions, unemployment insurance, aid to dependent children. Most enduring New Deal program.
    - **National Labor Relations Act / Wagner Act (1935)** — guaranteed workers' right to unionize, collective bargaining. Created National Labor Relations Board (NLRB).
    - **Works Progress Administration (WPA, 1935)** — massive federal employment program (8.5 million workers). Public works, art projects, writers' projects.
    - **Fair Labor Standards Act (1938)** — minimum wage, maximum 44-hour week (eventually 40-hour), banned child labor under 16.
  - **Court-Packing Plan (1937)** — FDR proposed adding Supreme Court justices to overcome Court's hostility to New Deal. Rejected by Congress; politically damaging. But Court reversed course (the "switch in time that saved nine") and began upholding New Deal legislation.
  - **Recession of 1937–1938** ("Roosevelt Recession") — FDR cut spending too soon; economy worsened. Restored Keynesian-style spending.
  - **Eleanor Roosevelt** — most active First Lady to that point. Advocated for women, civil rights, labor. Daniel Inouye later called her "the most influential first lady."
- **Critics of the New Deal:**
  - **Right** — accused FDR of socialism, federal overreach. American Liberty League (founded 1934).
  - **Left** — accused FDR of insufficient redistribution. **Huey Long** (Louisiana senator) "Share Our Wealth" program. Father Charles Coughlin (radio priest), eventually anti-Semitic. Dr. Francis Townsend (old-age pensions).
  - **Black critics** — many New Deal programs (Social Security, Wagner Act) excluded agricultural and domestic workers, where most Black workers were concentrated. New Deal didn't directly challenge Jim Crow.
- **Interwar Foreign Policy:**
  - **Washington Naval Conference (1921–1922)** — limited naval armaments among major powers.
  - **Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928)** — outlawed war as instrument of national policy. Signed by 62 nations; toothless.
  - **Good Neighbor Policy** (FDR) — abandoned formal interventionism in Latin America.
  - **Neutrality Acts (1935, 1936, 1937)** — banned arms sales to belligerents; mandated arms embargoes.
  - **Lend-Lease Act (March 1941)** — allowed FDR to "lend" military equipment to Britain (and later USSR). Effectively ended formal neutrality.
- **WWII (1939–1945, US involvement 1941–1945):**
  - **European theater:** Germany invaded Poland (September 1, 1939), starting WWII. France fell June 1940. Britain alone until USSR entry (June 1941) and US entry (December 1941).
  - **Pacific theater:** Japan invaded China (1937); occupied French Indochina (1940). Pearl Harbor attack (December 7, 1941) brought US into war.
  - **US declared war on Japan** (December 8, 1941); Germany and Italy declared war on US (December 11, 1941).
  - **Mobilization:**
    - 16 million men and women served in armed forces.
    - Federal spending grew from $9 billion (1940) to $98 billion (1944).
    - **War Production Board** coordinated industrial conversion.
    - **Office of Price Administration (OPA)** — rationing, price controls.
    - Top marginal tax rate: 94% (incomes over $200,000).
  - **Women in workforce** ("Rosie the Riveter") — ~6 million entered industrial workforce. Most pushed out after 1945 to make room for returning veterans.
  - **Black "Double V" campaign** — victory against fascism abroad AND racism at home. **A. Philip Randolph** threatened March on Washington (1941); FDR responded with **Executive Order 8802** banning racial discrimination in defense industries (creating Fair Employment Practices Committee).
  - **Japanese American internment (1942–1945)** — **Executive Order 9066** (FDR, February 1942) authorized military to designate "exclusion zones." ~120,000 Japanese Americans (2/3 US citizens) forcibly relocated to internment camps in interior West. **Korematsu v. United States (1944)** — Supreme Court upheld constitutionality (since formally repudiated; reparations paid 1988 under Reagan).
  - **Major battles / events:**
    - **Battle of Midway (June 1942)** — turning point in Pacific; US Navy destroyed 4 Japanese aircraft carriers.
    - **D-Day (June 6, 1944)** — Allied invasion of Normandy. Largest amphibious invasion in history.
    - **Battle of the Bulge (December 1944–January 1945)** — final major German offensive; failed.
    - **VE Day (May 8, 1945)** — Germany surrendered.
    - **Manhattan Project** — secret US program to develop atomic bomb. Cost $2 billion (~$30 billion today). Developed under leadership of J. Robert Oppenheimer at Los Alamos.
    - **Atomic bombings: Hiroshima (August 6, 1945) and Nagasaki (August 9, 1945)** — killed ~150,000–250,000 immediately and from radiation. Japan surrendered August 15, 1945; **VJ Day September 2, 1945**.
- **Holocaust** — Nazi genocide of 6 million Jews (and millions of others — Roma, disabled, Polish/Slavic peoples, political prisoners, gay men). US response inadequate — turned away refugees (*St. Louis* incident 1939), didn't bomb rail lines to Auschwitz despite intelligence. War Refugee Board established 1944, late.
- **Postwar settlements:**
  - **Yalta Conference (February 1945)** — FDR, Churchill, Stalin discussed postwar Europe. Stalin agreed to free elections in Eastern Europe (later violated).
  - **Potsdam Conference (July–August 1945)** — Truman, Churchill (then Attlee), Stalin. Set occupation zones in Germany.
  - **United Nations** founded (April–June 1945, San Francisco). Successor to failed League of Nations. US Senate ratified (this time).
  - **Bretton Woods (1944)** — established postwar international monetary system; created International Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why Progressivism succeeded where Populism failed.** Multiple factors:
  - Populism was rural and Southern/Western; Progressivism added urban middle-class Northeastern reformers.
  - Populism challenged the major parties (third-party run); Progressives worked within both major parties (TR Republican, Wilson Democrat).
  - Populism's economic radicalism (free silver, federal railroad ownership) was scarier to elites; Progressivism's reforms were more moderate (regulation, not nationalization).
  - Populism's race/immigration politics were inconsistent; Progressivism mostly accepted Jim Crow and immigration restriction (limiting opposition).
- **Progressivism's racial limits.** Progressive achievements were mostly for white Americans:
  - Wilson segregated federal government (1913).
  - Birth of a Nation (1915) film celebrated KKK; screened at White House.
  - Many Progressive leaders supported eugenics (Wilson, Margaret Sanger early career, TR).
  - Black thinkers had to work AROUND mainstream Progressivism — Du Bois, Ida B. Wells, James Weldon Johnson, Mary Church Terrell.
  - **NAACP (1909)** — founded by W.E.B. Du Bois, Ida B. Wells, others. Pursued legal challenges to discrimination. Long-term strategy of using federal courts and 14th Amendment to dismantle Jim Crow.
- **The Anti-Imperialist debate (1898–1900) in detail.** Imperialists (Theodore Roosevelt, Henry Cabot Lodge, Albert Beveridge) argued:
  - US needed colonies for naval bases, markets, raw materials.
  - "White man's burden" (Kipling) to civilize "lesser" peoples.
  - National greatness required overseas expansion.
  - Anti-imperialists (Twain, Carnegie, Bryan, Jane Addams) argued:
  - Colonies violated American principles of self-government and consent of governed.
  - Hypocritical to declare independence from one empire and become another.
  - Constitutional questions about governing colonies.
  - Costs of empire would outweigh benefits.
  - Insular Cases (1901–1922) ruled the Constitution didn't fully apply to acquired territories.
- **Wilson's contradictions.** Wilson talked self-determination at Versailles but ignored colonial peoples (Vietnamese delegation including young Ho Chi Minh tried to meet Wilson, was ignored). Wilson talked democracy but segregated the federal government. Wilson talked peace but signed the Espionage Act and presided over Palmer Raids. The contradictions undercut both his idealism and the postwar order he tried to build.
- **The Great Migration's impact.** Approximately 6 million Black Americans moved from South to North 1910–1970. WWI launched the first wave (~1.6 million 1910–1940); WWII launched the second (~5 million 1940–1970). Created Black Northern political constituencies (Chicago, Detroit, New York, Cleveland), urban Black culture (Harlem Renaissance, Chicago blues, Detroit Motown), and shifted Civil Rights Movement's geographic and political base. Also produced new conflicts as Black migrants competed with white workers and immigrants in Northern cities (race riots — East St. Louis 1917, Chicago 1919, Tulsa 1921, Detroit 1943).
- **Why the 1920s economy collapsed.** Multiple causes:
  - Agricultural overproduction (postwar) → falling crop prices → rural debt.
  - Industrial overproduction outstripped consumer purchasing power (despite installment credit).
  - Wealth concentration limited consumption (top 0.1% had as much income as bottom 42%).
  - Stock market speculation (margin trading) created bubbles.
  - Banking system was fragmented and unregulated.
  - International trade collapsed under high tariffs (Smoot-Hawley 1930 made it worse).
  - Monetary policy (Federal Reserve) reduced money supply at exactly the wrong moment.
- **The New Deal's enduring transformations.** The "alphabet agencies" represent only the surface; the deeper transformation was:
  - Federal economic intervention as norm (vs Hoover's voluntarism).
  - Federal labor protection (Wagner Act, FLSA).
  - Federal social insurance (Social Security Act).
  - Federal banking regulation (FDIC, SEC, Glass-Steagall).
  - Federal infrastructure investment (TVA, WPA, PWA).
  - Many of these survived intact to 2026; some were deregulated in 1980s–2000s; the political-economic framework FDR established remained dominant from 1933 to ~1980.
- **The New Deal's racial limits.** Many New Deal programs were structured to preserve Southern Jim Crow:
  - Social Security and Wagner Act excluded agricultural and domestic workers (where most Black workers were concentrated).
  - AAA payments often went to white landowners, who then evicted Black sharecroppers.
  - CCC and federal housing programs were segregated.
  - FDR refused to support federal anti-lynching legislation, fearing loss of Southern Democratic votes.
  - But: Eleanor Roosevelt, Mary McLeod Bethune (Black Cabinet), and FDR's appointment of Black officials began shifting the Democratic Party toward civil rights — laying groundwork for the 1960s breakthrough.
- **Japanese American internment specifics.** EO 9066 affected ~120,000 Japanese Americans (more than 70,000 US citizens) on the West Coast. Held in 10 internment camps in interior West (Manzanar, Tule Lake, Heart Mountain, etc.). Property losses estimated at $1.3-3 billion (current dollars, lower bound). 442nd Regimental Combat Team — Japanese American unit — became one of the most decorated in US military history while their families were imprisoned. Hawaii had 150,000 Japanese Americans (1/3 of population) but did NOT intern them — too central to the labor force. Mainland internment was based on racial fears, not security need. Korematsu v. United States (1944) upheld; Civil Liberties Act of 1988 (Reagan) issued formal apology and $20,000 reparations to surviving internees.
- **The decision to use atomic bombs** — historiographical debate:
  - Standard view (post-1945): bombs ended the war quickly, saved hundreds of thousands of US lives that an invasion of Japan would have cost.
  - Revisionist view (Gar Alperovitz): Japan was already on verge of surrender; bombs were primarily aimed at intimidating the Soviet Union and shaping postwar order.
  - Most current historians: a combination — military pressure to end the war + diplomatic signaling to USSR + bureaucratic momentum + lack of serious internal debate after the bombs were ready. Truman likely didn't fully grasp what he authorized.
  - Hiroshima killed ~70,000 immediately (140,000 by year end from radiation). Nagasaki killed ~40,000 immediately (74,000 by year end).

### Adds for [5]

- **The "Long Progressive Era" framing.** Some historians extend Progressivism beyond 1920 to encompass continuities into the New Deal — federal expansion, regulatory state, social legislation. The 1920s "Republican normalcy" represented a temporary pause rather than reversal. From this view, FDR's New Deal completed what TR and Wilson started — making the federal government the central economic actor. Sophistication move: avoid treating Progressivism as a discrete 1900–1920 episode; trace continuities into the 1930s.
- **The "Garveyism vs Du Boisism" debate** within Black politics. Marcus Garvey's UNIA emphasized Black pride, economic self-reliance, and emigration to Africa — appealing to working-class Black Americans. Du Bois's NAACP emphasized integration, civil rights litigation, and elite leadership ("Talented Tenth"). The two movements offered competing visions of Black liberation strategy. Garvey's movement collapsed after his 1922 mail fraud conviction and 1927 deportation, but Garveyism's ideological influence persisted (Black Power movement of the 1960s drew on Garveyite themes).
- **Wilson and the failed promise of self-determination.** Wilson's Fourteen Points and self-determination rhetoric raised expectations among colonized peoples worldwide. The Versailles Treaty disappointed virtually all of them — colonial peoples got nothing; the principle of self-determination applied only to former Austro-Hungarian territories. Wilson refused even to discuss racial equality at Versailles (rejecting a Japanese proposal). The disappointment radicalized colonial peoples — Ho Chi Minh, Mao Zedong, and Indian nationalists were among those who turned from Wilsonian liberalism to Marxism after 1919. The failed peace contributed not only to WWII but to the wave of decolonization after WWII.
- **Why isolationism dominated 1920s–1930s.** Multiple reasons:
  - Disillusionment with WWI's failed peace.
  - Senate rejection of Versailles (Article X collective security).
  - Republican administrations preferred unilateralism.
  - Public opinion wanted no more European wars.
  - Nye Committee (1934–1936) blamed WWI on "merchants of death" (arms manufacturers and bankers).
  - Neutrality Acts (1935, 1936, 1937) institutionalized isolationism into law.
  - Pearl Harbor finally broke isolationism; even after Hitler dominated Europe (1940), most Americans wanted to stay out.
- **The contradiction of fighting for freedom while interning Japanese Americans.** WWII's "war for freedom" rhetoric (Four Freedoms speech 1941, war propaganda) was contradicted by:
  - Japanese American internment.
  - Continued Jim Crow segregation in armed forces and at home.
  - Continued legal discrimination against Asians (Chinese Exclusion repealed 1943; broader Asian immigration ban not lifted until 1965).
  - The "Double V" campaign (Black press) explicitly pointed out this contradiction.
  - The contradiction became the foundation for the postwar civil rights movement — having fought fascism abroad, Black soldiers (and others) returned demanding domestic civil rights.
- **The historiographical debate on the New Deal's effects.** Did the New Deal end the Depression? No, WWII did — government spending in 1942–1944 dwarfed New Deal spending. But the New Deal:
  - Stabilized banks (FDIC).
  - Reformed financial markets (SEC).
  - Provided immediate relief (CCC, WPA).
  - Created lasting social safety net (Social Security).
  - Established federal labor protection.
  - Restructured agriculture (AAA).
  - Built infrastructure (TVA, dams, roads).
  - Saved democratic capitalism by demonstrating that government could mitigate capitalism's worst failures.
- **The "Liberal Consensus" framework.** Post-WWII US politics was dominated (until ~1980) by a consensus accepting:
  - Federal regulation of the economy.
  - Social Security and other federal social insurance.
  - Federal protection of labor rights.
  - Active foreign policy (vs prewar isolationism).
  - This consensus crossed party lines (Eisenhower expanded Social Security; Nixon created EPA). It was disrupted by the 1980s Reagan revolution. The New Deal created the political-economic framework within which both parties operated for nearly 50 years.
- **Why the atomic bomb decision matters historiographically.** It marked the moment the US became the dominant global military power AND the moment when the destructive capacity of state warfare reached qualitatively new levels. The Cold War nuclear standoff, the proliferation problem (Israel, India, Pakistan, North Korea), and contemporary AI/autonomous weapons debates all flow from August 1945. The historiographical dispute (military necessity vs Soviet signaling) hasn't been resolved; AP-quality essays should acknowledge the debate rather than asserting one side.

## Worked Examples (Thesis & DBQ-Style Argumentation)

### Example 1 [3] — Progressive Era LEQ thesis

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which the Progressive Era (1900–1920) succeeded in addressing the problems created by industrialization.

- **Acceptable thesis [3]:** "The Progressive Era partially succeeded by passing reforms like the Pure Food and Drug Act and women's suffrage, but it failed to address racism and many problems remained."
- **Strong thesis [4–5]:** "The Progressive Era achieved substantial success in addressing industrial-era problems through expanded federal regulation (Pure Food and Drug Act 1906, Clayton Antitrust 1914, Federal Reserve Act 1913), expanded democratic participation (16th, 17th, 19th Amendments), and reform of municipal government — fundamentally redefining the federal government's economic role and laying groundwork for the New Deal. But Progressive reforms were largely organized by and for white middle-class Americans; the era's accomplishments coexisted with the consolidation of Jim Crow, federal segregation under Wilson, immigration restriction movements, and the 1920s eugenics consensus. Progressivism's racial limits were not incidental but constitutive — its political success often depended on accepting Jim Crow's exclusion of Black voters from any reform coalition."
- **Why it works:** identifies specific Progressive achievements with dates, identifies specific limits (Jim Crow consolidation, Wilson segregation, immigration restriction), uses the analytical move that limits weren't accidental but structural, and connects forward to the New Deal as continuation.

### Example 2 [3][4] — New Deal SAQ

**Prompt:** Identify and explain ONE specific way the New Deal transformed the relationship between the federal government and the American people.

- **Acceptable answer [3]:** "The New Deal created Social Security, which gave old-age pensions and unemployment insurance to many Americans. This made the federal government responsible for people's economic well-being for the first time."
- **Strong answer [4]:** "The Social Security Act of 1935 transformed the relationship between the federal government and individual Americans by establishing direct federal responsibility for old-age pensions, unemployment insurance, and aid to dependent children. Before Social Security, retirement security had been the responsibility of families, employers, or local charity; afterward, it became a federal entitlement based on payroll tax contributions. The program structurally embedded federal economic obligation into Americans' working and retirement lives — and crucially, it has survived continuously to the present (more than 90 years), proving more politically durable than any other major New Deal program. Its enduring appeal across political coalitions reflects the deeper transformation: the New Deal made federal economic responsibility a permanent feature of American politics rather than an exceptional emergency response."
- **Why it earns the point:** specific program (Social Security Act 1935), specific provisions (old-age, unemployment, ADC), specific causal mechanism (replacing family/charity with federal entitlement), specific long-term consequence (90+ year durability), connects to broader transformation.

### Example 3 [4] — HIPP-style sourcing of Wilson's Fourteen Points

**Document:** Excerpt from Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points address to Congress (January 8, 1918).

- **Historical situation:** Delivered nine months after US entry into WWI. Russia had withdrawn from the war (Bolshevik Revolution, October 1917; Brest-Litovsk treaty pending). The war was stalemated; American troops were just beginning to arrive in significant numbers in Europe. Wilson sought to give the war moral purpose beyond defeating Germany.
- **Intended audience:** Multiple — (1) US Congress and public, justifying continued American participation; (2) European Allied governments, attempting to set the postwar agenda; (3) German people (over the heads of their militaristic government), encouraging them to seek peace on principled terms; (4) colonial peoples and small nations worldwide, raising the prospect of self-determination.
- **Point of view:** Wilson, a former Princeton president and political scientist, fused Protestant moralism with academic political science. He believed in collective security, open diplomacy, and free trade. His worldview was deeply ethnocentric — "self-determination" was for European peoples, not African, Asian, or African American.
- **Purpose:** Strategic and moral — establish American war aims as more idealistic than Allied war aims (which focused on territorial gains and German weakening), give the war a moral framework that could justify continued sacrifice, and shape the postwar settlement in advance of any peace conference.
- **Sophisticated extension:** the Fourteen Points raised expectations Wilson couldn't fulfill at Versailles. The Treaty of Versailles violated many points — colonial peoples got nothing; "self-determination" applied selectively; harsh terms imposed on Germany. The disappointment radicalized colonial peoples (Ho Chi Minh, Mao, Indian nationalists who had hoped Wilsonian liberalism would deliver independence). Wilson's failure to deliver on his rhetoric helped delegitimize the postwar order before it was even built.

### Example 4 [4][5] — Compare Old and New Deal coalition formation

**Prompt:** Compare and contrast Roosevelt's New Deal coalition with the older Republican coalition it displaced.

- **Old Republican coalition (1896–1932):** business community, Northern Protestant white-collar workers, Northern Black voters (the "Party of Lincoln"), most Western farmers, native-born professionals.
- **FDR's New Deal coalition (1932–~1968):** urban industrial workers (especially CIO unions), Southern white Democrats (still bound by post-Reconstruction party loyalty), Northern Black voters (newly switched in 1936), Catholics and Jews, urban political machines, intellectuals, much of the West (TVA-style federal investment).
- **Key shift:** Black voters realigned from Republican to Democratic in the mid-1930s (Roosevelt got 71% of Black vote in 1936, up from 28% in 1932). Eleanor Roosevelt's civil rights advocacy, the Black Cabinet (Mary McLeod Bethune et al.), and economic relief programs (despite their racial limits) drew Black voters to FDR.
- **Internal tensions:** the coalition combined Northern Black voters demanding civil rights with Southern white Democrats committed to Jim Crow. FDR managed this by avoiding civil rights legislation (refusing to support federal anti-lynching laws) while quietly supporting Black inclusion in some New Deal programs.
- **Sophistication [5]:** the coalition's internal contradictions ultimately destroyed it. The 1948 Democratic Convention's civil rights plank caused Strom Thurmond's "Dixiecrat" walkout. Truman's Civil Rights Committee and 1948 desegregation of armed forces continued the trajectory. The 1964 Civil Rights Act and 1965 Voting Rights Act completed the transformation: Southern white Democrats migrated to the Republican Party (the "Solid South" became Republican by 1990s), while Black voters became overwhelmingly Democratic. The New Deal coalition's success in incorporating Black voters destroyed its hold on the South — a long-running political realignment whose effects shape American politics in 2026.

### Example 5 [5] — Sophistication move on WWII as transformative event

**Prompt:** To what extent did World War II transform American society?

- **Standard view:** WWII transformed America by ending the Depression, expanding women's workforce participation, beginning desegregation of armed forces, and making the US a global superpower.
- **Sophisticated argument:** WWII's transformations were genuine but uneven and partly reversed in the immediate postwar period. The war established the framework for transformations that would continue for decades.
- **Real transformations:** federal government became dominant economic actor (war spending dwarfed New Deal); women entered industrial workforce; Black Americans demanded "Double V" — winning the war and winning civil rights at home; the US emerged as economic and military superpower; nuclear weapons irreversibly changed warfare.
- **Limited or reversed transformations:** women were largely pushed out of industrial jobs after 1945 (return to 1920s gender roles); Black soldiers returned to a still-segregated society (full military desegregation didn't happen until Truman's 1948 EO 9981; civil rights legislation waited until 1964–1965); Japanese Americans remained interned until 1944; immigration policy stayed restrictive (Asian immigration not fully opened until 1965).
- **The deeper continuity:** WWII established the political and economic framework — federal economic management, internationalist foreign policy, broad-based middle-class affluence, military-industrial complex — that defined American life from 1945 to ~1980. The "Liberal Consensus" of the early Cold War era was the institutional legacy of WWII. Even when specific transformations were partial, the framework within which subsequent change occurred was permanent.
- **Why this earns sophistication:** acknowledges the standard "transformative" view, complicates it with specific reversals, identifies the deeper structural continuity, and connects to the Cold War political economy of Period 8.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Treating Progressivism as racially inclusive.** Wilson segregated the federal government (1913). Most Progressive leaders accepted Jim Crow. Black activists worked AROUND mainstream Progressivism (NAACP).
2. **Confusing Theodore Roosevelt with Franklin Roosevelt.** TR (R, 1901–1909, Square Deal). FDR (D, 1933–1945, New Deal). Distant cousins.
3. **Misdating Spanish-American War.** 1898 (April–August). Brief conflict.
4. **Misnaming the Versailles Treaty.** Signed 1919; US Senate REJECTED it. US made separate peace with Germany 1921.
5. **Treating Wilson as a true racial progressive.** He was a Southern Democrat who segregated the federal government and screened *Birth of a Nation* at the White House.
6. **Misnaming the 19th Amendment.** Women's suffrage (1920). NOT Black suffrage (15th Amendment, 1870) or income tax (16th, 1913).
7. **Treating Prohibition as long-term success.** It was repealed by the 21st Amendment (1933) — only constitutional amendment ever repealed.
8. **Misnaming the Johnson-Reed Act / National Origins Act.** 1924. Established quotas based on 1890 ethnic composition (favoring NW Europe). Banned Asian immigration.
9. **Confusing the Stock Market Crash (October 1929) with the cause of the Depression.** The crash signaled and accelerated the Depression but didn't cause it. Underlying causes: weak banking, agricultural distress, wealth concentration, international trade collapse.
10. **Misdating FDR's first inauguration.** March 4, 1933 (last March 4 inauguration). The 20th Amendment (1933) moved subsequent inaugurations to January 20.
11. **Confusing the National Recovery Administration (NRA) with the National Rifle Association** (also NRA). New Deal NRA was struck down by Supreme Court (1935).
12. **Treating the New Deal as ending the Depression.** It alleviated suffering and reformed institutions but unemployment was still ~15% in 1939. World War II's massive federal spending ended the Depression.
13. **Forgetting that Social Security excluded most Black workers initially.** Agricultural and domestic workers (where most Black workers were) were excluded from Social Security and Wagner Act protections.
14. **Confusing Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941) with Hiroshima (August 6, 1945).** Pearl Harbor brought US into WWII; Hiroshima atomic bombing helped end it.
15. **Misnaming Executive Order 9066.** FDR's 1942 order authorizing Japanese American internment. Korematsu v. United States (1944) upheld it.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For Progressive Era essays:**
- Distinguish federal-level (TR, Wilson) from state/local Progressive reforms.
- Cite specific muckrakers and their works.
- Cite specific federal legislation (Pure Food and Drug, Federal Reserve, Clayton).
- Address racial limits explicitly.

**For WWI essays:**
- Distinguish causes of US entry from causes of war broadly.
- Cite specific events (Lusitania, Zimmerman Telegram, German submarine warfare).
- Address home-front mobilization (CPI, war boards, Espionage/Sedition Acts).
- Note Wilson's failure at Versailles and Senate rejection.

**For 1920s essays:**
- Acknowledge the era's TWO sides — economic boom + cultural innovation AND nativism + Klan + Prohibition + Scopes.
- Cite specific Harlem Renaissance figures.
- Cite specific immigration restrictions (1921 Quota, 1924 National Origins).

**For New Deal essays:**
- Distinguish First New Deal (1933, recovery focus) from Second New Deal (1935, reform focus).
- Cite specific programs by name and abbreviation.
- Address racial limits.
- Note ongoing legacy (Social Security, FDIC, SEC, Wagner Act).

**For WWII essays:**
- Address mobilization (federal spending, conscription, women's workforce, Black Double V).
- Address Japanese American internment as constitutional failure.
- Address atomic bomb as turning point in warfare.
- Connect to postwar transformation (UN, Bretton Woods, Cold War origins).

**For HIPP sourcing:**
- Identify the specific political moment (Progressive Era reform pressure, WWI mobilization, New Deal alphabet agency justification, etc.).
- Connect document to author's reform/political agenda.
- Note multiple intended audiences.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Progressive Era reforms expanded federal authority through the Pure Food and Drug Act (1906), Federal Reserve Act (1913), Clayton Antitrust Act (1914), and the 16th, 17th, and 19th Amendments. These reforms fundamentally redefined the federal government's economic role and democratic structure, laying groundwork for the New Deal expansion that would follow."
2. "Progressivism's racial limits were not incidental but constitutive. Wilson segregated the federal government (1913); most Progressive leaders accepted Jim Crow; Black reformers (W.E.B. Du Bois, Ida B. Wells, NAACP) had to work around mainstream Progressivism rather than within it."
3. "The Spanish-American War (1898) launched the United States as a global imperial power through acquisition of the Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam, and Hawaii — fundamentally redefining American foreign policy. The Anti-Imperialist League (Twain, Carnegie, Bryan, Addams) lost the political battle, and the imperialist consensus dominated US foreign policy through 1920."
4. "Multiple causes drove US entry into World War I: German unrestricted submarine warfare (Lusitania 1915, resumption February 1917), the Zimmerman Telegram (March 1917) proposing German-Mexican alliance against the US, US economic interest in Allied victory through wartime loans, and Wilson's commitment to 'making the world safe for democracy.' The Senate's later rejection of the Versailles Treaty and League of Nations marked the beginning of interwar isolationism."
5. "The New Deal transformed the relationship between the federal government and the American people through banking reform (FDIC, Glass-Steagall), labor protection (Wagner Act, FLSA), agricultural restructuring (AAA), public works (CCC, WPA, TVA), and the social insurance system (Social Security Act 1935). Many of these institutions remain operating in the present, having outlasted the political coalition that built them by nearly a century."
6. "World War II completed the federal expansion begun by the New Deal. Federal spending grew from $9 billion (1940) to $98 billion (1944); 16 million Americans served in armed forces; women entered industrial workforce in unprecedented numbers (Rosie the Riveter); the US emerged as economic and military superpower with the dollar as global reserve currency."
7. "Japanese American internment (Executive Order 9066, 1942) forcibly relocated approximately 120,000 persons of Japanese descent (two-thirds US citizens) to interior camps. Upheld by the Supreme Court in Korematsu v. United States (1944), the policy was a constitutional failure based on racial fears rather than security need — Hawaii, with proportionally far more Japanese Americans, conducted no internment. Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act in 1988 issuing formal apology and reparations."

## If You Do Nothing Else for This Period

*Master four anchor narratives: (1) Progressive expansion of federal authority — TR/Taft/Wilson regulation, four constitutional amendments (16th, 17th, 18th, 19th), with explicit attention to racial limits; (2) WWI as both expansion of federal power (CPI, Espionage Act, war boards) and failed peace (Versailles rejection, isolationism); (3) the New Deal as transformation of federal economic role — alphabet agencies, Social Security as enduring centerpiece, racial limits in implementation, Court-packing as overreach; (4) WWII as completion of federal economic dominance, social transformation (women, Black "Double V," Japanese internment as constitutional failure), and US emergence as global superpower with nuclear weapons. These four cover roughly 90% of all Period 7 questions.*
