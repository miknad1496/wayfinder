## Big Ideas

1. **The American Revolution had two phases: independence (1775–1783) and constitutional founding (1781–1789).** Don't conflate them. Winning independence was a military and diplomatic achievement; founding a workable government was a separate political achievement, harder in some ways than the war.
2. **Causation of the Revolution is BEST framed as a colonial reaction to the END of salutary neglect, not the imposition of new tyranny.** Britain wasn't suddenly more oppressive — it was suddenly enforcing what had previously been ignored, AND adding new revenue measures to pay for the Seven Years' War. Colonists had developed expectations of self-government during salutary neglect; restored enforcement felt like usurpation.
3. **Enlightenment philosophy gave colonists the language for revolution.** Locke's natural rights and social contract, Montesquieu's separation of powers, Rousseau's general will — all appear in Revolutionary documents. The Declaration of Independence is essentially a Lockean argument with an itemized indictment of George III.
4. **The Constitution was a radical innovation but also a series of compromises.** Federalism (state vs national power), bicameralism (Connecticut Compromise), the 3/5 Compromise on slavery, the Electoral College — all emerged from contested negotiations, not from a single founding vision.
5. **The 1790s established a partisan divide that persists in different forms today.** Federalists (Hamilton, Adams) — strong central government, manufacturing, alliance with Britain. Democratic-Republicans (Jefferson, Madison) — limited government, agrarian, sympathy for revolutionary France. The "Revolution of 1800" — the peaceful transfer of power between rival parties — was itself revolutionary.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **French and Indian War (Seven Years' War, 1754–1763):** British vs French and their respective Native allies, fought largely over control of the Ohio River Valley. **Treaty of Paris 1763** ended the war: France ceded Canada and all territory east of the Mississippi to Britain; Spain (French ally) ceded Florida to Britain in exchange for Louisiana from France.
- **Proclamation of 1763:** British forbade colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains, hoping to stabilize Native relations after Pontiac's Rebellion (1763–1766). Outraged colonists who had fought the war partly to gain access to those lands.
- **Salutary neglect ENDS, 1763:** Britain begins strict enforcement of trade laws and imposing new taxes to pay war debts. This shift triggers everything that follows.
- **The taxation crisis sequence:**
  - **Sugar Act (1764)** — tax on molasses, sugar; reduced rate from earlier Molasses Act but actually enforced. Colonial protests over admiralty courts (no jury).
  - **Stamp Act (1765)** — tax on all printed materials. Sparked widespread colonial protest. **Stamp Act Congress** (1765) — first formal inter-colonial response. Patrick Henry's Virginia Resolves. Sons of Liberty formed. Repealed 1766 but Britain passed **Declaratory Act** asserting the right to legislate "in all cases whatsoever."
  - **Townshend Acts (1767)** — taxes on glass, paper, paint, lead, tea. Boycotts spread. Daughters of Liberty made homemade goods to replace British imports.
  - **Boston Massacre (1770)** — British soldiers fired into a crowd, killing 5 (including Crispus Attucks, a Black man). John Adams defended the soldiers in court (most acquitted). Colonial propaganda (especially Paul Revere's engraving) inflamed sentiment.
  - **Tea Act (1773)** — gave British East India Company monopoly on tea sales. Boston Tea Party (December 1773) — Sons of Liberty dumped 342 chests of tea into Boston Harbor.
  - **Coercive Acts / "Intolerable Acts" (1774)** — punished Massachusetts: closed Boston Harbor, revoked Massachusetts charter, allowed quartering troops in private homes, moved trials to Britain. **Quebec Act** (related): extended Quebec's borders south to the Ohio River, granted Catholics religious freedom (alarming Protestant colonists).
- **Continental Congresses:**
  - **First Continental Congress (1774)** — met in Philadelphia. Issued Declaration of Rights and Grievances. Coordinated boycott (Continental Association). Did NOT seek independence yet.
  - **Second Continental Congress (1775)** — met after fighting began at Lexington and Concord. Created Continental Army with **George Washington** as commander. Issued **Olive Branch Petition** (rejected by George III) and **Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms**. Eventually issued the **Declaration of Independence** (1776).
- **Lexington and Concord (April 19, 1775)** — first armed engagement of the Revolution. British troops marching to seize colonial weapons; Paul Revere's "midnight ride." "Shot heard round the world."
- **Common Sense (January 1776)** — Thomas Paine's pamphlet arguing for independence in plain language for ordinary readers. Sold ~150,000 copies; transformed colonial opinion in favor of independence.
- **Declaration of Independence (July 4, 1776)** — drafted primarily by Thomas Jefferson. Three sections: (1) Lockean philosophical preamble — "all men are created equal," "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness," government's authority comes from "consent of the governed." (2) Indictment of George III — list of 27 grievances. (3) Formal declaration of independence.
- **Key Revolutionary battles:**
  - **Bunker Hill (June 1775)** — British took the hill but at heavy cost; demonstrated colonial fighting resolve.
  - **Saratoga (October 1777)** — TURNING POINT. American victory under General Horatio Gates convinced France to enter the war on the American side (Treaty of Alliance, 1778).
  - **Valley Forge (winter 1777–1778)** — Continental Army endured brutal winter; Baron von Steuben drilled them into a more disciplined force.
  - **Yorktown (October 1781)** — Cornwallis surrendered to combined American (Washington) and French (Rochambeau) forces, ending major hostilities.
- **Treaty of Paris (1783)** — recognized American independence, established borders (Mississippi River as western boundary), guaranteed fishing rights off Newfoundland. Negotiated by Franklin, Adams, John Jay.
- **Articles of Confederation (1781–1789)** — first national government. Weaknesses:
  - No power to tax (could only request funds from states).
  - No power to regulate interstate or foreign commerce.
  - No executive branch.
  - No national judiciary.
  - Required 9/13 states to pass major legislation; unanimous consent for amendments.
  - **Achievements:** **Northwest Ordinance (1787)** — established procedure for territories to become states; banned slavery in the Northwest Territory. **Land Ordinance of 1785** — surveyed and sold western lands.
- **Shays' Rebellion (1786–1787)** — Massachusetts farmer Daniel Shays led debt-burdened farmers in armed uprising against state government over high taxes and farm foreclosures. Convinced many elites that the Articles were too weak to prevent civic disorder. Helped catalyze the Constitutional Convention.
- **Constitutional Convention (May–September 1787)** — Philadelphia. Originally chartered to revise the Articles; instead, drafted a new constitution.
  - **Virginia Plan** (Madison) — bicameral legislature with representation by population; favored large states.
  - **New Jersey Plan** (Paterson) — unicameral legislature with equal state representation; favored small states.
  - **Connecticut (Great) Compromise** — bicameral legislature: House by population, Senate with equal state representation (2 per state).
  - **Three-Fifths Compromise** — enslaved persons counted as 3/5 of a person for both representation in House and direct taxation.
  - **Slave Trade Compromise** — Congress could not ban the importation of enslaved persons until 1808.
  - **Electoral College** — compromise on presidential selection (not popular vote, not Congress, but state-appointed electors).
- **Federalist vs Anti-Federalist debate:**
  - **Federalists** — supported ratification. Hamilton, Madison, Jay wrote *The Federalist Papers* (85 essays) defending the Constitution. Argued for stronger central government.
  - **Anti-Federalists** — opposed ratification. Patrick Henry, Sam Adams, George Mason. Feared central tyranny; wanted explicit Bill of Rights. *Letters from a Federal Farmer* and *Brutus* essays.
  - **Bill of Rights (ratified 1791)** — first 10 amendments, added to secure ratification. Protected individual rights against federal government.
- **Washington's Presidency (1789–1797):**
  - First Cabinet: Hamilton (Treasury), Jefferson (State), Knox (War), Randolph (Attorney General).
  - **Hamilton's financial program:** assume state debts, create national bank, tariffs, excise taxes (whiskey).
  - **Whiskey Rebellion (1794)** — Pennsylvania farmers protested whiskey excise tax. Washington personally led 13,000 troops to suppress it. Demonstrated federal authority to enforce its laws.
  - **Proclamation of Neutrality (1793)** — Washington declared US neutral in war between France and Britain. Foundational US foreign policy.
  - **Jay's Treaty (1794)** — Britain agreed to evacuate northwestern forts; resolved trade disputes. Jefferson and Democratic-Republicans saw it as too pro-British.
  - **Pinckney's Treaty (1795)** — Spain granted US navigation rights on the Mississippi and right of deposit at New Orleans.
  - **Washington's Farewell Address (1796)** — warned against permanent foreign alliances ("entangling alliances") and political party divisions.
- **Adams's Presidency (1797–1801):**
  - **XYZ Affair (1797–1798)** — French diplomats demanded bribes before negotiating with US envoys. Adams refused; near-war with France ("Quasi-War," 1798–1800).
  - **Alien and Sedition Acts (1798)** — extended naturalization period; allowed deportation of non-citizens; criminalized criticism of the government. Targeted Democratic-Republican press.
  - **Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798–1799)** — Madison and Jefferson argued states could nullify unconstitutional federal laws. Established the doctrine of nullification later invoked in nullification crisis (1832) and to justify secession (1861).
- **Election of 1800** — Jefferson defeated Adams. First peaceful transfer of power between rival political parties in US history. Jefferson called it "the Revolution of 1800."

### Adds for [4]

- **Why protests escalated:** each British concession (Stamp Act repeal, partial Townshend repeal) was paired with new assertions (Declaratory Act, Tea Act) that re-inflamed conflict. Colonists came to suspect British policy reflected deliberate plot to enslave them — Bailyn's "ideology of liberty" framework.
- **Republican vs Lockean ideology in the Revolution.** Colonists drew on BOTH classical republicanism (civic virtue, fear of corruption, suspicion of standing armies) AND Lockean liberalism (natural rights, consent of governed). Anti-Federalist preference for small republics drew on classical republicanism; Federalist #10 explicitly rejected it.
- **Madison's Federalist #10** — most-cited Federalist Paper. Argued an extended republic with diverse factions makes majority tyranny LESS likely because no faction can dominate the whole. Inverted the classical principle that republics had to be small.
- **Slavery and the Revolution.** Revolutionary ideology challenged slavery; Northern states gradually abolished it (Vermont 1777, Pennsylvania 1780, Massachusetts 1783). But the Constitution protected slavery (3/5 Compromise, Slave Trade Compromise to 1808, Fugitive Slave Clause). Simultaneously emancipatory (for some Blacks via wartime service) and entrenching (for Southern slavery).
- **Women: "Republican Motherhood"** assigned women the role of raising virtuous citizens, justifying their education within domesticity but not granting political rights. Abigail Adams's "Remember the Ladies" letter (1776) was unheeded. New Jersey briefly allowed propertied women to vote (1790–1807).
- **Native Americans:** most Native nations sided with the British as a more reliable check on settler land hunger. British loss in 1783 was a Native catastrophe. Northwest Ordinance promised "good faith" but opened Native lands to settlement.
- **Loyalists** = ~15–20% of colonists, concentrated in New York, parts of Carolinas, urban merchants. ~60,000–80,000 emigrated to Canada or Britain after the war.
- **Articles of Confederation's two big achievements:** Land Ordinance of 1785 (rectangular survey, township section 16 for schools) and Northwest Ordinance of 1787 (statehood procedure at 60,000 free inhabitants, banned slavery north of Ohio, new states enter as EQUALS).
- **Federalist Papers beyond #10:** #39 (compound federalism — partly federal, partly national); #51 ("If men were angels, no government would be necessary" — separation of powers, checks); #78 (Hamilton on judiciary as "least dangerous branch" — basis for judicial review).
- **Hamilton's financial program specifics:** Assumption of state debts (Compromise of 1790 traded capital location to Potomac/DC). Bank of the United States (1791, modeled on Bank of England, Jefferson opposed as unconstitutional via strict construction; Hamilton defended via implied powers). Tariff of 1789. Whiskey excise tax (1791) provoked the Whiskey Rebellion.
- **Election of 1796** — Adams (Federalist) president, Jefferson (Democratic-Republican) vice president. Last election before 12th Amendment (1804) separated presidential and VP ballots.

### Adds for [5]

- **The Bailyn-Wood historiographical debate.** Bernard Bailyn (*The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution*) argued the Revolution was driven by colonial fear of British conspiracy against liberty. Gordon Wood (*The Radicalism of the American Revolution*) argued it was a more profound social transformation dismantling colonial hierarchy. The "Critical Period" debate parallels this: Fiske called 1781–1789 a dangerous instability the Constitution rescued; Merrill Jensen argued the Articles worked reasonably well and the "crisis" was partly manufactured by Federalists.
- **The Revolution's radical-conservative tension.** Compared to French (1789), Russian (1917), or Chinese (1949) revolutions, the American Revolution preserved more existing social structures — slavery continued, women remained politically excluded, property qualifications for voting persisted. Politically radical (overthrew monarchy, codified Lockean rights), socially conservative (preserved slavery, hierarchy, exclusion).
- **Why the 3/5 Compromise mattered politically.** It gave Southern states extra House seats and electoral votes based on enslaved populations they did not represent. This boosted Southern political dominance from 1788 to 1865 ("Slave Power" argument, Period 5). Without 3/5, Jefferson would have lost the 1800 election.
- **Federalism as American innovation.** Before 1787, "federation" meant a weak league of sovereign states (Articles, Holy Roman Empire). The Constitution invented something new: both federal AND state governments sovereign over individuals in different domains. A genuine first in human governance.
- **The Whiskey Rebellion vs Shays' Rebellion contrast.** Shays (1786) under the Articles: weak federal response, terrified elites. Whiskey (1794) under the Constitution: overwhelming federal response, successful. Together they bracket the change in federal capacity the Constitution enabled.
- **Lord Dunmore's Proclamation (1775).** British Virginia governor offered freedom to enslaved persons of rebels who joined British forces. Tens of thousands escaped; ~3,000 evacuated as "Black Loyalists" to Nova Scotia and later Sierra Leone. The Revolution offered a paradoxical path to freedom through British service.
- **Why no Bill of Rights originally.** Hamilton (Federalist #84) argued a Bill of Rights was UNNECESSARY because the federal government had only enumerated powers; listing individual rights might imply other unlisted rights weren't protected. Anti-Federalists insisted on explicit protections. The Ninth Amendment ("rights not enumerated...are retained by the people") preserved the original Federalist concern within the eventual Bill of Rights.

## Worked Examples (Thesis & DBQ-Style Argumentation)

### Example 1 [3] — Causation thesis on the Revolution (LEQ)

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which British imperial policies after 1763 caused the American Revolution.

- **Acceptable thesis [3]:** "British imperial policies after 1763 caused the American Revolution by imposing taxes and restrictions that colonists viewed as violations of their rights."
- **Strong thesis [4–5]:** "British imperial policies after 1763 — beginning with the Proclamation of 1763, the Stamp Act (1765), and the Tea Act (1773) — provoked colonial resistance not because they were uniquely oppressive, but because they ended the practical autonomy that colonists had enjoyed under salutary neglect. Colonists' Lockean ideology of natural rights, combined with their inherited tradition of representative self-government in colonial assemblies, transformed reaction against specific taxes into a comprehensive challenge to British sovereignty itself, culminating in the Declaration of Independence in 1776."
- **Why it works:** identifies specific policies (Proclamation, Stamp, Tea), explains the mechanism (end of salutary neglect, not unique oppression), names the ideology (Lockean), connects to the result (Declaration). Sets up sophistication around the salutary-neglect framing.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Articles vs Constitution SAQ

**Prompt:** Identify ONE specific weakness of the Articles of Confederation and explain how the Constitution addressed it.

- **Acceptable answer [3]:** "Under the Articles, Congress could not tax. The Constitution gave Congress the power to lay and collect taxes."
- **Strong answer [4]:** "Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress lacked the power to tax — it could only request funds from the states, which often refused or paid late. This left the federal government without reliable revenue and unable to pay war debts. The Constitution addressed this in Article I, Section 8, granting Congress the power to 'lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts, and excises.' This enabled Hamilton's financial program in the 1790s, including the assumption of state war debts and the establishment of the Bank of the United States, transforming the federal government's fiscal capacity."
- **Why it earns the point:** specific weakness (no tax power), specific consequence (couldn't pay war debts), specific remedy (Article I, Section 8), specific later application (Hamilton's program).

### Example 3 [4] — HIPP sourcing of the Declaration of Independence (1776)

- **Historical situation:** Summer 1776, after over a year of armed conflict (Lexington and Concord 1775). Common Sense (January 1776) had shifted public opinion. Most colonies had instructed delegates to vote for independence.
- **Intended audience:** colonial public (justifying revolution), potential foreign allies (especially France), and the British government (formal announcement).
- **Point of view:** Jefferson, a Virginia planter who owned 600+ enslaved persons across his lifetime — a fact that complicates "all men are created equal." Continental Congress edited the draft, striking Jefferson's condemnation of the slave trade under Southern pressure.
- **Purpose:** persuasive and legal. Lockean preamble provided THEORY justifying revolution; the indictment of George III provided SPECIFIC evidence justifying action against this ruler.
- **Sophisticated extension:** the "all men are created equal" clause, contradicted by drafters' practice, became a moral resource for later movements — Douglass's "What to the Slave is the Fourth of July?", Seneca Falls Declaration of Sentiments (1848), Lincoln at Gettysburg.

### Example 4 [4][5] — Federalist-Anti-Federalist debate as enduring tension

The 1787–1788 debate crystallized two tensions that recur throughout American history:
- **Strong central authority vs state/local autonomy.** Federalists wanted national power for finance, foreign affairs, interstate disputes. Anti-Federalists feared distant tyranny and preferred state-level government closer to the people.
- **Republican size and virtue.** Anti-Federalists invoked classical theory: republics must be small enough for shared virtues. Madison's Federalist #10 inverted this: an EXTENDED republic was SAFER because no faction could dominate.
- **Continuity:** Jacksonians vs Whigs on national bank (Period 4), Slave Power vs Free Soil on territories (Period 5), New Deal vs opponents (Period 7), modern federal-state disputes on civil rights, environment, healthcare.
- **Sophistication [5]:** the categories weren't stable. Anti-Federalists became Democratic-Republicans, who under Jefferson expanded federal power (Louisiana Purchase, Embargo Act, declared war on Britain). The "small government" position migrated across parties; the underlying tension persists.

### Example 5 [5] — Was the Revolution radical?

- **Standard view:** radical because it produced republican government and independence from monarchy.
- **Sophisticated argument:** politically radical (overthrew monarchy, codified Lockean rights, established peaceful inter-party transitions) but socially conservative (preserved slavery, property voting qualifications, patriarchal family, Native dispossession).
- **Specific evidence:** politically radical — Northern emancipation laws (1777–1804), state bills of rights, wholesale national reconstruction. Socially conservative — 3/5 Compromise, Slave Trade Compromise to 1808, Fugitive Slave Clause, no women's suffrage, Land Ordinance opening Native lands.
- **Comparative move:** compared to French (1789), which guillotined elites and abolished feudalism, the American Revolution preserved more. Compared to monarchy-preserving revolutions, it was radical. The characterization depends on the comparison set.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Two Treaties of Paris.** 1763 ended the French and Indian War; 1783 ended the Revolutionary War.
2. **"Shot heard round the world"** = Lexington and Concord (April 19, 1775), NOT Bunker Hill or Saratoga.
3. **Boston Tea Party** = December 16, 1773. Don't confuse with Boston Massacre (1770).
4. **Revolution did NOT start with the Declaration.** Fighting began at Lexington/Concord (April 1775), over a year before the Declaration (July 1776).
5. **Articles vs Constitution.** Articles ratified 1781, replaced by Constitution effective 1789. Articles weak central government; Constitution stronger.
6. **Connecticut Compromise** (also called Great Compromise) = bicameralism. Different from Three-Fifths Compromise (representation of enslaved persons).
7. **Crediting Madison alone for the Constitution.** Madison was principal architect but it was the work of 55 delegates over months of compromise.
8. **Federalists 1787 vs Federalist Party 1790s.** Overlap but distinct. Madison started Federalist (1787), became Democratic-Republican by 1792.
9. **Jefferson did NOT author the Bill of Rights.** Madison did; Jefferson was in Paris during drafting.
10. **Washington's presidency** = 1789–1797 (two terms).
11. **Whiskey Rebellion was suppressed, not victorious.** The rebellion failed; the federal response succeeded in establishing federal authority.
12. **Saratoga's significance** = brought France into war as US ally (1778). Without French aid, victory unlikely.
13. **Confusing Lord Dunmore's Proclamation (1775) with Emancipation Proclamation (1863).** Dunmore offered freedom to enslaved persons of rebels who joined the British — military strategy, not abolition.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For Revolution causation essays:**
- Frame causation as the END of salutary neglect, not the imposition of new tyranny.
- Cite specific acts in chronological order (Sugar 1764 → Stamp 1765 → Townshend 1767 → Tea 1773 → Coercive 1774).
- Connect to ideology: Locke's natural rights, classical republicanism's fear of corruption.

**For Articles vs Constitution essays:**
- Identify SPECIFIC weaknesses (no tax, no commerce regulation, no executive, no judiciary, 9/13 supermajority).
- Identify SPECIFIC achievements of the Articles (Land Ordinance, Northwest Ordinance) — sophistication move.
- Connect Articles weaknesses to specific Constitutional remedies.

**For Constitution / Federalist debate essays:**
- Name SPECIFIC compromises (Connecticut, Three-Fifths, Slave Trade, Electoral College).
- Cite SPECIFIC Federalist Papers (#10 on factions, #51 on checks, #78 on judiciary).
- Acknowledge Anti-Federalist concerns and the resulting Bill of Rights.

**For Washington/Adams presidency essays:**
- Name SPECIFIC precedents (two-term tradition, neutrality, federal authority via Whiskey Rebellion suppression).
- Connect Hamilton's program to specific policies (assumption, bank, tariffs).
- Note party formation (Federalist vs Democratic-Republican) by mid-1790s.

**For HIPP sourcing of Revolutionary documents:**
- Identify which side of the Revolutionary debate the author represents.
- Note the audience: colonial public, foreign powers, British government?
- Connect document features to the author's persuasive purpose.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "British imperial policy after 1763 ended the salutary neglect of earlier decades. The Sugar Act (1764), Stamp Act (1765), Townshend Acts (1767), Tea Act (1773), and Coercive Acts (1774) appeared not as new oppression but as the abrupt end of practical autonomy that colonists had come to consider their birthright as English subjects."
2. "The Declaration of Independence (1776) drew on John Locke's natural rights theory and social contract — that government's authority comes from 'the consent of the governed' and that when government becomes destructive of the people's rights, 'it is the right of the people to alter or to abolish it.' The document combined philosophical justification with a specific indictment of George III."
3. "The Articles of Confederation (1781–1789) deliberately created a weak federal government in reaction to British centralized power. The lack of taxation authority, executive branch, and federal judiciary became evident after Shays' Rebellion (1786–1787), catalyzing the Constitutional Convention of 1787."
4. "The Constitution emerged from a series of compromises: the Connecticut Compromise resolved large vs small state representation through bicameralism; the Three-Fifths Compromise gave Southern states partial credit for enslaved populations; the Slave Trade Compromise protected the importation of enslaved persons until 1808; the Electoral College compromised on presidential selection."
5. "Madison's Federalist No. 10 inverted classical republican wisdom that republics must be small. He argued that an EXTENDED republic with diverse factions would prove SAFER for liberty because no single faction could dominate the whole — a defense of the new Constitution against Anti-Federalist objections that it created an oversized national government."
6. "Hamilton's financial program — the assumption of state debts, the creation of the Bank of the United States, the protective tariff, and the whiskey excise — established the federal government's fiscal authority. The Whiskey Rebellion (1794), suppressed by federal troops led personally by Washington, demonstrated that the new government could enforce its laws against domestic resistance."
7. "The Election of 1800 — Jefferson's defeat of Adams — marked the first peaceful transfer of power between rival political parties in American history. Despite the bitter partisan conflict that had produced the Alien and Sedition Acts, Adams accepted the result, establishing the precedent that political loss did not justify resistance to constitutional outcomes."

## If You Do Nothing Else for This Period

*Master three anchor narratives: (1) the road to Revolution as the end of salutary neglect, with the specific tax-act sequence (Sugar/Stamp/Townshend/Tea/Coercive); (2) the Articles-to-Constitution transition with specific weaknesses, specific compromises, and the Federalist-Anti-Federalist debate; (3) Washington's precedents and the formation of the first party system (Federalist vs Democratic-Republican). These three narratives together cover roughly 80% of all Period 3 questions on the AP exam.*
