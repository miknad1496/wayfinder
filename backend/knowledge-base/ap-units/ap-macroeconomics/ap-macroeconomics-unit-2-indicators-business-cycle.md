Unemployment types (cyclical, frictional, structural) appear in nearly every exam. CPI calculation and inflation rate computation are common. The distinction between NOMINAL and REAL (whether for GDP, interest rates, or wages) is the unit's signature insight — students who don't internalize it lose easy points throughout the rest of the course.

## Big Ideas

1. **GDP measures total economic output.** Gross Domestic Product = the market value of all FINAL goods and services produced WITHIN a country during a specific period (typically a year or quarter). It's the headline measure of economic size.
2. **Nominal vs. Real distinction is THE most important concept in the unit.** Nominal = current dollars (no inflation adjustment). Real = inflation-adjusted (constant dollars). The distinction matters for GDP, wages, interest rates, and most economic comparisons over time.
3. **Three types of unemployment have different policy implications.** Cyclical (caused by recession, addressable through demand-side policy). Frictional (job-search transitions, healthy and inevitable). Structural (skill mismatches, requires retraining and education investment).
4. **Inflation can be measured multiple ways.** CPI (basket of consumer goods), GDP deflator (all final goods and services). They tell related but distinct stories. Inflation rate = percentage change in price level.
5. **The business cycle is the recurring pattern of economic expansion and contraction.** Peak → contraction → trough → expansion → peak. Modern economic policy attempts to smooth these cycles through fiscal and monetary policy (Units 3, 4, 5).

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Gross Domestic Product (GDP):**
  - The market value of all FINAL goods and services produced WITHIN a country during a specific time period.
  - Key qualifiers: FINAL (not intermediate goods, to avoid double-counting); WITHIN (location-based, not citizenship); MARKET VALUE (priced exchanges).
- **GDP — three approaches** (all yield the same total):
  - **Expenditure approach (most common):** GDP = C + I + G + NX, where:
    - **C (Consumption):** household spending on goods and services. Largest component (~70% of US GDP).
    - **I (Investment):** business spending on capital goods, residential construction, and inventories. NOT financial investment.
    - **G (Government Spending):** federal, state, and local government purchases. Does NOT include transfer payments (Social Security, unemployment).
    - **NX (Net Exports):** Exports − Imports.
  - **Income approach:** sum of all income earned in production.
  - **Value-added approach:** sum of value added at each stage of production.
- **What GDP DOESN'T measure:**
  - Non-market activities (volunteer work, household labor).
  - Underground economy.
  - Quality of life, environmental damage, leisure.
  - Distribution of wealth.
- **Nominal vs Real GDP:**
  - **Nominal GDP:** measured in CURRENT dollars (whatever year's prices).
  - **Real GDP:** adjusted for inflation; measured in CONSTANT dollars (a base year's prices).
  - **Why it matters:** comparing 1990 and 2020 nominal GDP confuses inflation with real growth. Real GDP allows valid comparison.
- **GDP deflator** = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100. Measures economy-wide price level.
- **Real GDP per capita** = Real GDP / Population. Best measure of average economic well-being.
- **Unemployment:**
  - **Unemployment rate** = (Unemployed / Labor Force) × 100, where Labor Force = Employed + Unemployed.
  - **Labor force participation rate** = (Labor Force / Working-age Population) × 100.
  - **Discouraged workers:** people not actively seeking work (no longer in labor force). Not counted as unemployed.
  - **Underemployed:** part-time workers wanting full-time work, or workers in jobs below their skill level. Not directly captured in unemployment rate.
- **Three types of unemployment:**
  - **Cyclical unemployment:** caused by economic downturn / recession. Addressed by demand-side policies (fiscal/monetary stimulus, Units 3 and 4).
  - **Frictional unemployment:** workers between jobs (recently graduated, just moved, voluntarily quit and searching). HEALTHY and inevitable.
  - **Structural unemployment:** mismatch between worker skills and job requirements. Caused by technology, geographic shifts, declining industries. Addressed through education and retraining.
- **Natural rate of unemployment:** frictional + structural unemployment. The rate at which the economy is at "full employment" (no cyclical unemployment). Typically estimated at 4-5% in modern US.
- **Full employment:** when actual unemployment = natural rate of unemployment. Cyclical unemployment is zero.
- **Inflation:**
  - **Inflation rate:** percentage change in price level over time.
  - **Causes:**
    - **Demand-pull inflation:** too much money chasing too few goods. Aggregate demand exceeds aggregate supply.
    - **Cost-push inflation:** rising production costs (oil prices, wages) push prices up regardless of demand.
  - **Hyperinflation:** extremely rapid inflation (often defined as 50%+ per month). Destroys economic stability (Weimar Germany, Zimbabwe, Venezuela).
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI):**
  - Measures the average change in prices paid by urban consumers for a market basket of consumer goods and services.
  - **Calculation:** (Cost of basket in current year / Cost of basket in base year) × 100.
  - **Inflation rate from CPI:** ((CPI new − CPI old) / CPI old) × 100.
  - **Limitations:** uses fixed basket (substitution bias); doesn't account for quality improvements; biased toward urban consumption patterns.
- **Real vs Nominal interest rates:**
  - **Nominal interest rate:** the stated rate (what your bank account earns).
  - **Real interest rate:** Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate.
  - **Why it matters:** real interest rate measures the actual purchasing power gain from saving. If your savings earn 3% but inflation is 4%, your REAL return is −1% — you've lost purchasing power despite the positive nominal rate.
- **Business cycle:** recurring pattern of expansion and contraction.
  - **Peak:** maximum point of cycle (high output, low unemployment, possibly inflation).
  - **Contraction (recession):** declining output, rising unemployment.
  - **Trough:** lowest point of cycle.
  - **Expansion (recovery):** rising output, falling unemployment.
  - **Two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth** = informal definition of recession (NBER officially declares recessions using broader criteria).

### Adds for [4]

- **Why GDP excludes intermediate goods.** Including intermediate goods (e.g., the steel that goes into a car) AND the final good (the car) would double-count. GDP counts only FINAL goods to avoid this.
- **Why transfer payments are excluded from G.** Transfer payments (Social Security checks, unemployment benefits, food stamps) move money but don't produce new goods or services. They're already counted when the recipient spends them as C.
- **Real GDP calculation methods:**
  - **Chain-weighted real GDP:** updated method using a moving average of base-year prices. Avoids the bias of fixed base years.
  - **Older method:** fixed base year. Becomes less accurate as economy changes.
- **Why CPI overstates inflation:**
  - **Substitution bias:** when prices rise for one good, consumers substitute toward others. CPI's fixed basket doesn't capture this.
  - **Quality bias:** new technology often improves quality at the same price. CPI may treat this as "no inflation" when it's actually deflation.
  - **New product bias:** CPI is slow to incorporate new products.
  - **Outlet bias:** consumers shift to lower-cost outlets (Walmart, online); CPI is slow to capture this.
  - The Boskin Commission (1996) estimated CPI overstates inflation by ~1.1% per year.
- **Calculating inflation rate from CPI** worked example:
  - CPI 2020 = 258. CPI 2021 = 271.
  - Inflation rate = ((271 − 258) / 258) × 100 = 5.04%.
- **Real vs Nominal applied to wages:**
  - If your nominal wage rises 3% but inflation is 5%, your REAL wage falls 2%. You're worse off despite the nominal raise.
  - Workers should compare wage gains to inflation for true purchasing power impact.
- **Real GDP per capita as well-being measure:**
  - **Strengths:** measures average material standard of living; allows international comparison.
  - **Limits:** ignores distribution (income inequality); ignores non-market production (household labor); ignores environmental quality, health, leisure; says nothing about subjective well-being.
  - **Alternative measures:** Human Development Index (HDI), Genuine Progress Indicator, Gross National Happiness.
- **Cyclical vs frictional vs structural distinguished:**
  - **Coal miner laid off because demand for coal collapsed:** structural (industry declining, skills mismatched).
  - **Recently graduated college student looking for first job:** frictional (transition between school and work).
  - **Auto worker laid off during recession when car sales drop temporarily:** cyclical (demand drop, will likely return when economy recovers).
- **Discouraged workers and underemployment.** Headline unemployment rate UNDERSTATES labor market slack:
  - Discouraged workers (not seeking work because they believe none available) aren't counted as unemployed.
  - Underemployed workers (part-time wanting full-time, or skilled workers in lower-skill jobs) aren't captured.
  - The "U-6" rate from BLS includes these and is typically several percentage points higher than headline U-3 rate.
- **Output gap:** difference between actual real GDP and potential real GDP (full-employment output).
  - **Negative output gap:** actual < potential. Recession; cyclical unemployment.
  - **Positive output gap:** actual > potential. Inflation pressure; economy "overheating."
- **NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment):** the unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation. Roughly equivalent to the natural rate of unemployment but emphasizes inflation stability. Below NAIRU: inflation tends to accelerate. Above: inflation tends to decelerate.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why GDP isn't a perfect well-being measure.** GDP measures market production but ignores:
  - **Distribution:** $50,000 average GDP per capita can mean either widespread modest prosperity or extreme inequality with mass poverty.
  - **Non-market production:** raising children, household work, volunteering — all valuable but not in GDP.
  - **Environmental degradation:** depleting resources or polluting air/water doesn't reduce GDP; in some cases it increases it (cleanup spending counts).
  - **Leisure:** GDP rises if you work more hours; doesn't capture value of free time.
  - **Quality of life:** healthcare access, education quality, community well-being aren't directly measured.
- **The "money illusion."** People sometimes confuse nominal changes for real changes. A worker may feel pleased with a 3% raise without realizing 5% inflation means they're losing purchasing power. This illusion can affect economic behavior — workers may agree to nominal pay cuts they'd resist in real terms (employers reducing base pay vs slowing raises, even though both have similar real-wage effects).
- **Why inflation rates vary by group.** CPI represents an average urban consumer's basket. Real inflation experienced varies by:
  - **Income level:** lower-income households spend higher share on food, energy, housing — categories that often inflate faster.
  - **Geographic location:** California vs Mississippi inflation rates differ.
  - **Age:** older Americans use more healthcare; CPI-E (experimental price index for elderly) shows different inflation than headline CPI.
  - **Lifestyle:** vegetarians vs meat-eaters experience different food inflation.
- **The "sticky wages" phenomenon.** Wages are slow to adjust downward in nominal terms — workers resist and employers fear morale damage. This creates problems in deflationary environments (1930s) when nominal wage rigidity translates to high unemployment instead of falling wages. It also means inflation can erode real wages without nominal cuts — an indirect mechanism for labor market adjustment.
- **The "Beveridge curve" relating job openings and unemployment.** When job openings are HIGH but unemployment is also HIGH, that suggests structural mismatch (jobs available but workers don't have right skills/locations). Modern US economy has shown this pattern since COVID — many job openings, persistent unemployment, suggesting structural mismatches in labor market.
- **Why inflation expectations matter.** If people expect future inflation, they:
  - Demand higher nominal wages.
  - Set higher prices.
  - Borrow more (debts shrink in real terms).
  - **Self-fulfilling prophecy:** expectations of inflation can cause inflation. This is why central banks worry about "inflation expectations becoming unanchored."
- **Hysteresis effects.** Long-term unemployment can become structural — workers lose skills, networks, and confidence. The longer someone is unemployed, the harder it becomes to find work. This means recessions can cause LASTING damage to potential GDP, not just temporary output losses.
- **Why "two consecutive quarters of negative GDP" isn't an official definition of recession.** The NBER (National Bureau of Economic Research) Business Cycle Dating Committee considers multiple indicators: GDP, employment, real income, industrial production, retail sales. The "two quarters of negative GDP" is a useful informal rule but doesn't capture the full picture.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — GDP calculation

A country reports the following economic activity:
- Consumer spending: $800 billion
- Business investment: $200 billion
- Government spending: $400 billion (of which $50 billion is transfer payments)
- Exports: $150 billion
- Imports: $250 billion

Calculate GDP.

- **Step 1.** GDP = C + I + G + NX.
- **Step 2.** C = $800 billion. I = $200 billion. G = $400 billion BUT we exclude transfer payments → effective G = $400 − $50 = $350 billion. NX = Exports − Imports = $150 − $250 = −$100 billion.
- **Step 3.** GDP = $800 + $200 + $350 + (−$100) = $1,250 billion.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Real vs Nominal GDP

A country's nominal GDP grew from $1,000 billion in 2020 to $1,100 billion in 2021. CPI rose from 200 to 215 over the same period.

(a) Calculate real GDP for 2021 in 2020 dollars.
(b) Calculate real GDP growth rate.

- **(a)** Real GDP 2021 (in 2020 dollars) = Nominal GDP 2021 / (CPI 2021 / CPI 2020) = $1,100 / (215/200) = $1,100 / 1.075 = $1,023 billion.
- **(b)** Real GDP growth rate = ($1,023 − $1,000) / $1,000 = 0.023 = 2.3%.
- **Insight:** nominal GDP grew 10% but real GDP grew only 2.3% because much of the nominal increase was inflation, not real economic growth.

### Example 3 [3] — Unemployment classification

Identify each as cyclical, frictional, or structural:
(a) A coal miner laid off because the mine closed permanently due to declining coal demand.
(b) A college graduate searching for her first job.
(c) A factory worker laid off during a recession.
(d) A retail worker who quit to look for a better-paying job.
(e) A bank teller replaced by an ATM.

- **(a) Structural:** industry decline; worker's skills don't match available jobs.
- **(b) Frictional:** transition into the labor force; normal job-search period.
- **(c) Cyclical:** caused by recession; will likely find new work when economy recovers.
- **(d) Frictional:** voluntary transition between jobs.
- **(e) Structural:** technological change has eliminated the job category.

### Example 4 [4] — Real interest rate calculation

A bank account pays 4% nominal interest. CPI rises 6% over the year.

(a) What is the real interest rate?
(b) What does this mean for the saver?

- **(a)** Real interest rate = Nominal − Inflation = 4% − 6% = −2%.
- **(b)** The saver loses purchasing power despite earning 4% nominally. $100 deposited becomes $104 nominally, but it can buy 2% LESS than $100 could a year earlier. The real return is negative.
- **Implication:** in inflationary environments, savers in low-rate accounts effectively lose money. This is one reason inflation hurts savers and helps borrowers (whose debts shrink in real terms).

### Example 5 [4][5] — Output gap and policy implications

A country's potential real GDP is $5 trillion. Actual real GDP is $4.5 trillion.

(a) Calculate output gap.
(b) What does this suggest about the business cycle?
(c) What policies might address the situation?

- **(a)** Output gap = Actual − Potential = $4.5T − $5T = −$0.5 trillion (or −10% of potential GDP).
- **(b)** Negative output gap indicates the economy is operating BELOW its potential. There's likely cyclical unemployment, idle capacity, and slack in the economy. Business cycle is in or near contraction/recession territory.
- **(c)** **Demand-side policies** would address cyclical unemployment:
  - **Expansionary fiscal policy:** government increases spending, cuts taxes (Unit 3 and 5).
  - **Expansionary monetary policy:** Federal Reserve cuts interest rates, increases money supply (Unit 4).
- **The aim:** boost aggregate demand to bring actual GDP up to potential, returning to full employment.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing intermediate and final goods.** GDP counts FINAL goods only. Intermediate goods (raw materials used in further production) would double-count.
2. **Including transfer payments in G.** Transfer payments (Social Security, unemployment benefits) don't create new production. They're not part of G.
3. **Treating financial investment as I.** "Investment" in macro = business spending on capital. NOT stocks/bonds purchases.
4. **Using nominal when real is needed.** Comparisons over time should use REAL values.
5. **Wrong inflation calculation direction.** Inflation rate = (new − old) / old × 100. Not the reverse.
6. **Confusing CPI and GDP deflator.** CPI = consumer goods basket. GDP deflator = all final goods/services in economy. They can move differently.
7. **Adding cyclical, frictional, structural without distinction.** Each has different causes and policy implications.
8. **Conflating natural rate with zero unemployment.** Natural rate includes frictional and structural — typically 4-5%. Zero unemployment is impossible and undesirable.
9. **Treating discouraged workers as unemployed.** They're not counted in headline unemployment because they're not actively seeking work.
10. **Confusing nominal and real interest rates.** Real = Nominal − Inflation. Saving's real return depends on real rate.
11. **Treating GDP per capita as well-being measure.** GDP per capita measures average material output, NOT well-being, distribution, or quality of life.
12. **Calling any GDP decline a "recession."** Two consecutive quarters of negative GDP is informal; NBER uses broader criteria.
13. **Assuming unemployment rate captures full picture.** U-3 (headline) ignores discouraged workers, underemployed, those out of labor force.
14. **Misnaming the components of GDP.** C (consumption), I (business investment), G (government purchases excluding transfers), NX (net exports = exports − imports).
15. **Using CPI for international comparison.** CPI methodology differs across countries; international comparisons require harmonized methodologies.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For GDP calculations:**
- Use C + I + G + NX formula correctly.
- Exclude transfer payments from G.
- Calculate NX as exports − imports.

**For real vs nominal:**
- Real = Nominal / Price index ratio.
- Real interest rate = Nominal − Inflation.
- Always specify which when discussing wages, GDP, interest rates.

**For unemployment:**
- Classify by cause (cyclical, frictional, structural).
- Connect to appropriate policy response.

**For inflation:**
- Identify CPI vs GDP deflator (different baskets).
- Calculate inflation rate as percentage change.
- Distinguish demand-pull from cost-push.

**For business cycle:**
- Identify the phase (peak, contraction, trough, expansion).
- Connect to output gap and unemployment.
