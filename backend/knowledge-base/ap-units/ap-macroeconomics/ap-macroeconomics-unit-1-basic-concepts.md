## Big Ideas

1. **Scarcity is the universal economic problem.** Resources are limited; wants are unlimited. Every economic decision is about ALLOCATING scarce resources among competing uses.
2. **Opportunity cost = the value of the next-best alternative foregone.** Every choice has a cost — what you GIVE UP. Understanding this transforms how you analyze every economic decision.
3. **The Production Possibilities Curve (PPC) shows the trade-offs between two goods given fixed resources.** Points ON the curve = efficient. Points INSIDE = inefficient/underutilization. Points OUTSIDE = currently impossible (would require more resources or technology).
4. **Comparative advantage drives trade gains.** Countries (or individuals) should produce what they have COMPARATIVE advantage in (lower opportunity cost) and trade for the rest. This is true even if one party has absolute advantage in everything.
5. **Marginal analysis = comparing additional benefit to additional cost.** Decisions should be made at the MARGIN — should I do ONE MORE unit? This is the foundation for all rational economic decision-making.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Scarcity:** unlimited wants meet limited resources. The fundamental economic problem.
- **Resources / factors of production:**
  - **Land:** natural resources (land itself, water, minerals, oil).
  - **Labor:** human work.
  - **Capital:** human-made resources used to produce goods (machinery, buildings, tools). NOTE: in economics, "capital" means PHYSICAL capital, not financial capital.
  - **Entrepreneurship:** organizing the other factors, taking risks.
- **Opportunity cost:** the value of the NEXT-BEST alternative foregone. If you choose to study, the opportunity cost is the highest-valued thing you would have done with that time (work, sleep, hang out with friends).
- **Three economic questions** every economy must answer:
  - **What** to produce?
  - **How** to produce it?
  - **For whom** to produce?
- **Economic systems:**
  - **Market economy:** decisions made by individuals and firms through market prices. Capitalism. Mostly US, modern Western Europe.
  - **Command economy:** decisions made by central government. Soviet Union, North Korea, Cuba (historically).
  - **Mixed economy:** combination of both. Most modern economies, including the US.
  - **Traditional economy:** decisions based on customs, traditions. Increasingly rare.
- **Production Possibilities Curve (PPC):**
  - Graph showing maximum combinations of two goods that can be produced with given resources and technology.
  - **Points ON the curve:** efficient (using all resources, no waste).
  - **Points INSIDE the curve:** inefficient (resources unused or misallocated).
  - **Points OUTSIDE the curve:** currently impossible (would require more resources or better technology).
  - **PPC shape:**
    - **Linear (straight line):** constant opportunity costs (rare in real world).
    - **Concave (bowed outward):** increasing opportunity costs (typical) — as you produce more of one good, each additional unit costs more units of the other good (because resources aren't perfectly substitutable).
- **Shifts in PPC:**
  - **Outward shift:** more resources, better technology, growth. PPC moves outward, allowing more of both goods.
  - **Inward shift:** fewer resources, war, disaster, brain drain.
  - **Pivot:** improvement in technology for ONE good (PPC shifts on one axis but not the other).
- **Absolute advantage:** ability to produce MORE of a good with the same resources, OR produce the same with FEWER resources. Pure productivity comparison.
- **Comparative advantage:** ability to produce a good at LOWER OPPORTUNITY COST. Comparative advantage drives trade gains.
- **Two methods to compute comparative advantage:**
  - **Output method:** when each side produces output (e.g., bushels of wheat per worker per day). Compare opportunity cost ratios.
  - **Input method:** when each side uses inputs (e.g., hours per bushel). Compare opportunity cost ratios.
- **Gains from trade:** when each party specializes in their comparative advantage and trades, both parties consume MORE than they could producing alone. The terms of trade must fall between the two parties' opportunity costs for trade to benefit both.
- **Marginal analysis:**
  - **Marginal benefit:** additional benefit from one more unit.
  - **Marginal cost:** additional cost of one more unit.
  - **Decision rule:** do something if marginal benefit ≥ marginal cost. Stop when MB = MC.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why PPC bows outward (concave shape).** Resources aren't perfectly substitutable. Some workers, machines, land are better suited to producing one good than another. As you shift more resources from Good A to Good B, you have to use less-suitable resources for Good B, requiring more units of Good A to be sacrificed for each additional unit of Good B. This is the law of increasing opportunity cost.
- **Comparative advantage worked example (output method):**
  - Suppose USA produces 10 cars OR 5 wheat per worker. China produces 4 cars OR 6 wheat per worker.
  - **Absolute advantage:** USA has absolute advantage in cars (10 > 4); China has absolute advantage in wheat (6 > 5).
  - **Comparative advantage:**
    - USA's opportunity cost of 1 car = 5/10 = 0.5 wheat. China's opportunity cost of 1 car = 6/4 = 1.5 wheat. **USA has comparative advantage in cars** (lower opportunity cost).
    - USA's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 10/5 = 2 cars. China's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 4/6 = 0.67 cars. **China has comparative advantage in wheat** (lower opportunity cost).
  - **Trade gains:** USA produces cars and trades for wheat; China produces wheat and trades for cars. Both consume more than producing alone.
- **Comparative advantage worked example (input method):**
  - Suppose USA needs 4 hours per car, 8 hours per wheat. China needs 10 hours per car, 5 hours per wheat.
  - **Absolute advantage:** USA in cars (4 < 10); China in wheat (5 < 8).
  - **Comparative advantage:**
    - USA's opportunity cost of 1 car = 4 hours / (8 hours/wheat) = 0.5 wheat. China's opportunity cost of 1 car = 10/5 = 2 wheat. **USA has comparative advantage in cars.**
    - USA's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 8/4 = 2 cars. China's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 5/10 = 0.5 cars. **China has comparative advantage in wheat.**
- **Terms of trade:** the rate at which goods trade. Must fall BETWEEN the two parties' opportunity costs for both to benefit. In the example: USA values 1 car at 0.5 wheat; China values 1 car at 1.5 wheat. Terms of trade between 0.5 and 1.5 wheat per car would benefit both.
- **Specialization** = focusing production on one or few goods.
- **Why specialization works:**
  - **Increased productivity** through learning by doing.
  - **Lower opportunity costs** through using best-suited resources for each good.
  - **Trade enables consumption** beyond own production.
  - **Result:** more goods total, both parties consume more.
- **PPC vs other curves:** PPC is about production possibilities of TWO goods. Don't confuse with:
  - Demand curves (Unit 3) — show price-quantity relationships.
  - Supply curves (Unit 3) — show price-quantity relationships from producer side.
  - Aggregate Demand / Supply (Unit 3) — show price-output relationships at macro level.
- **Marginal analysis applied:**
  - **Consumer:** buy more of a good if marginal utility per dollar exceeds marginal utility per dollar of alternatives.
  - **Producer:** produce more if marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost.
  - **Government:** spend on a program if marginal benefit (to society) exceeds marginal cost (to taxpayers).
  - **Optimal point:** when MB = MC for last unit.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why opportunity cost matters even when no money is exchanged.** Choosing to read this article has an opportunity cost (whatever you would have done instead). Choosing to study one subject has an opportunity cost (other subjects you could have studied). Time, attention, and resources are scarce; every choice forecloses other possibilities. This concept extends well beyond formal markets.
- **Comparative advantage's policy implications.** Free trade based on comparative advantage benefits BOTH trading partners — both can consume more than producing alone. This is the economic case against trade restrictions (tariffs, quotas) — they reduce total gains. But the case has limits:
  - **Distribution effects:** specific industries and workers lose from imports even when nation as a whole gains.
  - **Strategic considerations:** national security may justify restricting trade in defense-critical goods.
  - **Infant industry argument:** new industries may need temporary protection to develop competitiveness.
  - **Modern debate:** Trump-era tariffs partly reflected discontent with comparative-advantage trade theory's distribution effects.
- **The "production possibilities" framework as broader thinking tool.** Even outside economics, the PPC framework can illuminate trade-offs:
  - A country choosing between defense and education spending faces a "guns vs butter" PPC.
  - An organization choosing between innovation and operations faces a similar trade-off.
  - Time allocation between work and leisure faces a PPC.
  - Recognizing these trade-offs structures clearer thinking.
- **Why comparative (not absolute) advantage drives trade.** A common misconception: a country (or person) needs absolute advantage to benefit from trade. NOT TRUE. Even if you're worse at everything, you have COMPARATIVE advantage in something — what you're "least bad" at. By specializing in your comparative advantage and trading, you're better off than producing everything yourself. This is one of economics' most important insights.
- **The marginal revolution.** The shift from average to marginal thinking transformed economics in the late 19th century. Classical economists (Smith, Ricardo) thought in terms of total cost and total revenue. The "marginalists" (Jevons, Menger, Walras) showed that decisions are made AT THE MARGIN — the next unit, not the average. Modern economic theory (consumer choice, firm theory, public goods, externalities) all use marginal analysis.
- **Increasing vs constant opportunity costs in PPCs.** A linear (straight) PPC has constant opportunity costs — every unit of one good costs the same units of the other. This requires perfectly substitutable resources. In reality, most production has increasing opportunity costs (resources are specialized), so PPCs typically bow outward. The bowed shape captures real economic constraints.
- **Why "we should produce more" doesn't mean "we should produce more of everything."** A country INSIDE its PPC has slack — it could produce more of both goods without giving anything up. A country ON its PPC must give up one good to produce more of another. Recognizing where you ARE on the PPC determines what's possible.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — PPC shifts

A country produces wheat and cars. Initially on its PPC at point A (200 wheat, 50 cars).

(a) New technology DOUBLES wheat productivity. Show on graph.
(b) War destroys 30% of factories. Show on graph.
(c) Country discovers oil deposits enabling more capital. Show on graph.

- **(a)** PPC pivots OUTWARD on wheat axis (more wheat possible) but UNCHANGED on cars axis. Maximum wheat doubles; maximum cars unchanged.
- **(b)** PPC shifts INWARD overall — fewer cars AND less wheat possible. Loss of capital reduces production capacity in both sectors.
- **(c)** PPC shifts OUTWARD overall — more wheat AND more cars possible. New resources expand production possibilities.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Comparative advantage calculation

Country X can produce 100 cars OR 200 bushels of wheat per year.
Country Y can produce 50 cars OR 150 bushels of wheat per year.

(a) Identify absolute advantages.
(b) Identify comparative advantages.
(c) State who should specialize in what.
(d) Find a trade ratio that benefits both.

- **(a)** Country X has absolute advantage in BOTH cars (100 > 50) and wheat (200 > 150). It produces more of both than Country Y.
- **(b)** Comparative advantage:
  - X's opportunity cost of 1 car = 200 wheat / 100 cars = 2 wheat. X's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 100 cars / 200 wheat = 0.5 cars.
  - Y's opportunity cost of 1 car = 150 wheat / 50 cars = 3 wheat. Y's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 50 cars / 150 wheat = 0.33 cars.
  - X has lower opportunity cost for cars (2 wheat < 3 wheat) → COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE in cars.
  - Y has lower opportunity cost for wheat (0.33 cars < 0.5 cars) → COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE in wheat.
- **(c)** X should specialize in cars; Y should specialize in wheat. They should trade to consume both goods.
- **(d)** Terms of trade must fall between 2 and 3 wheat per car (between X's opportunity cost of 2 and Y's opportunity cost of 3). At a ratio of 2.5 wheat per car: both parties gain from trade.

### Example 3 [4] — Marginal analysis applied

A company is considering whether to produce one more unit. Marginal cost = $50. Marginal revenue = $60.

- **Decision:** produce the additional unit. MR ($60) > MC ($50), so producing it adds $10 to profit.
- **General rule:** continue producing as long as MR > MC. Stop when MR = MC.
- **Common error:** considering total revenue and total cost instead of MARGINAL. The marginal analysis is what matters for INCREMENTAL decisions.

### Example 4 [4] — Opportunity cost in personal decision

Sarah has two options for Saturday:
- Option A: work 4 hours at $20/hour ($80 income).
- Option B: study for AP exams (no income but improved exam performance).

She chooses Option A.

- **Opportunity cost of Option A:** the value she places on the studying she didn't do (which is hard to quantify but might be substantial — better AP scores → better college options → higher long-term earnings).
- **Opportunity cost of Option B (had she chosen):** $80 income foregone.
- **Insight:** opportunity cost includes BOTH explicit costs (money) AND implicit costs (time, foregone alternatives). Many economic decisions look different when implicit costs are included.

### Example 5 [5] — Comparative advantage limits in policy debates

Modern trade policy debates illustrate comparative advantage's value AND limits:

- **The classic case:** Apple iPhones use US-designed software, Chinese assembly. Both countries gain from this division based on comparative advantages. Tariffs would raise iPhone prices, hurting US consumers, without bringing back the kinds of jobs they target.
- **The complication:** comparative advantage is not static. China's comparative advantage in electronics manufacturing was BUILT through state investment, technology transfer, infrastructure development. The "natural" comparative advantage shifted because of policy choices. Other countries might develop comparative advantages similarly with similar investment.
- **The distribution issue:** even if free trade increases TOTAL US wealth, specific workers, communities, and industries lose. Coal towns in West Virginia, manufacturing towns in the Midwest experienced concentrated losses while gains were dispersed across consumers and stockholders. Politically, the losers vote and the winners rarely organize as such.
- **Policy implications:** the standard economic prescription (free trade) needs to be paired with policies addressing distribution effects (worker training, regional adjustment, social insurance). Without these, the political backlash to free trade (Trump tariffs, Brexit) reflects rational concerns about uneven gains.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Conflating absolute and comparative advantage.** Absolute = more efficient overall. Comparative = lower opportunity cost. Comparative advantage drives trade.
2. **Comparing the wrong opportunity costs.** Compare each country's opportunity cost of the SAME good. Lower opportunity cost = comparative advantage.
3. **Setting up the opportunity cost ratio wrong.** Opportunity cost of 1 unit of A = total possible units of B / total possible units of A.
4. **Forgetting opportunity cost includes IMPLICIT costs.** Time, foregone alternatives are real opportunity costs even without money exchange.
5. **Not knowing PPC shape interpretation.** Concave (bowed outward) = increasing opportunity costs (typical). Linear = constant opportunity costs (rare).
6. **Confusing PPC shifts.** Outward = more growth/resources/tech. Inward = loss/decline. Pivot = change in only one good.
7. **Saying we should produce on the PPC.** We should produce ON or as close to ON as possible (efficiency). Inside is wasteful.
8. **Ignoring the PPC's constraint.** A point OUTSIDE the PPC is impossible without growth.
9. **Confusing capital with money.** In economics, capital = physical capital (machines, buildings, tools), not financial capital (stocks, money).
10. **Calling all decisions "marginal."** Some decisions are NOT marginal (must do entire project or none — e.g., building a bridge). But many are: should I do one more hour, one more unit, one more year.
11. **Missing the terms-of-trade requirement.** Both parties must benefit; terms must fall between their opportunity costs.
12. **Treating economic systems as pure.** Most modern economies are MIXED — some markets, some government. Pure market or pure command are rare.
13. **Using total cost when marginal cost is needed.** Decisions are made at the margin.
14. **Forgetting opportunity cost is per-unit.** "What's the opportunity cost?" must be specified per unit.
15. **Conflating efficiency and equity.** PPC shows efficient production (technical efficiency). Says nothing about who gets what (distributive justice).

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For PPC questions:**
- Sketch the curve correctly (concave for increasing opportunity costs).
- Label points (efficient, inefficient, impossible).
- Show shifts correctly for given changes.

**For comparative advantage questions:**
- Compute opportunity costs correctly.
- Identify who has comparative advantage in WHICH good.
- State who should specialize.
- Identify trade ratio that benefits both.

**For marginal analysis:**
- Compare marginal benefit to marginal cost.
- Decision rule: do it if MB ≥ MC.
- Optimal at MB = MC.

**For opportunity cost:**
- Identify what is given up (the next-best alternative).
- Include implicit costs (time, foregone activities).

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The opportunity cost of [Choice A] is the value of [the next-best alternative foregone]."
2. "Country X has COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE in [good] because its opportunity cost of producing [good] is LOWER than the alternative country's. The opportunity cost is [calculation]."
3. "Trade benefits both parties when terms of trade fall between their respective opportunity costs. In this case, between [X's OC] and [Y's OC] of [good]."
4. "An outward shift in the PPC reflects [economic growth/improved technology/more resources], allowing more production of both goods."
5. "Marginal analysis: the firm should produce additional units as long as marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost. The optimal output is where MR = MC."
6. "The PPC bows outward (concave) because of increasing opportunity costs — resources are not perfectly substitutable, so as more of one good is produced, each additional unit requires sacrificing more of the other good."
7. "Even though Country X has absolute advantage in both goods, both countries can BENEFIT from trade when each specializes in its comparative advantage and trades for the other good."
