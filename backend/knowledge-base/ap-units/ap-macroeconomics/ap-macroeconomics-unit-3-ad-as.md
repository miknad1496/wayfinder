## Big Ideas

1. **AD-AS is the central macroeconomic model.** It shows the relationship between price level (vertical axis) and real GDP (horizontal axis) for the entire economy. Every macroeconomic shock — fiscal stimulus, oil price shock, financial crisis, productivity gain — can be analyzed as a shift in AD or AS.
2. **Aggregate Demand slopes downward for three reasons.** Wealth effect (lower prices → real wealth rises → more spending); interest rate effect (lower prices → less money demanded → lower interest rates → more investment/consumption); foreign trade effect (lower domestic prices → exports more competitive → NX rises).
3. **Short-Run Aggregate Supply slopes upward; Long-Run Aggregate Supply is vertical.** SRAS reflects price-output relationship when wages and other costs are sticky. LRAS reflects potential output (full employment) — independent of price level.
4. **Recessionary gap = actual GDP below potential GDP. Inflationary gap = actual GDP above potential.** Recessionary gaps require expansionary policy (boost AD or wait for self-correction). Inflationary gaps require contractionary policy (reduce AD).
5. **Fiscal policy works through aggregate demand.** Government spending and tax changes shift AD. Spending multiplier amplifies the effect. Tax multiplier is smaller (in absolute value) than spending multiplier because some tax cuts are saved.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Aggregate Demand (AD):** total demand for all final goods and services in an economy at various price levels. Same components as GDP: AD = C + I + G + NX.
- **Why AD slopes downward** (three effects):
  - **Wealth effect (real balances effect):** lower price level → real value of money/assets rises → people feel wealthier → spend more → AD rises.
  - **Interest rate effect:** lower price level → less money needed → lower interest rates → more investment and interest-sensitive consumption → AD rises.
  - **Net export effect (foreign exchange effect):** lower domestic prices → exports more competitive abroad, imports less competitive at home → NX rises → AD rises.
- **Shifters of AD:**
  - **Changes in C (consumption):** consumer confidence, wealth (housing prices, stock prices), taxes (lower taxes → more spending), interest rates (consumer borrowing).
  - **Changes in I (business investment):** business confidence, interest rates, taxes on businesses, technological change.
  - **Changes in G (government spending):** fiscal policy decisions.
  - **Changes in NX (net exports):** foreign income (more → more exports), exchange rates (weak dollar → more exports).
  - **Changes in money supply** (technically through interest rates, but often discussed as an AD shifter).
- **Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS):** total supply of all final goods and services at various price levels in the SHORT RUN (when wages and prices are sticky).
- **Why SRAS slopes upward:**
  - In the short run, wages and other input prices are STICKY (don't adjust quickly).
  - When output prices rise, firms get higher revenue but pay the same wages → profits rise → firms produce more.
  - Higher prices, more output → upward-sloping SRAS.
- **Shifters of SRAS:**
  - **Input prices** (wages, raw materials, energy): higher input prices → SRAS shifts LEFT (decrease).
  - **Productivity:** higher productivity → SRAS shifts RIGHT (increase).
  - **Government regulations and taxes on businesses:** more burdensome → SRAS shifts LEFT.
  - **Number of suppliers in the economy:** more suppliers → SRAS shifts RIGHT.
- **Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS):** total output the economy can produce at FULL EMPLOYMENT. Vertical line.
- **Why LRAS is vertical:**
  - In the long run, wages and prices fully adjust to inflation.
  - Output is determined by productive capacity (resources, technology), not by price level.
  - LRAS represents POTENTIAL output / full-employment output.
- **Shifters of LRAS:**
  - Increase in resources (labor force growth, more capital, more natural resources).
  - Improvements in technology.
  - Improvements in productivity.
  - Education and skill improvements.
  - LRAS is essentially the macro equivalent of the PPC's outward shift.
- **AD-AS equilibrium:**
  - **Short-run equilibrium:** where AD = SRAS. Establishes price level and real GDP in the short run.
  - **Long-run equilibrium:** where AD = SRAS = LRAS. Economy at full employment with stable price level.
- **Output gaps:**
  - **Recessionary gap:** actual GDP < potential GDP (below LRAS). Cyclical unemployment present.
  - **Inflationary gap:** actual GDP > potential GDP (above LRAS). Economy "overheated"; inflation rising.
- **Fiscal policy:** government use of spending and taxation to influence AD.
  - **Expansionary fiscal policy:** increase G or cut taxes → AD shifts RIGHT. Used to fight recession.
  - **Contractionary fiscal policy:** decrease G or raise taxes → AD shifts LEFT. Used to fight inflation.
- **Multipliers:**
  - **Spending multiplier** = 1 / (1 − MPC), where MPC = marginal propensity to consume.
  - **Tax multiplier** = MPC / (1 − MPC) = spending multiplier × MPC. Negative if cutting taxes increases AD (positive shift).
  - Spending multiplier > tax multiplier in absolute value because some tax cut is saved.
  - Example: if MPC = 0.8, spending multiplier = 1/(1 − 0.8) = 5; tax multiplier = 0.8/(1 − 0.8) = 4.
- **Automatic stabilizers:** government programs that automatically increase spending or reduce taxes during recession (and vice versa during boom).
  - Examples: unemployment insurance (rises during recession), progressive income tax (collects less when incomes fall), means-tested programs (Medicaid, food stamps — more eligible during recession).
  - Don't require legislative action — kick in automatically with economic conditions.

### Adds for [4]

- **Self-correcting mechanism (long-run adjustment):**
  - **In a recessionary gap:** unemployment is high. Workers eventually accept lower wages (or accept layoffs without seeking equivalent pay elsewhere). Lower wages reduce business input costs → SRAS shifts RIGHT → economy returns to LRAS at lower price level.
  - **In an inflationary gap:** unemployment is below natural rate. Workers demand higher wages. Higher wages increase input costs → SRAS shifts LEFT → economy returns to LRAS at higher price level.
  - **Speed:** the self-correcting mechanism can be slow (years) because wages are sticky downward.
- **Demand-pull inflation:** AD increases (shifts right) → price level rises and output rises (in the short run) → inflation. Caused by excess demand.
- **Cost-push inflation (supply shock):** SRAS decreases (shifts left, e.g., oil price shock) → price level rises and output FALLS. Stagflation = simultaneous inflation and unemployment.
- **Showing fiscal policy on AD-AS:**
  - Expansionary: AD shifts right. Effect: price level rises, real GDP rises (from recessionary gap toward potential).
  - Contractionary: AD shifts left. Effect: price level falls, real GDP falls (from inflationary gap toward potential).
- **Showing supply shocks on AD-AS:**
  - Negative supply shock (oil price spike, natural disaster): SRAS shifts left. Effect: price level rises, real GDP falls. STAGFLATION.
  - Positive supply shock (technological breakthrough, productivity gain): SRAS shifts right (and possibly LRAS too). Effect: price level falls, real GDP rises.
- **Multiplier effect mechanics:**
  - Initial spending = $100. MPC = 0.8.
  - Recipient spends $80, saves $20.
  - That $80 becomes income for someone else; they spend $64, save $16.
  - That $64 becomes income for someone else; they spend $51.20, save $12.80.
  - Total cumulative effect (geometric series): $100 × 1/(1 − 0.8) = $100 × 5 = $500.
  - Spending multiplier = 5.
- **Crowding out** (preview of Unit 5):
  - Expansionary fiscal policy → government borrows more → increases demand for loanable funds → interest rates rise → reduces business investment.
  - The reduction in investment partially OFFSETS the fiscal stimulus.
  - The "crowding out" effect.
- **Lags in fiscal policy:**
  - **Recognition lag:** time to recognize a recession.
  - **Decision/legislative lag:** time for Congress to pass legislation.
  - **Implementation lag:** time for spending or tax changes to take effect.
  - Combined lags can be 6+ months, making fiscal policy slow to respond.
- **Discretionary vs automatic fiscal policy:**
  - **Discretionary:** Congress passes specific legislation (stimulus checks, infrastructure spending).
  - **Automatic:** built-in features (progressive taxes, unemployment benefits) automatically respond to economic conditions.
- **Calculating output gap and required policy:**
  - Output gap = (Actual GDP − Potential GDP).
  - To close a recessionary gap, government spending needed = (Output gap) / Spending multiplier.
  - Example: $100 billion recessionary gap, MPC = 0.75, spending multiplier = 4. Required government spending = $100 / 4 = $25 billion.

### Adds for [5]

- **The Keynesian vs Classical debate.** Classical economists (pre-Keynes) believed the economy self-corrects quickly through wage flexibility. Keynes (1936, *General Theory*) argued wages are sticky, so the economy can be stuck in recession for extended periods, requiring active government intervention (fiscal policy) to restore full employment. Modern macroeconomic policy treats Keynes's insight as essentially correct in the short run while accepting Classical insights about long-run adjustment.
- **Why wages are sticky downward.** Multiple reasons:
  - **Long-term contracts** with wage-setting commitments.
  - **Worker resistance** to nominal pay cuts (which feel like personal demotion even if real wages stay flat).
  - **Efficiency wages** (paying above market to attract/retain better workers).
  - **Unionization** (collective bargaining stickiness).
  - **Minimum wage laws** legally prevent some downward adjustment.
- **The crowding-out controversy.** Some economists argue crowding-out is large (fiscal stimulus is mostly offset by reduced private investment); others argue it's small (especially in recessions when private investment is already low). The empirical answer depends on:
  - **Phase of business cycle:** crowding-out larger in expansions, smaller in recessions.
  - **Type of spending:** investment in productive infrastructure may not crowd out private investment (and may complement it).
  - **Monetary policy response:** if Fed accommodates fiscal stimulus by holding rates low, crowding-out is minimized.
- **Why expansionary fiscal policy doesn't necessarily cause inflation.** In a recession with significant slack (high unemployment, idle capital), expansionary fiscal policy can boost output without significant inflation because there's plenty of capacity to absorb the increased demand. Inflation pressure builds when policy is excessive relative to economic capacity.
- **The "deficit hawks" vs "deficit doves" debate.** Hawks argue persistent deficits crowd out investment, raise long-term interest rates, and threaten future fiscal capacity. Doves argue deficits are appropriate during recessions and that the long-term costs are exaggerated. Modern economics has evolved to see deficits as more situationally appropriate than the hawks once argued (especially in low-interest, slack-economy periods).
- **Multiplier estimates in real economies.** Empirical estimates of fiscal multipliers vary by:
  - **Phase of business cycle:** larger multipliers in recessions (more idle capacity).
  - **Monetary policy stance:** larger when Fed holds rates low and accommodates.
  - **Type of spending:** infrastructure investment > tax cuts to wealthy (because spending impact is larger than savings impact).
  - **Open vs closed economy:** smaller in open economies (some spending leaks to imports).
  - Estimates range from 0.5 to 2.5 depending on circumstances.
- **The "automatic stabilizer" advantage.** Discretionary fiscal policy faces lags; automatic stabilizers respond instantly to economic conditions. Unemployment insurance, food stamps, progressive income taxes all kick in automatically during recession. They limit downturns without requiring Congressional action — a major reason modern economies experience milder recessions than pre-WWII economies.
- **The Phillips Curve preview (Unit 5).** AD shifts in AD-AS connect to inflation-unemployment trade-off (Phillips Curve). Demand-pull inflation = lower unemployment + higher inflation. Cost-push (supply shock) = stagflation = higher unemployment AND higher inflation simultaneously. The Phillips Curve in Unit 5 makes this relationship explicit.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Showing fiscal policy on AD-AS graph

The economy is in a recessionary gap (actual GDP < potential GDP). Government implements expansionary fiscal policy.

- **Step 1.** Initial AD-AS: AD intersects SRAS to the LEFT of LRAS — recessionary gap.
- **Step 2.** Expansionary fiscal policy: government increases G or cuts taxes.
- **Step 3.** AD shifts RIGHT.
- **Step 4.** New equilibrium: at higher price level AND higher real GDP. If well-calibrated, real GDP returns to LRAS (potential GDP).
- **Result:** recessionary gap closed; price level higher; full employment restored.
- **Graph labels:** initial AD as AD₁, new AD as AD₂; show shift with arrows.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Calculating spending multiplier and required policy

Economy has output gap of $200 billion (recessionary, actual below potential). MPC = 0.75.

(a) Calculate spending multiplier and tax multiplier.
(b) Calculate required government spending to close gap.
(c) Calculate required tax cut to close gap.

- **(a)** Spending multiplier = 1/(1 − 0.75) = 1/0.25 = 4. Tax multiplier = MPC/(1 − MPC) = 0.75/0.25 = 3.
- **(b)** Required government spending = Output gap / Spending multiplier = $200 / 4 = $50 billion.
- **(c)** Required tax cut = Output gap / Tax multiplier = $200 / 3 = $66.67 billion.
- **Insight:** $50 billion in spending closes the gap, but $66.67 billion in tax cuts is needed for the same effect. This is because some tax cut is saved (reducing the multiplier effect).

### Example 3 [4] — Supply shock and stagflation

Suddenly, oil prices triple due to international conflict. Economy was at full employment.

- **Step 1.** Initial AD-AS: AD intersects SRAS at LRAS (full employment equilibrium).
- **Step 2.** Negative supply shock: oil prices triple → input costs rise → SRAS shifts LEFT.
- **Step 3.** New equilibrium: price level RISES, real GDP FALLS.
- **Result:** **STAGFLATION** — simultaneous inflation and unemployment. Particularly nasty because expansionary policy that fights unemployment makes inflation worse, and contractionary policy that fights inflation makes unemployment worse.
- **Historical example:** 1970s oil crises caused exactly this in US and UK.
- **Policy response:** difficult. Some combination of waiting for adjustment, supply-side measures (energy independence), or accepting trade-off.

### Example 4 [4] — Long-run self-correction from inflationary gap

Economy is in inflationary gap (actual GDP > potential, unemployment below natural rate).

- **Initial state:** AD intersects SRAS to the RIGHT of LRAS. Price level high; output above potential.
- **Self-correction mechanism:** unemployment below natural rate creates upward wage pressure. Workers demand higher pay. Firms face higher input costs → SRAS shifts LEFT.
- **As SRAS shifts left:** price level rises further (in short run); output declines.
- **Long-run equilibrium:** SRAS continues shifting left until intersecting AD AT LRAS. Output returns to potential; price level higher than initial.
- **Implication:** inflationary gaps don't last; they self-correct through wage adjustment. The process can be slow and painful.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Comparing fiscal policy and monetary policy effects (preview of Unit 4)

A recessionary gap of $400 billion, MPC = 0.8.

(a) What expansionary fiscal policy could close this gap?
(b) What expansionary monetary policy could complement?
(c) What's the role of automatic stabilizers?

- **(a)** Spending multiplier = 1/(1 − 0.8) = 5. Required government spending = $400 / 5 = $80 billion. Or required tax cut = $400 / 4 (tax multiplier) = $100 billion.
- **(b)** Monetary policy: Federal Reserve cuts interest rates → lower borrowing costs → increased C and I (private investment) → AD shifts right (Unit 4 covers this in detail).
- **(c)** Automatic stabilizers: unemployment insurance increases as people lose jobs (boosts C); progressive taxes collect less as incomes fall (boosts disposable income); food stamps and Medicaid spending rise. These mitigate the recession automatically without legislative action.
- **Combined effect:** total economic stabilization combines discretionary fiscal policy (Congressional action), monetary policy (Fed), and automatic stabilizers (built-in mechanisms).

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing AD with demand curve from microeconomics.** AD is for the WHOLE economy at various price levels. Microeconomic demand is for a specific good at various prices.
2. **Wrong direction of AD slope explanation.** AD slopes downward because of wealth, interest rate, and net export effects. NOT diminishing marginal utility (that's micro).
3. **Mixing up SRAS and LRAS.** SRAS slopes upward (short-run, sticky wages). LRAS is vertical (long-run, full adjustment, potential output).
4. **Misidentifying gaps.** Recessionary = actual BELOW potential (left of LRAS). Inflationary = actual ABOVE potential (right of LRAS).
5. **Wrong spending multiplier formula.** Spending multiplier = 1/(1 − MPC). Tax multiplier = MPC/(1 − MPC).
6. **Forgetting sign of tax multiplier.** When government CUTS taxes, AD shifts RIGHT. So mathematically the tax multiplier acts negatively on the change.
7. **Showing AS shift instead of AD shift for fiscal policy.** Fiscal policy works through AD (government spending or tax changes affect aggregate demand).
8. **Forgetting to label graph properly.** Y-axis = price level. X-axis = real GDP. Curves clearly labeled (AD, SRAS, LRAS).
9. **Confusing demand-pull and cost-push inflation.** Demand-pull = AD shift right. Cost-push = SRAS shift left (typically with unchanged AD).
10. **Treating self-correction as immediate.** Wages are sticky; self-correction can take years.
11. **Forgetting crowding-out effect.** Expansionary fiscal policy → higher interest rates → reduces investment. Partial offset.
12. **Treating tax cut as fully spent.** Some tax cut is saved (only MPC × tax cut is spent), making tax multiplier smaller than spending multiplier.
13. **Misnaming components of AD.** C, I, G, NX are the same components as GDP.
14. **Confusing nominal GDP and real GDP on graph.** AD-AS uses REAL GDP on horizontal axis (price level on vertical axis).
15. **Treating LRAS as fixed.** LRAS shifts when potential output changes (more resources, better technology, productivity gains).

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For AD-AS graphing FRQs:**
- Draw the graph correctly: y-axis = price level (PL), x-axis = real GDP (Y).
- Label AD, SRAS, LRAS curves clearly.
- Label initial equilibrium price level and output.
- Show shift with arrows.
- Identify new equilibrium price level and output.

**For fiscal policy questions:**
- Identify type (expansionary or contractionary).
- Show as AD shift (right for expansionary, left for contractionary).
- Use multiplier to calculate required policy magnitude.
- Identify effect on output, employment, price level.

**For supply shock questions:**
- Identify as SRAS shift (left for negative shock, right for positive).
- Identify stagflation (negative shock = both higher prices and lower output).

**For self-correction:**
- Recessionary gap: SRAS shifts right (over time, wages fall).
- Inflationary gap: SRAS shifts left (over time, wages rise).
