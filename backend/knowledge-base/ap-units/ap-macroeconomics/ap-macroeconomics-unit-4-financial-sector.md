## Big Ideas

1. **Money serves three functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value.** Modern fiat money (no commodity backing) works because people accept it for these functions.
2. **Banks CREATE money through fractional reserve banking.** When a bank receives a deposit, it keeps a fraction in reserves and lends out the rest. The borrower deposits the loan elsewhere; the next bank lends out a fraction; etc. Total money created = initial deposit × money multiplier (1/reserve ratio).
3. **The Money Market shows nominal interest rate determination.** Money supply (vertical) intersects money demand (downward-sloping) to set the nominal interest rate. This is THE graph for analyzing monetary policy.
4. **The Federal Reserve uses three (or four) main tools to control money supply.** Open Market Operations (most important — buying/selling government securities), Reserve Requirement (rarely changed), Discount Rate (rate Fed charges banks), Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB — modern primary tool since 2008).
5. **Money Market = NOMINAL interest rate. Loanable Funds Market = REAL interest rate.** This distinction is THE most-violated concept in Unit 4. The Fed's monetary policy works through the Money Market (nominal rates); savings/investment through the Loanable Funds Market (real rates). Don't confuse them.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Three functions of money:**
  - **Medium of exchange:** money is accepted in exchange for goods and services, eliminating need for barter.
  - **Unit of account:** money provides a standard measure of value (prices in dollars, not bushels of wheat).
  - **Store of value:** money preserves purchasing power over time (though inflation erodes this).
- **Properties of good money:**
  - **Durable:** doesn't deteriorate easily.
  - **Portable:** can be carried.
  - **Divisible:** can be split into smaller units.
  - **Uniform:** all units identical.
  - **Limited supply:** scarcity gives it value.
  - **Acceptable:** people accept it as payment.
- **Types of money:**
  - **Commodity money:** has intrinsic value (gold, silver).
  - **Fiat money:** has value because government decrees it (US dollars). Modern money is fiat.
- **Money supply measures:**
  - **M1:** currency in circulation + checkable deposits + traveler's checks. The most liquid.
  - **M2:** M1 + savings deposits + small time deposits + money market funds. Broader measure.
  - **MB (monetary base):** currency + bank reserves at the Fed. The Fed directly controls MB.
- **Fractional reserve banking:**
  - Banks keep a FRACTION of deposits as reserves; lend out the rest.
  - **Required reserve ratio (RRR):** fraction banks MUST hold (set by Fed, currently 0% for most banks since 2020).
  - **Excess reserves:** reserves above the required minimum.
- **Money multiplier:**
  - **Money multiplier** = 1 / Reserve Ratio.
  - **Total money created** from initial deposit = Initial deposit × Money multiplier.
  - **Example:** $1,000 deposit, 10% reserve ratio. Bank holds $100, lends $900. Borrower deposits $900 elsewhere; that bank holds $90, lends $810. And so on. Total money supply increase = $1,000 × (1/0.10) = $10,000.
  - **Note:** real-world money multiplier is smaller than theoretical because some money leaks out (cash held outside banks; banks may hold excess reserves).
- **The Money Market graph** [ONE OF THE 5 MASTER GRAPHS]:
  - **Y-axis:** nominal interest rate (i).
  - **X-axis:** quantity of money.
  - **Money demand (MD):** downward-sloping. As interest rates rise, holding cash becomes more costly (opportunity cost is higher interest you could earn) → people demand less money.
  - **Money supply (MS):** vertical line (set by the Federal Reserve). Doesn't depend on interest rate.
  - **Equilibrium:** where MS intersects MD; sets the nominal interest rate.
- **Why money demand slopes downward:**
  - **Opportunity cost** of holding money is the interest you could earn elsewhere.
  - Higher interest rate → higher opportunity cost → less money held.
  - Lower interest rate → lower opportunity cost → more money held.
- **Shifters of money demand:**
  - **Real GDP / income** rises → more transactions → more money demanded → MD shifts RIGHT.
  - **Price level** rises → more money needed for same transactions → MD shifts RIGHT.
  - **Technology and payment innovations** (credit cards, mobile payments) → less money needed → MD shifts LEFT.
- **Shifters of money supply:**
  - The Fed controls money supply through monetary policy tools.
  - When Fed wants to INCREASE MS: buy securities (open market purchase), lower discount rate, lower reserve requirement, lower IORB.
  - When Fed wants to DECREASE MS: sell securities (open market sale), raise discount rate, raise reserve requirement, raise IORB.
- **Federal Reserve System:**
  - The CENTRAL BANK of the United States.
  - Created in 1913 (Federal Reserve Act).
  - Independent of direct political control (though chair appointed by president).
  - **Dual mandate:** maximum employment AND stable prices (added 1977 via Humphrey-Hawkins Act).
  - **Structure:** Board of Governors (7 members, including chair); 12 regional Federal Reserve Banks; Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) — key policy-making body.
- **Monetary policy tools:**
  - **Open Market Operations (most important historically):** Fed buys or sells US government securities (Treasury bonds).
    - **Buying securities** (open market purchase) injects money into banking system → MS rises → interest rates fall → expansionary.
    - **Selling securities** (open market sale) removes money → MS falls → interest rates rise → contractionary.
  - **Discount rate:** rate the Fed charges banks for short-term loans.
    - Lower discount rate → encourages bank borrowing from Fed → MS rises → expansionary.
    - Higher discount rate → contractionary.
  - **Reserve Requirement (rarely changed):** fraction of deposits banks must hold.
    - Lower reserve requirement → banks can lend more → MS rises → expansionary.
    - Higher reserve requirement → contractionary.
    - **Note:** Fed reduced this to 0% for most banks in March 2020. Discontinued as primary tool.
  - **Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB) — primary tool since 2008:** rate Fed pays banks for reserves held at the Fed.
    - Higher IORB → banks prefer holding reserves over lending → effective floor under interest rates → contractionary.
    - Lower IORB → banks prefer lending → expansionary.
- **Monetary policy effects on the economy:**
  - Fed cuts interest rate → cheaper to borrow → C and I rise → AD shifts right → output up, employment up, possibly inflation.
  - Fed raises interest rate → more expensive to borrow → C and I fall → AD shifts left → output down, employment down, inflation down.
  - **Expansionary monetary policy:** lower rates, fight unemployment.
  - **Contractionary monetary policy:** higher rates, fight inflation.

### Adds for [4]

- **The Money Market vs Loanable Funds Market — THE critical distinction** (per the brain file's signature):
  - **Money Market** (Unit 4): determines NOMINAL interest rate. Y-axis = nominal interest rate. X-axis = quantity of money. Federal Reserve directly controls money supply.
  - **Loanable Funds Market** (Unit 5): determines REAL interest rate. Y-axis = real interest rate. X-axis = quantity of loanable funds. Determined by saving (supply) and investment + government borrowing (demand).
  - **Why the distinction matters:** monetary policy works through the Money Market. Crowding out (Unit 5) works through the Loanable Funds Market. Confusing them produces wrong analysis on FRQs.
- **Open Market Operations mechanics:**
  - **Open market purchase** (expansionary):
    - Fed buys Treasury bond from private bank.
    - Bank receives money → bank reserves increase.
    - Bank can now lend more → money supply expands through money multiplier.
    - Increased money supply → lower interest rates.
  - **Open market sale** (contractionary): reverse of above.
- **The Federal Funds Rate** is the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. The Fed targets this rate as its main policy variable. When the Fed raises or lowers rates, this is typically the target.
- **Why MS is vertical in the Money Market.** The money supply doesn't change in response to interest rate changes — it's set by the Fed regardless of what interest rate the market offers. So MS is a vertical line at whatever quantity the Fed has chosen.
- **Why money demand slopes downward (transactions, asset, precautionary motives):**
  - **Transactions demand:** money needed for purchases. Doesn't depend much on interest rate.
  - **Asset demand:** holding money vs other assets. Higher interest rates → less money held as asset → less money demanded.
  - **Precautionary demand:** money for emergencies. Doesn't depend much on interest rate.
  - Combined: money demand slopes down because the asset demand portion responds to interest rates.
- **The transmission mechanism of monetary policy:**
  - Step 1: Fed buys bonds → bank reserves increase.
  - Step 2: Banks lend more → money supply expands.
  - Step 3: Higher money supply → equilibrium interest rate falls (Money Market).
  - Step 4: Lower interest rates → cheaper borrowing for businesses (I rises) and consumers (durable goods, housing).
  - Step 5: Increased C and I → AD shifts right.
  - Step 6: Higher AD → higher output, employment, possibly higher prices.
- **Why monetary policy might not work fully:**
  - **Liquidity trap:** when interest rates are near zero, additional money supply can't lower rates further. Federal Reserve faced this in 2008-2015 and 2020.
  - **Credit conditions:** banks may not lend even with abundant reserves if borrowers are risky.
  - **Confidence:** if households and businesses lack confidence, they may not borrow despite low rates.
- **Quantitative easing (QE):** unconventional monetary policy. Fed buys long-term securities (mortgage-backed securities, longer-term Treasuries) to push down long-term rates. Used 2008-2014 and 2020-2022.
- **Inflation targeting:** the Fed's commitment to keeping inflation around 2% per year. Provides a stable anchor for inflation expectations. Central to modern monetary policy.
- **Reserve requirement at 0%:** in March 2020, the Fed reduced the reserve requirement to 0% for most banks. This was partly because banks already held substantial excess reserves; required reserves were no longer the binding constraint. The reserve requirement is now essentially defunct as a policy tool.
- **The Fed's modern policy tools** (post-2008):
  - **Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB):** primary tool. Rate Fed pays banks for reserves.
  - **Open Market Operations:** still used to add/drain reserves.
  - **Forward guidance:** Fed's communication about future policy intentions affects expectations.
  - **Quantitative easing/tightening:** asset purchases/sales beyond traditional Treasury operations.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why the Money Market analysis uses NOMINAL interest rates.** Money is held for transactions today; what matters for the cost of holding it is the NOMINAL return foregone (what you could earn in nominal terms by investing). The opportunity cost of holding $1 of cash is the nominal interest rate it could have earned. Real interest rates matter for SAVING and INVESTMENT decisions (Loanable Funds Market) where future purchasing power is what's compared.
- **Why the Loanable Funds Market uses REAL interest rates.** Saving and investment decisions are about future purchasing power. Real interest rates measure the actual gain (or loss) in purchasing power from saving. Lenders care about real return; borrowers care about real cost.
- **The "neutrality of money" debate.** In the long run, monetary policy is largely "neutral" — changes in money supply primarily affect price level, not real variables (output, employment). In the short run (when prices and wages are sticky), monetary policy has real effects. This distinction explains why Fed acts to stabilize the business cycle but doesn't try to permanently boost output through monetary expansion.
- **The 2008 financial crisis and unconventional policy.** Traditional monetary policy hit the "zero lower bound" (interest rates couldn't go below zero — actually negative rates were tried in some other countries). Fed responded with:
  - **Quantitative easing (QE)**: massive purchases of long-term securities to push down long-term rates.
  - **Forward guidance:** commitment to keep rates low for extended period.
  - **Lender of last resort actions:** emergency credit programs.
  - These transformed the Fed's balance sheet from $900 billion (2008) to $4.5 trillion (2014).
- **The 2020 COVID monetary response.** Fed cut rates to near zero in March 2020. Implemented massive QE ($120 billion/month). Combined with fiscal stimulus, this enabled the rapid recovery and unfortunately contributed to inflation that emerged in 2021-2022.
- **Modern monetary policy and inflation.** The 2021-2022 inflation surge tested the Fed's policy framework. Fed initially called inflation "transitory" (expected to pass without aggressive response). When inflation persisted, Fed raised rates aggressively (2022-2023, fastest tightening cycle since the 1980s). The episode showed monetary policy's limits in addressing supply shocks (oil prices, supply chain disruptions, war in Ukraine) that drove much of the inflation.
- **Why the money multiplier in practice is less than theoretical maximum.** Theoretical money multiplier = 1/reserve ratio. Real-world is smaller because:
  - **Cash leakage:** some money is held as cash outside the banking system (doesn't get re-deposited and lent out).
  - **Excess reserves:** banks may hold more reserves than required, reducing lending.
  - **Demand for loans:** if borrowers don't want loans (recession, low confidence), banks can't lend out all available reserves.
- **The "fiscal-monetary mix" question.** Both fiscal policy (Unit 3) and monetary policy (Unit 4) can influence AD. Their interaction matters:
  - **Coordinated stimulus:** fiscal expansion + monetary expansion = strong push.
  - **Conflict:** fiscal expansion + monetary contraction can offset each other.
  - **2010s pattern:** weak fiscal response + aggressive monetary policy.
  - **2020 pattern:** strong fiscal AND strong monetary response → rapid recovery + inflation.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Money multiplier calculation

A bank receives a $5,000 deposit. The reserve requirement is 20%.

(a) Calculate the maximum money supply expansion.
(b) What if reserve requirement is reduced to 10%?

- **(a)** Money multiplier = 1/RR = 1/0.20 = 5. Maximum expansion = $5,000 × 5 = $25,000.
- **(b)** New money multiplier = 1/0.10 = 10. Maximum expansion = $5,000 × 10 = $50,000.
- **Insight:** lower reserve requirement → larger money multiplier → more money supply expansion from the same initial deposit.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Money Market graphing

The Federal Reserve conducts an open market PURCHASE of $10 billion in government bonds.

Show on Money Market graph: initial state, action, and new equilibrium.

- **Initial state:** Money Supply (MS₁, vertical) intersects Money Demand (MD, downward-sloping). Initial equilibrium nominal interest rate i₁.
- **Action:** Fed buys bonds → bank reserves increase → money supply expands.
- **Graph effect:** MS shifts RIGHT (from MS₁ to MS₂).
- **New equilibrium:** intersection of MS₂ and MD at lower nominal interest rate i₂.
- **Result:** money supply increases, nominal interest rate falls.
- **Onward effects:** lower interest rates → higher consumption and investment → AD shifts right (in the AD-AS graph).

### Example 3 [4] — Comparing Money Market and Loanable Funds (CRITICAL distinction)

A student writes: "When the Fed lowers interest rates, it shifts the loanable funds supply curve."

(a) What's wrong with this statement?
(b) State the correct relationship.

- **(a)** The Fed operates through the MONEY MARKET, not the loanable funds market. The Fed adjusts the MONEY supply, which determines the NOMINAL interest rate.
- **(b)** Correct relationship: the Fed shifts the MONEY SUPPLY in the Money Market, changing the NOMINAL interest rate. This is DIFFERENT from the Loanable Funds Market (Unit 5), where saving and investment determine the REAL interest rate.
- **Why the distinction matters:** monetary policy ⟶ Money Market ⟶ nominal rate. Saving/investment behavior ⟶ Loanable Funds Market ⟶ real rate. They're related but distinct.
- **Bridge:** in normal conditions, the Fed's nominal rate changes affect real rates because inflation expectations are relatively stable. So when Fed cuts rates, real rates also typically fall, affecting investment in the Loanable Funds Market.

### Example 4 [4] — Monetary policy in recession

The economy is in recession. Unemployment 8%. Inflation 1%.

(a) What monetary policy is appropriate?
(b) Show on Money Market graph.
(c) Show effect on AD-AS graph.

- **(a)** EXPANSIONARY monetary policy. Tools: open market purchase, lower discount rate, lower IORB.
- **(b)** Money Market: MS shifts RIGHT (Fed adds money supply). Nominal interest rate falls.
- **(c)** AD-AS: lower interest rates → cheaper borrowing → C and I rise → AD shifts RIGHT. Real GDP rises (toward potential), unemployment falls. Some upward pressure on price level.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Quantitative easing explained

In 2008-2014 and again in 2020-2022, the Fed implemented quantitative easing (QE).

(a) What is QE?
(b) Why was it needed?
(c) What were its effects?

- **(a)** Quantitative easing = Fed purchasing LONG-TERM securities (longer-term Treasuries, mortgage-backed securities) to lower long-term interest rates and provide liquidity to specific markets. Beyond traditional open market operations targeting short-term rates.
- **(b)** Traditional monetary policy was hitting the zero lower bound — the federal funds rate was at zero, but the economy needed more stimulus. QE was an unconventional way to provide additional accommodation.
- **(c)** Effects:
  - Lowered long-term interest rates (mortgages, business loans).
  - Increased asset prices (stocks, real estate).
  - Encouraged risk-taking by investors (search for yield).
  - Contributed to economic recovery in both 2008-2014 and 2020-2021 episodes.
  - Side effects: contributed to wealth inequality (asset owners benefited more); contributed to subsequent inflation (especially after 2020 episode).

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing Money Market and Loanable Funds Market.** Money Market = nominal rate, Fed-controlled supply. Loanable Funds Market = real rate, saving/investment determined.
2. **Drawing money supply curve as upward-sloping.** Money supply is VERTICAL (Fed sets it, doesn't respond to interest rate).
3. **Misnaming components of M1 vs M2.** M1 = currency + checkable deposits. M2 = M1 + savings + small time deposits + money market funds.
4. **Wrong money multiplier formula.** Money multiplier = 1/Reserve Ratio. NOT 1/(1 − Reserve Ratio).
5. **Treating reserve requirement as primary monetary policy tool.** It's now 0% for most banks (since March 2020). IORB and open market operations are primary.
6. **Wrong direction for open market operations.** Buy bonds = expansionary (injects money). Sell bonds = contractionary (drains money).
7. **Confusing federal funds rate with discount rate.** Federal funds rate = banks lending to each other overnight. Discount rate = Fed lending to banks.
8. **Treating Fed as part of executive branch.** The Fed is INDEPENDENT (chair appointed by president, but Board operates independently).
9. **Forgetting the dual mandate.** Maximum employment AND stable prices. Both, not one.
10. **Showing wrong direction of MS shift on graph.** Increase MS → MS shifts RIGHT. Decrease MS → MS shifts LEFT.
11. **Calculating money multiplier wrong direction.** Money multiplier = 1/RR. So lower RR means higher multiplier.
12. **Treating money demand as fixed.** Money demand shifts with real GDP, price level, and payment technology.
13. **Skipping the transmission mechanism.** Monetary policy works through: Fed action → reserves → bank lending → money supply → interest rates → C and I → AD. Skipping steps loses points.
14. **Confusing nominal and real interest rates in the Money Market.** Money Market explicitly uses nominal rates. Real rates are for Loanable Funds.
15. **Treating QE as standard monetary policy.** QE is UNCONVENTIONAL — used when traditional policy can't go further (zero lower bound).

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For Money Market graphs:**
- Y-axis = nominal interest rate (i).
- X-axis = quantity of money.
- MS = vertical line.
- MD = downward-sloping.
- Show shifts with arrows.
- Identify equilibrium point.

**For monetary policy questions:**
- Identify expansionary or contractionary.
- Specify the tool (open market operations, IORB, etc.).
- Show on Money Market graph (MS shifts).
- Connect to interest rate change.
- Connect to AD shift in AD-AS.

**For money multiplier:**
- Use 1/Reserve Ratio.
- Multiply by initial deposit for total expansion.
- Note real-world multipliers are smaller.

**For Money Market vs Loanable Funds:**
- Identify which market is being analyzed.
- Money Market = nominal rates, Fed controls supply.
- Loanable Funds Market = real rates, saving/investment determine.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The Federal Reserve conducts expansionary monetary policy by purchasing government securities, lowering the discount rate, or lowering the interest rate on reserve balances. These actions increase the money supply, shifting the MS curve to the right in the Money Market and lowering the nominal interest rate."
2. "The Money Market determines the NOMINAL interest rate, where the vertical Money Supply (set by the Fed) intersects the downward-sloping Money Demand. The Loanable Funds Market (Unit 5) determines the REAL interest rate."
3. "The money multiplier equals 1/Reserve Ratio. With a [X%] reserve requirement, the money multiplier is [calculation], so a $[Y] deposit can theoretically expand the money supply by $[Y × multiplier]."
4. "Money demand slopes downward because higher interest rates increase the opportunity cost of holding money — people choose to hold less money and more interest-bearing assets when rates rise."
5. "The transmission mechanism of monetary policy: Fed action → bank reserves change → money supply changes → nominal interest rate changes → cost of borrowing changes → consumption and investment change → AD shifts → output and price level adjust."
6. "Quantitative easing (QE) is unconventional monetary policy used when the federal funds rate is at the zero lower bound. The Fed purchases long-term securities to lower long-term interest rates and provide liquidity beyond what traditional open market operations achieve."
7. "The Federal Reserve has a DUAL MANDATE: maximum employment and stable prices. Monetary policy is calibrated to balance both goals — easing when unemployment is high (regardless of inflation), tightening when inflation rises (regardless of employment situation)."
