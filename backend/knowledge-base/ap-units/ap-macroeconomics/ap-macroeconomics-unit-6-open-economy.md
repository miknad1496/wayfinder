## Big Ideas

1. **The Foreign Exchange Market sets currency exchange rates.** Like other markets, FX has supply and demand: demand for the dollar comes from people wanting to buy US goods/assets; supply comes from US dollar holders wanting foreign currency. Equilibrium sets the exchange rate.
2. **Currency appreciation HURTS exports and HELPS imports.** A stronger dollar means foreigners need more of their currency to buy US goods (US exports become more expensive abroad → exports fall) AND Americans need fewer dollars to buy foreign goods (imports become cheaper → imports rise). Net exports fall.
3. **Currency depreciation HELPS exports and HURTS imports.** Reverse of above.
4. **Interest rate changes drive currency movements through capital flows.** Higher US interest rates attract foreign capital seeking higher returns → demand for dollars rises → dollar appreciates → net exports fall. This is how monetary policy affects exchange rates and trade.
5. **Balance of payments must balance.** Current account (trade in goods, services, income) and capital/financial account (flows of capital) must offset each other. A current account deficit (importing more than exporting) requires a capital account surplus (foreigners investing in your country).

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Balance of payments:** record of all economic transactions between a country and the rest of the world.
- **Two main accounts:**
  - **Current account:** trade in goods and services, plus net income from abroad and net unilateral transfers.
    - **Trade balance** = Exports of goods and services − Imports of goods and services.
    - **Net income** from foreign investments, etc.
    - **Net transfers** (foreign aid, remittances).
  - **Capital and financial account:** flows of financial assets (foreign investment in US, US investment abroad).
- **Balance principle:** Current Account + Capital and Financial Account = 0 (must balance).
  - **Current account deficit** (US imports more than exports) = **Capital and financial account surplus** (foreigners investing in US makes up the difference).
  - **Current account surplus** = **Capital account deficit** (US sending more capital abroad than receiving).
- **Foreign Exchange Market (FX)** [ONE OF THE 5 MASTER GRAPHS]:
  - Where currencies are exchanged.
  - **Y-axis:** exchange rate (price of one currency in terms of another, e.g., dollars per euro).
  - **X-axis:** quantity of currency.
  - **Demand curve:** downward-sloping. Demand for dollars by foreigners (to buy US goods, services, assets).
  - **Supply curve:** upward-sloping. Supply of dollars (Americans wanting to convert dollars to foreign currency).
  - **Equilibrium:** where supply = demand, sets the exchange rate.
- **Currency appreciation and depreciation:**
  - **Appreciation:** currency rises in value (you can buy MORE foreign currency per unit of your currency).
  - **Depreciation:** currency falls in value (you can buy LESS foreign currency per unit).
  - **Strong dollar = appreciated dollar.** Weak dollar = depreciated dollar.
- **What causes appreciation of US dollar:**
  - **Higher US interest rates** → foreigners want to invest in US → demand for dollars rises → dollar appreciates.
  - **US growth outpaces foreign growth** → foreigners want US assets → demand rises.
  - **Lower foreign interest rates** → foreigners pull capital from foreign countries to US.
  - **Increased US exports demand** (from foreigners wanting US goods) → demand for dollars rises.
  - **Reduced US imports demand** → fewer dollars supplied to FX market.
- **What causes depreciation of US dollar:**
  - Reverse of above. Lower US interest rates, etc.
- **Effect of exchange rates on trade:**
  - **Strong dollar (appreciation):**
    - US exports become MORE expensive abroad → exports FALL.
    - US imports become CHEAPER → imports RISE.
    - **Net exports DECREASE.**
  - **Weak dollar (depreciation):**
    - US exports become CHEAPER abroad → exports RISE.
    - US imports become MORE expensive → imports FALL.
    - **Net exports INCREASE.**
- **The brain file's specific flag:** "Strong dollar HURTS exports (NX falls)." This is the single most-tested counterintuitive insight in Unit 6.
- **Floating vs fixed exchange rates:**
  - **Floating (most major currencies, including USD):** exchange rate determined by market.
  - **Fixed (managed):** government commits to maintain a specific exchange rate. Requires central bank intervention to hold it.
  - **Pegged:** fixed against another currency (often US dollar — Hong Kong, several Middle Eastern countries).
- **Government intervention in FX:**
  - Central banks can buy or sell currency to influence exchange rate.
  - **To weaken own currency:** sell own currency, buy foreign.
  - **To strengthen own currency:** buy own currency, sell foreign.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why dollar demand is downward-sloping in FX market.**
  - At HIGH dollar exchange rate: dollars are expensive in foreign currency. Foreigners need lots of their currency to buy each dollar → buy fewer US goods/assets → less demand for dollars.
  - At LOW dollar exchange rate: dollars are cheap in foreign currency. Foreigners need less of their currency per dollar → buy more US goods/assets → more demand for dollars.
- **Why dollar supply is upward-sloping in FX market.**
  - At HIGH dollar exchange rate: each dollar buys a lot of foreign currency. Americans want to convert dollars to foreign currency to buy foreign goods/assets → more dollars supplied.
  - At LOW dollar exchange rate: each dollar buys less foreign currency. Americans buy fewer foreign goods → fewer dollars supplied.
- **Showing FX changes on graph:**
  - **Increased demand for dollars:** D shifts RIGHT. Dollar appreciates (rises). Quantity of dollars exchanged rises.
  - **Increased supply of dollars:** S shifts RIGHT. Dollar depreciates (falls). Quantity rises.
  - **Decreased demand:** D shifts LEFT. Dollar depreciates.
  - **Decreased supply:** S shifts LEFT. Dollar appreciates.
- **Connection to monetary policy (synthesizing Units 4 and 6):**
  - Fed RAISES rates → US bonds more attractive to foreigners → foreigners demand MORE dollars to buy US bonds → demand for dollars increases → dollar APPRECIATES → net exports FALL.
  - Fed LOWERS rates → reverse → dollar DEPRECIATES → net exports RISE.
  - This means monetary policy works through TWO channels:
    1. Domestic interest rate effects on C and I (Unit 4).
    2. Exchange rate effects on net exports (Unit 6).
- **Connection to fiscal policy (synthesizing Units 3 and 6):**
  - Fiscal stimulus → increased AD → upward pressure on interest rates → dollar appreciates → net exports fall (partial offset).
  - This is the "twin deficit" hypothesis: budget deficit and trade deficit may rise together because fiscal expansion attracts foreign capital.
- **Marshall-Lerner condition** (more advanced concept, sometimes referenced):
  - For depreciation to improve net exports, the elasticities of import and export demand must be sufficient.
  - In short run, elasticities may be low (J-curve effect: trade balance can WORSEN before it improves after depreciation).
  - In long run, elasticities are higher; depreciation typically does improve net exports.
- **Real exchange rate** = (nominal exchange rate × foreign price level) / domestic price level.
  - Adjusts nominal rates for differential inflation.
  - Better measure of true competitiveness.
  - High inflation in US relative to trading partners → real depreciation (less competitive over time).
- **Purchasing power parity (PPP)** — long-run theory that exchange rates adjust to equalize purchasing power across countries. The Big Mac Index is a popular informal application. PPP holds approximately in long run; less reliably in short run.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why the dollar's role as world reserve currency matters.** The US dollar serves as the dominant currency for international trade and central bank reserves. Benefits to US:
  - **Lower borrowing costs** because foreigners hold US debt.
  - **"Exorbitant privilege"** of issuing the global currency (can run sustained deficits).
  - **Sanctions power** through dollar-denominated payment systems.
  - Costs:
  - **Stronger dollar** than would otherwise prevail (hurts US exports).
  - **Vulnerability to challenges** to dollar dominance (China's CIPS, BRICS currency proposals).
- **The "impossible trinity" of international finance.** A country can choose at most TWO of:
  - **Free capital mobility** (no controls on capital flows).
  - **Fixed exchange rate.**
  - **Independent monetary policy.**
  - The US has free capital mobility and independent monetary policy → must accept floating exchange rate. China has fixed (managed) exchange rate and (partial) capital controls → can have somewhat independent monetary policy.
- **Why fiscal stimulus can have weaker effects in open economy.** Some of the fiscal multiplier "leaks" to imports — Americans buy more foreign goods rather than just domestic. This is why the multiplier is lower in highly open economies than in closed ones.
- **The "twin deficit" hypothesis** — claim that government budget deficits and trade deficits rise together. Mechanism: fiscal stimulus raises interest rates, attracts foreign capital, appreciates currency, hurts net exports. Empirical evidence is mixed but the theory has motivated much policy debate (especially during Reagan years when both deficits grew).
- **The "savings-investment identity" in open economy.** Saving must equal investment + net exports.
  - **Closed economy:** S = I.
  - **Open economy:** S = I + NX.
  - **Implication:** if domestic saving is less than investment, the difference must come from foreign borrowing (NX < 0 means net capital inflow).
  - The US has run persistent trade deficits because domestic saving is low relative to investment opportunities.
- **Why China's growth coincided with US trade deficits.** China's high savings (consumer culture less developed, fewer social safety nets meant precautionary saving). Excess savings flowed to US via Chinese central bank purchases of US Treasuries. Effect: kept US interest rates low, kept Chinese yuan weak, allowed China to export competitively. The US-China economic relationship illustrated how interconnected open economies have become.
- **Currency wars and competitive devaluation.** Countries may try to weaken their currencies to boost exports and growth at the expense of trading partners. This was a concern during the Great Depression (when many countries tried it simultaneously, hurting global trade) and has recurred in modern policy debates.
- **The 2010s era of low US trade deficits in services and high in goods.** Even as US goods trade deficits have grown, the US has run surpluses in many service sectors (financial services, tech services, IP licensing). Total trade deficit smaller than goods-only suggests.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — FX Market analysis

US Federal Reserve raises interest rates significantly while European Central Bank holds rates steady.

Show effect on FX market for dollar (vs euro).

- **Initial state:** Demand for dollars (D₁) intersects Supply of dollars (S₁) at initial exchange rate (e₁) and quantity (Q₁).
- **Action:** Higher US rates make US bonds more attractive to European investors.
- **Effect:** Demand for dollars rises (Europeans need dollars to buy US bonds). D shifts RIGHT (D₁ → D₂).
- **New equilibrium:** Higher exchange rate (e₂) — dollar APPRECIATES. Quantity exchanged rises (Q₂).
- **Result:** dollar appreciates relative to euro.

### Example 2 [3] — Effect on net exports

Following the dollar appreciation in Example 1, what happens to US net exports?

- **Step 1.** Stronger dollar means: Americans need fewer dollars to buy euros → European goods cheaper for Americans → US imports rise.
- **Step 2.** AND: Europeans need more euros to buy dollars → US goods more expensive in Europe → US exports fall.
- **Step 3.** Imports rise + exports fall → net exports (NX = X − M) fall.
- **AP-style answer:** "The appreciation of the dollar makes US exports more expensive in foreign markets and foreign imports cheaper for Americans. As a result, US net exports decrease — confirming that a strong dollar hurts US trade balance."

### Example 3 [4] — Monetary policy through TWO channels

The Federal Reserve cuts interest rates aggressively to fight a recession.

(a) Show effect through DOMESTIC channel (Money Market and AD-AS).
(b) Show effect through EXCHANGE RATE channel (FX Market and AD-AS).
(c) Both effects on AD.

- **(a) Domestic channel:**
  - Money Market: Fed buys bonds → MS shifts right → nominal interest rate falls.
  - AD-AS: lower rates → more C and I → AD shifts right.
- **(b) Exchange rate channel:**
  - FX Market: lower US rates → US bonds less attractive to foreigners → demand for dollars falls → dollar DEPRECIATES.
  - Trade effect: weaker dollar → US exports cheaper abroad → exports rise; foreign goods more expensive in US → imports fall.
  - NX rises.
- **(c) Combined:** AD shifts right both because of higher domestic spending (channel a) AND because of higher net exports (channel b). The total effect of monetary stimulus is amplified by the exchange rate channel.

### Example 4 [4] — Fiscal policy and "twin deficit"

The US implements a major tax cut and spending increase that significantly raises the federal budget deficit.

(a) Show effect on Loanable Funds Market.
(b) Show effect on FX Market.
(c) Show effect on net exports.

- **(a) Loanable Funds:** Government deficit increases → demand for loanable funds shifts right → real interest rate rises.
- **(b) FX Market:** Higher US real interest rates attract foreign capital → demand for dollars rises → dollar APPRECIATES.
- **(c) Net exports:** Stronger dollar → exports decrease, imports increase → net exports FALL.
- **The "twin deficit" connection:** fiscal expansion → higher rates → stronger dollar → trade deficit. Both deficits (budget and trade) move in the same direction. This was widely observed during Reagan-era fiscal expansion (1980s) and partially in 2017-2019 tax cuts.

### Example 5 [5] — Exchange rate "J-curve" effect

After a major dollar depreciation, US trade balance initially WORSENS for ~6-12 months before improving.

- **Why?** Two timing issues:
  - **Existing contracts:** trade contracts are typically set in advance with fixed prices in foreign currency. When dollar depreciates, US importers still pay the previously-agreed foreign currency amount — but it now costs MORE in dollars. So the dollar value of imports rises immediately.
  - **Quantity adjustment lag:** consumers and businesses take time to adjust purchasing decisions to new prices. New contracts reflect cheaper dollar; export quantity gradually rises.
- **The J-curve:** plotting trade balance over time after depreciation produces a J shape — initially trade balance falls (gets worse) before rising back above original level.
- **Implication:** depreciation's positive trade effects appear with significant lag. Politicians who promise "weak dollar will fix the trade deficit" should expect 1+ years before benefits show.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Reversing strong/weak dollar effects on exports.** STRONG dollar HURTS exports (NX falls). WEAK dollar HELPS exports (NX rises). Easy to flip.
2. **Misnaming components of balance of payments.** Current account = trade in goods/services + income + transfers. Capital/financial account = capital flows.
3. **Forgetting the balance principle.** Current account + Capital/financial account = 0 (excluding statistical discrepancy).
4. **Drawing FX market wrong.** Y-axis = exchange rate. X-axis = quantity of currency.
5. **Confusing appreciation and depreciation.** Appreciation = currency rises in value (stronger). Depreciation = currency falls in value (weaker).
6. **Using nominal vs real exchange rate incorrectly.** Real exchange rate adjusts for inflation. Nominal is the headline rate.
7. **Treating fixed and floating rates the same.** Fixed requires central bank intervention; floating is market-determined.
8. **Forgetting fiscal-monetary interaction with exchange rates.** Fiscal stimulus → higher rates → currency appreciates → NX falls. Monetary stimulus → lower rates → currency depreciates → NX rises.
9. **Conflating capital account and current account.** They're different.
10. **Showing wrong shifters of FX demand.** Higher US rates → demand for dollars rises. Higher US growth → demand rises. Higher US prices (vs foreign) → demand falls (Americans buy more imports, fewer exports demanded).
11. **Treating PPP as instant.** Purchasing power parity holds approximately in long run but rarely in short run.
12. **Ignoring the J-curve.** Depreciation's positive trade effects come with lag.
13. **Assuming exchange rates determine trade alone.** Trade depends on prices, quality, preferences, market access — not just exchange rates.
14. **Forgetting that exchange rates aren't manipulable in long run.** Even fixed-rate regimes ultimately yield to market pressures (currency crises).
15. **Treating reserve currency status as cost-free.** Dollar's reserve role brings benefits but also keeps dollar stronger than would otherwise prevail.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For FX Market graphs:**
- Y-axis = exchange rate (price of one currency in another).
- X-axis = quantity of currency.
- D = downward-sloping (foreigners buying that currency).
- S = upward-sloping (own residents converting to foreign currency).
- Show shifts with arrows.
- Identify appreciation/depreciation.

**For trade analysis:**
- Identify direction of currency change.
- Identify effect on exports and imports separately.
- Combine to determine NX direction.

**For monetary/fiscal policy with exchange rates:**
- Identify primary domestic effect.
- Identify secondary FX/trade effect.
- Recognize whether effects reinforce or offset.

**For balance of payments:**
- Identify components correctly.
- Apply balance principle.
- Recognize relationship between current and capital account.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "When [condition] occurs, demand for the dollar increases, shifting the demand curve right in the FX Market and causing the dollar to APPRECIATE."
2. "An appreciated (strong) dollar makes US exports more expensive in foreign markets, reducing demand for them, while making foreign goods cheaper for Americans, increasing imports. The result is a DECREASE in US net exports."
3. "Higher US interest rates attract foreign capital seeking higher returns, increasing demand for dollars and causing the dollar to appreciate. This appreciation hurts US net exports."
4. "Monetary policy works through two channels in an open economy: domestic interest rate effects on C and I, AND exchange rate effects on NX. Both channels reinforce each other when the Fed eases (lower rates → more domestic spending AND weaker dollar → more exports)."
5. "Fiscal expansion can lead to a 'twin deficit' situation where larger budget deficits coincide with larger trade deficits: fiscal stimulus → higher real interest rates (Loanable Funds Market) → currency appreciation (FX Market) → reduced net exports."
6. "The current account and capital/financial account must balance: a current account deficit (more imports than exports) is offset by a capital account surplus (foreigners investing in the country)."
7. "Currency depreciation typically improves the trade balance over time, but a J-curve effect means the trade balance initially worsens before improving — existing contracts and slow quantity adjustment delay the positive effects of depreciation."
