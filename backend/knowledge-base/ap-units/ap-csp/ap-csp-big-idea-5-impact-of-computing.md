## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 21–26% of the AP CSP exam
- **Sub-topics covered:** beneficial and harmful effects of computing; digital divide; computing bias; crowdsourcing; legal and ethical concerns; safe computing; privacy.
- **Where this Big Idea appears on the exam:** Beneficial vs harmful effects tested constantly. Digital divide. Computing bias. Privacy and security. The exam loves "discuss both positive AND negative" framings.

## Big Ideas

1. **Computing has both beneficial AND harmful effects** on individuals, communities, society.
2. **Digital divide:** unequal access to technology shapes opportunity.
3. **Computing bias:** algorithms can produce unfair outcomes.
4. **Crowdsourcing:** distributed work via Internet.
5. **Privacy, security, intellectual property** are major ethical/legal concerns.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Beneficial effects of computing:**
  - **Communication:** instant global connection.
  - **Access to information:** unprecedented (Wikipedia, search).
  - **Education:** Khan Academy, Coursera, online resources.
  - **Healthcare:** electronic records; telemedicine; AI diagnostics.
  - **Scientific discovery:** simulation, big data, AI.
  - **Productivity:** spreadsheets, word processors, cloud collaboration.
  - **Entertainment.**
  - **Civic engagement:** political organizing, awareness.
- **Harmful effects of computing:**
  - **Misinformation/disinformation:** spread of false information.
  - **Cyberbullying.**
  - **Privacy erosion:** surveillance by governments and corporations.
  - **Job displacement** by automation/AI.
  - **Mental health** impacts (especially social media).
  - **Addiction** to apps designed for engagement.
  - **Cybercrime:** scams, identity theft, ransomware.
  - **Echo chambers/polarization** in algorithmic feeds.
- **Digital divide:**
  - **Unequal access** to technology.
  - **Socioeconomic:** lower-income lack devices, broadband.
  - **Geographic:** rural areas often underserved.
  - **Global:** developed vs developing world.
  - **Generational:** younger more tech-fluent.
  - **Disability:** accessibility issues.
  - **Consequences:** access affects educational, economic opportunity.
- **Computing bias:**
  - **Algorithms reflect data they train on.**
  - **Biased data → biased algorithms.**
  - **Real-world examples:**
    - Facial recognition less accurate for darker skin (study by Joy Buolamwini).
    - Hiring algorithms biased against women (Amazon's scrapped tool).
    - Recidivism algorithms (COMPAS) biased against Black defendants.
  - **Hard to detect** without active auditing.
- **Crowdsourcing:**
  - **Distributed work via Internet.**
  - **Examples:** Wikipedia (knowledge), Kickstarter (funding), Yelp (reviews), Mechanical Turk (tasks), citizen science (eBird).
  - **Wisdom of crowds** vs **mob mentality.**
- **Citizen science:**
  - Public participates in scientific research.
  - **Examples:** eBird, GalaxyZoo, Foldit (protein folding).
- **Legal and ethical issues:**
  - **Intellectual property:** copyright protects creative works.
  - **Open source:** software with code freely available; modifiable.
  - **Creative Commons:** licenses for sharing creative work.
  - **Plagiarism:** using others' work without credit.
  - **Citing sources** essential.
- **Privacy concerns:**
  - **Data collection** by companies, governments.
  - **Tracking cookies** follow you across sites.
  - **Location data** from phones.
  - **Surveillance capitalism** (Shoshana Zuboff): business model based on extracting personal data.
  - **GDPR (EU)** and other privacy laws.
- **Computing security:**
  - **Strong passwords:** long, varied, unique per site.
  - **Two-factor authentication (2FA).**
  - **Phishing:** fake messages to steal credentials. Recognize and avoid.
  - **Malware:** viruses, trojans, ransomware, spyware.
  - **Encryption:** scrambles data so only authorized can read.
  - **Public-key cryptography** (asymmetric) underpins HTTPS.
- **Internet's effect on society:**
  - **Globalization** of communication, commerce.
  - **Disruption** of traditional industries (newspapers, retail, taxi, hotels).
  - **Political effects** (organizing, propaganda, elections).

### Adds for [4]

- **Why digital divide matters:**
  - **COVID-19 pandemic** exposed: kids without home Internet couldn't attend school.
  - **Job applications** increasingly online.
  - **Banking, healthcare, government services** moving online.
  - **Lack of access = exclusion.**
- **Why algorithm bias is hard:**
  - **Training data carries historical biases.**
  - **Algorithms amplify** patterns (including biased ones).
  - **Black box:** hard to explain how complex algorithms decide.
  - **Auditing required** but rarely mandated.
- **Open source vs proprietary:**
  - **Open:** Linux, Firefox, Wikipedia, many libraries.
  - **Proprietary:** Microsoft Windows, Apple macOS, most commercial software.
  - **Open source benefits:** transparency, modification, community.
  - **Proprietary benefits:** monetization, professional support.
- **PII (Personally Identifiable Information):**
  - Information identifying individual: name, SSN, phone, address, email, biometrics, IP.
  - **Should be protected.**
  - **Combinations** can identify even when individual pieces don't.
- **Safe computing practices:**
  - **Strong, unique passwords.**
  - **Password managers.**
  - **2FA enabled** wherever possible.
  - **Update software** regularly (patches).
  - **Verify before clicking** links/downloading.
  - **Backup important data.**
- **Symmetric vs asymmetric encryption:**
  - **Symmetric:** same key encrypts and decrypts. Fast. Need to share key securely.
  - **Asymmetric (public-key):** public key encrypts; private key decrypts. Don't share private key.
  - **HTTPS uses both** (asymmetric to exchange symmetric key).

### Adds for [5]

- **Why "both positive AND negative" framing.**
  - Computing has profound effects — rarely all good or all bad.
  - **Same technology** can liberate (organizers in dictatorships) or oppress (surveillance states).
  - **Critical thinking** about technology requires balanced view.
- **Why bias matters now.**
  - **Algorithms make consequential decisions:** hiring, lending, policing, healthcare.
  - **Bias scales** — one algorithm can affect millions.
  - **Active research and policy** addressing.
- **Why privacy matters.**
  - **Data is power.**
  - **Profiles enable manipulation** (advertising, propaganda).
  - **Chilling effect** on speech and behavior under surveillance.
  - **GDPR-style regulation** spreading.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Beneficial vs harmful

For each, identify mostly beneficial or mostly harmful (or both):
(a) Wikipedia.
(b) Online disinformation campaigns.
(c) Social media.

- (a) **Mostly beneficial** (free knowledge access).
- (b) **Mostly harmful** (deception, polarization).
- (c) **Both:** beneficial (connection, organizing, expression) AND harmful (mental health, polarization, addiction).

### Example 2 [3] — Digital divide

What is the digital divide?
- **Unequal access** to technology, broadband, digital skills.
- **By income, geography, age, ability.**
- **Consequences:** unequal access to education, employment, services, civic participation.

### Example 3 [4] — Algorithmic bias example

A hiring algorithm trained on past hiring data favors men. Why?
- **Past hiring** had bias toward men.
- **Algorithm learned** patterns from biased data.
- **Algorithm replicates** historical bias.
- **Even without explicitly using gender:** other features (e.g., college, hobbies) can correlate with gender.
- **Without auditing:** bias goes undetected.

### Example 4 [4] — Safe computing

What practices protect personal data online?
- **Strong, unique passwords** (use password manager).
- **Two-factor authentication.**
- **Update software** regularly.
- **Don't click unknown links** (phishing).
- **Use HTTPS sites** (encrypted).
- **Limit personal info** shared online.
- **Read privacy policies** (or use privacy-respecting services).

### Example 5 [5] — Crowdsourcing

How is Wikipedia an example of crowdsourcing?
- **Distributed contributors** worldwide write/edit articles.
- **No central editorial control.**
- **Quality through community moderation.**
- **Open source ethos.**
- **Wisdom of crowds:** many eyes catch errors.
- **Limits:** vandalism, expertise gaps, edit wars.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **One-sided framing.** Computing has both beneficial AND harmful effects. Discuss both.
2. **Underestimating digital divide.** Real, consequential, global.
3. **Forgetting bias.** Algorithms aren't neutral; they reflect their training data.
4. **Confusing copyright and Creative Commons.** Copyright restricts. CC licenses share.
5. **Confusing PII types.** Direct identifiers (SSN) AND combinations of indirect (zip+gender+DOB) can identify.
6. **Ignoring privacy.** Data collection is pervasive; few realize the extent.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For impact questions:**
- Discuss beneficial AND harmful effects.
- Connect to specific examples.

**For bias questions:**
- Identify source of bias (data, design, deployment).
- Address mitigation.

**For privacy questions:**
- Address what data is collected.
- Address by whom and for what.
- Address user protections.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Computing innovations have both beneficial effects (global communication, access to knowledge, scientific discovery, healthcare advances) and harmful effects (misinformation, cyberbullying, privacy erosion, job displacement, mental health impacts) — often the SAME technology produces both."
2. "The digital divide describes unequal access to technology and connectivity by income, geography, age, and ability — exposed starkly by COVID-19 when students without home Internet couldn't attend remote school."
3. "Algorithmic bias arises when training data reflects historical bias or non-representative samples. Examples include facial recognition less accurate for darker skin (Buolamwini), Amazon's hiring algorithm biased against women, and the COMPAS recidivism algorithm biased against Black defendants."
4. "Crowdsourcing uses distributed Internet contributors for tasks: Wikipedia (knowledge), Kickstarter (funding), eBird (citizen science), Wikipedia (collaborative editing). It harnesses 'wisdom of crowds' but risks vandalism and uneven expertise."
5. "Safe computing practices — strong unique passwords, two-factor authentication, regular software updates, recognition of phishing, encryption (HTTPS), and minimizing PII shared — protect against the substantial cybercrime and surveillance threats of modern computing."
