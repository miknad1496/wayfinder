## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 18–23% of the AP Physics 1 exam — among the heaviest units
- **Sub-topics covered:** work; kinetic energy; potential energy (gravitational, elastic); conservation of energy; power; springs and Hooke's Law.
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Energy methods are perennial. Conservation of energy is one of the most powerful problem-solving tools — often easier than kinematic + dynamics. Per the brain file: "Use ENERGY first when asked about FINAL SPEED — bypasses path; only initial + final states matter."

## Big Ideas

1. **Energy is conserved in isolated systems.** Total mechanical energy (KE + PE) constant when only conservative forces act.
2. **Work-energy theorem: net work = change in kinetic energy.** W_net = ΔKE.
3. **Conservative vs non-conservative forces.** Conservative (gravity, elastic): work doesn't depend on path. Non-conservative (friction): converts mechanical energy to heat.
4. **Power = rate of doing work.** P = W/t = F·v.
5. **Energy methods bypass detailed kinematics.** When asked about final speed (not time/path), conservation of energy is often easiest.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Work (W):** energy transferred when a force is applied over a displacement.
  - **W = F·d·cos(θ),** where θ is angle between force and displacement.
  - Units: joules (J = N·m).
- **Kinetic energy (KE):** energy of motion.
  - **KE = (1/2)mv².**
  - Always positive (or zero).
- **Work-Energy Theorem:** net work done on object = change in kinetic energy.
  - W_net = ΔKE = KE_f - KE_i.
- **Potential energy (PE):** energy stored due to position or configuration.
  - **Gravitational PE:** PE_g = mgh (height above reference).
  - **Elastic PE (spring):** PE_s = (1/2)kx² (spring constant k, displacement x from equilibrium).
- **Conservation of energy:** total energy of an isolated system is constant.
  - **Mechanical energy:** ME = KE + PE.
  - **For conservative forces only:** ME_i = ME_f.
- **Conservative forces:** work depends only on initial and final positions (not path). Examples: gravity, springs.
- **Non-conservative forces:** work depends on path. Examples: friction (converts ME to heat).
- **When friction acts:** ME_i = ME_f + heat dissipated by friction.
- **Power (P):** rate of doing work.
  - **P = W/t.**
  - **P = F·v** (instantaneous power for force in direction of motion).
  - Units: watts (W = J/s).
- **Hooke's Law:** spring force F = -kx (restoring force).
  - k = spring constant.
  - x = displacement from equilibrium.
  - Negative sign: force is opposite to displacement.

### Adds for [4]

- **When work is done:**
  - Force in direction of motion: positive work.
  - Force opposite to motion: negative work.
  - Force perpendicular to motion (e.g., normal force): zero work.
- **Why energy methods are powerful.**
  - Bypass time and path.
  - Just need initial and final positions/speeds.
  - Per brain file: "Use ENERGY first when asked about FINAL SPEED."
- **Energy bar charts (in some textbooks):**
  - Show energy distribution at different points.
  - Help visualize energy conservation.
- **Spring system at equilibrium:**
  - F = -kx → x = 0 means no spring force.
  - At maximum compression/extension: KE = 0, all energy in spring.
- **Inclined plane with energy:**
  - PE_g = mg·h, where h is vertical height.
  - For object on incline: h = distance along slope · sin(θ).
- **Friction's role:**
  - Friction does negative work on moving object.
  - Magnitude of work: |W_f| = F_f · d (force times distance).
  - Heat generated = |work done by friction| (when friction is sole non-conservative force).

### Adds for [5]

- **Why energy is so powerful for AP Physics.** Many problems can be solved EITHER with kinematics + Newton's Laws OR with energy. Energy is often cleaner because:
  - No time required.
  - No path information needed (only states).
  - Simple algebra.
- **The "FINAL SPEED" trick (per brain file).** When asked for final speed (not time, not path), conservation of energy is almost always the easiest method.
- **Power and efficiency.**
  - Real machines have efficiency < 100%.
  - **Efficiency = useful work output / energy input.**
  - Friction usually accounts for energy losses.
- **Why springs follow Hooke's Law.** Hooke's Law (F = -kx) is the linear approximation of a more complex restoring force. It works well for small displacements; departures occur for very large displacements.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Work-energy theorem

A 5 kg object initially at rest experiences a 20 N net force over 10 m. Find final velocity.
- W_net = F·d = 20·10 = 200 J.
- ΔKE = W_net = 200 J.
- KE_f = (1/2)·5·v² = 200 → v² = 80 → v ≈ 8.94 m/s.

### Example 2 [3] — Conservation of energy

A 1 kg ball is dropped from 20 m height. Find speed just before hitting ground.
- Initial: KE = 0, PE = mgh = 1·9.8·20 = 196 J.
- Final: KE_f = 196 J, PE_f = 0.
- (1/2)·1·v² = 196 → v² = 392 → v ≈ 19.8 m/s.
- (Same answer as kinematic approach but easier!)

### Example 3 [4] — Spring problem

A spring (k = 100 N/m) is compressed 0.2 m. A 0.5 kg ball is released. Find max speed.
- Spring PE = (1/2)kx² = (1/2)(100)(0.04) = 2 J.
- Conservation: spring PE = max KE.
- (1/2)·0.5·v² = 2 → v² = 8 → v ≈ 2.83 m/s.

### Example 4 [4] — Friction work

A 10 kg crate is pushed 5 m across floor (μ_k = 0.4) with constant velocity.
- Friction force: F_f = μ_k·N = 0.4·(10·9.8) = 39.2 N.
- Work by friction: W = -F_f·d = -39.2·5 = -196 J.
- (Negative because friction opposes motion.)
- Work by applied force = +196 J (since constant velocity, all goes to overcome friction).
- Heat generated: 196 J.

### Example 5 [5] — Power calculation

A motor lifts a 100 kg load 10 m in 5 seconds. Find the power.
- Work done: W = F·d = mg·d = (100·9.8)(10) = 9800 J.
- Power: P = W/t = 9800/5 = 1960 W.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Forgetting θ in work formula.** W = F·d·cos(θ).
2. **Wrong sign on potential energy.** PE_g = mgh, where h is HEIGHT above reference.
3. **Forgetting friction reduces mechanical energy.** ME_f < ME_i if friction acts.
4. **Using kinematic when energy easier.** For final speed problems, energy method usually faster.
5. **Wrong spring formula.** PE_s = (1/2)kx². NOT (1/2)kx.
6. **Forgetting Hooke's Law sign.** F = -kx (restoring force opposite to displacement).
7. **Misapplying power formula.** P = W/t (average) or F·v (instantaneous).
8. **Confusing work and energy units.** Both in joules (J = N·m).

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For energy problems:**
- Identify initial and final states.
- Apply conservation of energy (or work-energy theorem).
- Account for any energy losses (friction).

**For work problems:**
- Identify all forces.
- Compute work for each.
- Sum to get net work.

**For power problems:**
- Use P = W/t or F·v as appropriate.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "By the Work-Energy Theorem, the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: W_net = ΔKE."
2. "Conservation of energy: in the absence of non-conservative forces (like friction), total mechanical energy (KE + PE) is conserved. ME_i = ME_f."
3. "Power equals work per unit time: P = W/t. Instantaneous power for a force in the direction of motion: P = F·v."
4. "Gravitational potential energy: PE_g = mgh, where h is the height above the reference level."
5. "Elastic (spring) potential energy: PE_s = (1/2)kx², where k is the spring constant and x is the displacement from equilibrium."
