## Big Ideas

1. **Newton's Three Laws of Motion are the foundation of dynamics.**
   - 1st (Inertia): object at rest stays at rest; in motion stays in motion (constant velocity), unless acted on by net force.
   - 2nd: F_net = ma. Direction of acceleration matches direction of net force.
   - 3rd: For every action, there's an equal and opposite reaction. (Forces come in PAIRS, on DIFFERENT objects.)
2. **Free-body diagrams (FBDs) are essential.** Identify ALL forces on an object. Draw to scale. Use to apply Newton's 2nd Law.
3. **Forces are vectors.** Direction matters. Add as vectors.
4. **For circular motion, NET force points TOWARD center (centripetal).** This is NOT a separate force — it's the resultant of identifiable forces (gravity, tension, normal, etc.).
5. **The "5-sentence paragraph argument template" is the gold standard for AP Physics FRQs:** state law → identify forces → apply law → derive → interpret.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Newton's First Law (Inertia):** an object at rest stays at rest; in motion stays in motion (with constant velocity), UNLESS acted on by a net external force.
- **Newton's Second Law:** F_net = ma.
  - F_net in newtons (N); m in kg; a in m/s².
  - Force and acceleration are VECTORS in the same direction.
- **Newton's Third Law:** for every action, there's an equal and opposite reaction.
  - Forces come in PAIRS.
  - Each force in the pair acts on a DIFFERENT object.
- **Common forces:**
  - **Gravity (weight):** F_g = mg (downward at Earth's surface).
  - **Normal force (N):** perpendicular to surface, from surface pushing on object.
  - **Friction:** parallel to surface, opposing relative motion (kinetic) or potential motion (static).
  - **Tension (T):** along a rope or string, pulling.
  - **Applied force:** external force.
- **Friction:**
  - **Kinetic friction:** F_k = μ_k · N (in motion).
  - **Static friction:** F_s ≤ μ_s · N (at rest; can be less than maximum).
  - μ values typically 0 to 1; depend on surface materials.
- **Free-body diagrams (FBDs):** essential tool.
  - Show ONLY forces ACTING ON the object of interest.
  - Forces drawn as arrows from a single point.
  - Length proportional to magnitude.
  - Direction arrows show direction.
- **Newton's 2nd Law applied to FBD:**
  - Sum forces in each direction (x and y).
  - Set equal to ma in that direction.
  - Solve for unknowns.
- **Equilibrium (a = 0):** F_net = 0 in each direction.
- **Inclines (slopes):**
  - Tilt coordinate axes along incline.
  - Components of gravity: parallel to slope = mg·sin(θ); perpendicular = mg·cos(θ).
  - Normal force = mg·cos(θ).
- **Circular motion:**
  - Object moving in circle has centripetal acceleration TOWARD center.
  - **a_c = v²/r** (or ω²r, where ω is angular velocity).
  - **Net force toward center = m · a_c = mv²/r.**
  - **Centripetal force is NOT a separate force** — it's the NET force, provided by other forces (gravity, tension, normal, friction).
- **What provides centripetal force in different scenarios:**
  - Car turning: friction.
  - Ball on string: tension.
  - Earth orbiting Sun: gravity.
  - Vertical loop (top): gravity + normal.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why FBDs are essential.** Without correctly identifying ALL forces, can't apply Newton's 2nd Law correctly. Common error: forgetting normal force, friction, etc.
- **Vector addition of forces.**
  - F_x_net = sum of all x-components.
  - F_y_net = sum of all y-components.
  - Apply N2L in each direction: F_x_net = ma_x; F_y_net = ma_y.
- **Friction calculations:**
  - **Static** ≤ μ_s · N: object NOT moving but might.
  - **Kinetic** = μ_k · N: object IS moving.
  - μ_s typically > μ_k (static friction stronger than kinetic).
- **Inclined plane analysis:**
  - Coordinate system: rotate axes along incline.
  - **Down the slope:** mg·sin(θ).
  - **Perpendicular (normal direction):** mg·cos(θ).
  - **N = mg·cos(θ)** (in absence of other vertical forces).
  - **Sum forces along slope:** ma = applied - friction - gravity component.
- **Circular motion details:**
  - **a_c = v²/r:** centripetal acceleration. Direction: toward center.
  - **F_c = mv²/r:** centripetal force. Direction: toward center.
  - **Centripetal force is the NET force** — provided by physical forces (tension, gravity, normal, friction).
- **Common centripetal scenarios:**
  - **Car on flat curve:** friction provides centripetal force. Maximum speed = √(μgr).
  - **Banked curve:** normal force component provides centripetal force.
  - **Ball on string in horizontal circle:** tension provides.
  - **Vertical loop (top):** gravity + normal both downward = mv²/r.
  - **Vertical loop (bottom):** normal up = mg + mv²/r (downward gravity + upward normal balance to provide centripetal).
- **Universal Gravitation:**
  - **Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation:** F = G·m₁·m₂/r², where G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg².
  - **Earth's gravitational field:** g = G·M_E/r² ≈ 9.8 m/s² at surface.
  - **Force decreases with square of distance.**
- **Apparent weight:**
  - Weight you "feel" = normal force.
  - In free-fall (rollercoaster down ramp): apparent weight = 0 (weightlessness).
  - Accelerating up: apparent weight > true weight.
  - Accelerating down: apparent weight < true weight.

### Adds for [5]

- **The "5-sentence paragraph argument template" (per brain file's signature):**
  1. State the law/principle by name.
  2. Identify the relevant forces/quantities in this scenario.
  3. Apply the law: write the relationship.
  4. Derive/compare to get the conclusion.
  5. Interpret / connect to question asked.
  - This template structures every Physics 1 paragraph response.
- **Common AP Physics 1 conceptual errors:**
  - "Centripetal force" treated as separate force (it's the NET force from other forces).
  - At peak of toss: v = 0, a ≠ 0 (still -g).
  - Action-reaction (N3) pairs act on DIFFERENT objects.
  - Magnetic force does NO work (only changes direction).
- **Why N3 pairs act on different objects.**
  - Force pair: A pushes B with force F → B pushes A with force F (opposite direction).
  - These don't cancel — they act on different objects, so each one adds to its respective object's net force.
- **Limitations of FBDs.** Free-body diagrams typically assume objects can be treated as POINT masses. For extended objects, need to consider rotational effects (Unit 5).
- **Why static friction is "smarter" than kinetic.** Static friction adjusts to whatever force is applied (up to maximum). Kinetic friction is constant once motion begins.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Newton's 2nd Law

A 5 kg block experiences a 20 N applied force. What's its acceleration?
- F_net = ma → 20 = 5·a → a = 4 m/s².

### Example 2 [3] — FBD and equilibrium

A box rests on a horizontal surface. Identify forces in FBD.
- **Gravity (weight):** mg downward.
- **Normal force:** N upward (from surface pushing on box).
- **Equilibrium:** N = mg (no acceleration).

### Example 3 [4] — Friction problem

A 10 kg block on a horizontal surface (μ_k = 0.4) is pushed with a 50 N horizontal force.
- F_friction = μ_k · N = 0.4 · (10·9.8) = 39.2 N.
- F_net = 50 - 39.2 = 10.8 N.
- a = F_net/m = 10.8/10 = 1.08 m/s².

### Example 4 [4] — Inclined plane

A 5 kg block on a 30° incline (μ_k = 0.3) with no other forces. Find acceleration down slope.
- Gravity component along slope: mg·sin(30°) = 5·9.8·0.5 = 24.5 N.
- Normal force: N = mg·cos(30°) = 5·9.8·0.866 = 42.4 N.
- Friction: F_k = μ_k · N = 0.3 · 42.4 = 12.7 N (opposing motion, up slope).
- F_net = 24.5 - 12.7 = 11.8 N (down slope).
- a = 11.8/5 = 2.36 m/s².

### Example 5 [4][5] — Circular motion

A 0.5 kg ball on a 1.5 m string moves in a horizontal circle. Tension = 4 N. Find speed.
- Tension provides centripetal force.
- F_c = mv²/r → 4 = 0.5·v²/1.5 → v² = 12 → v = 3.46 m/s.
- **5-sentence template:**
  1. State Newton's 2nd Law for circular motion: F_c = mv²/r.
  2. Identify: tension provides centripetal force on ball.
  3. Apply: T = mv²/r.
  4. Derive: v = √(Tr/m) = √(4·1.5/0.5) = √12 = 3.46 m/s.
  5. Interpret: the ball moves at approximately 3.5 m/s in its circular path.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Forgetting to draw FBDs.** Skipping FBDs leads to errors in identifying forces.
2. **Treating "centripetal force" as separate force.** It's the NET force, provided by physical forces.
3. **Wrong direction for centripetal acceleration.** ALWAYS toward center.
4. **Forgetting Newton's 3rd Law pairs act on different objects.**
5. **Confusing static and kinetic friction.** Static (when at rest, ≤ μ_s·N); Kinetic (when moving, = μ_k·N).
6. **Wrong components of gravity on incline.** Down slope: mg·sin(θ); perpendicular: mg·cos(θ).
7. **Forgetting normal force changes on incline.** N = mg·cos(θ), not mg.
8. **Treating mass and weight interchangeably.** Mass is in kg (intrinsic). Weight is force (mass × g).

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For dynamics problems:**
- Always start with FBD.
- Use "5-sentence paragraph argument template" for paragraph responses.
- Apply Newton's 2nd Law in each direction.

**For circular motion:**
- Identify what provides centripetal force.
- Apply F_c = mv²/r toward center.

**For friction problems:**
- Distinguish static from kinetic.
- Always use F = μN.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "By Newton's Second Law, the net force on the object equals its mass times acceleration: F_net = ma. Identifying ALL forces in the FBD allows applying this law."
2. "The centripetal force is NOT a separate force; it is the NET force pointing toward the center of the circular path. In this scenario, [tension/gravity/friction/normal] provides the centripetal force."
3. "On an inclined plane at angle θ, the component of gravity along the slope is mg·sin(θ); perpendicular to the slope is mg·cos(θ). The normal force equals mg·cos(θ) (in the absence of other perpendicular forces)."
4. "Newton's Third Law: forces come in pairs, with equal magnitudes and opposite directions, ALWAYS acting on DIFFERENT objects. They don't cancel because they act on different bodies."
5. "Friction can be static (object at rest, F ≤ μ_s·N) or kinetic (object in motion, F = μ_k·N). Static friction adjusts to whatever force is applied, up to its maximum."
