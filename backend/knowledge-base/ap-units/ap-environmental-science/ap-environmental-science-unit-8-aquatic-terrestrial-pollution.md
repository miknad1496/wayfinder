# AP Environmental Science — Unit 8: Aquatic and Terrestrial Pollution — Wayfinder Teaching Brain

> Surfaces in chat when student mentions topics in this unit. Use to ground custom teaching responses + on-demand teaching guide generation.

## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 7–10% of the AP Environmental Science exam
- **Sub-topics covered:** point/non-point source pollution; eutrophication; oil spills; thermal pollution; sewage; solid waste; hazardous waste; bioaccumulation/biomagnification (review); endocrine disruptors; dose-response; LD50.
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Point vs non-point source. Eutrophication and dead zones. LD50 and dose-response. Solid waste options. Superfund sites.

## Big Ideas

1. **Point source pollution** comes from identifiable origin; **non-point** from diffuse sources.
2. **Eutrophication** from nutrient pollution causes algal blooms, dead zones.
3. **Solid waste** managed via landfills, incinerators, recycling, composting.
4. **Hazardous waste** subject to special handling (RCRA, CERCLA/Superfund).
5. **Toxicology** measures dose-response; LD50 = dose lethal to 50%.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Point vs non-point source pollution:**
  - **Point source:** identifiable origin (sewage outfall, factory pipe, oil spill).
  - **Non-point source:** diffuse, hard to trace (agricultural runoff, urban stormwater).
  - **Non-point typically harder to regulate.**
- **Water pollution types:**
  - **Pathogens:** disease-causing organisms (cholera, typhoid).
  - **Nutrients:** N and P from fertilizer, sewage.
  - **Sediment:** from erosion, construction.
  - **Toxic chemicals:** pesticides, heavy metals, industrial waste.
  - **Thermal:** warm water from power plants.
  - **Oil spills:** Deepwater Horizon (2010), Exxon Valdez (1989).
  - **Plastic:** macroplastics, microplastics.
- **Eutrophication:**
  - **Nutrient enrichment** of water (especially N, P from fertilizer, sewage).
  - **Algal bloom** (often cyanobacteria — "blue-green algae").
  - **Algae die, decompose;** decomposition uses oxygen.
  - **Hypoxia** (low O₂); fish die.
  - **Dead zones** form (Gulf of Mexico, Chesapeake Bay).
  - **Cultural eutrophication:** human-accelerated.
- **Sewage:**
  - **Treatment levels:**
    - **Primary:** physical removal of solids.
    - **Secondary:** biological breakdown of organic matter.
    - **Tertiary:** chemical removal of nutrients (N, P), pathogens.
  - **Untreated sewage** dumped in waterways causes disease, eutrophication.
  - **BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand):** measure of organic pollution; high BOD = high decomposer activity = low O₂.
- **Drinking water:**
  - **Pathogens:** treated by chlorination, UV, ozone, filtration.
  - **Lead:** old pipes (Flint Water Crisis 2014).
  - **Nitrates:** from fertilizer; can cause "blue baby syndrome."
  - **Heavy metals:** mercury, arsenic.
- **Solid waste:**
  - **Landfills:** most common in US; sealed; capture leachate, methane.
  - **Sanitary landfill:** lined to prevent leachate; vented to manage methane.
  - **Incinerators:** burn waste; reduce volume; energy recovery; emissions concerns (dioxins).
  - **Recycling:** reduces virgin material need; varies by material.
  - **Composting:** biological breakdown of organic waste.
  - **Source reduction:** less waste produced in first place — most effective.
- **Waste hierarchy (most to least preferred):**
  - **Reduce → Reuse → Recycle → Recover (energy) → Dispose.**
- **Hazardous waste:**
  - **Toxic, ignitable, corrosive, reactive.**
  - **Examples:** industrial chemicals, electronics (e-waste), batteries, paint.
  - **RCRA (Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, 1976):** regulates current waste.
  - **CERCLA/Superfund (1980):** cleanup of abandoned hazardous sites.
  - **Famous Superfund sites:** Love Canal NY (1978), Times Beach MO.
- **E-waste:** electronics; contains valuable metals AND toxic substances; often shipped to developing world.
- **Toxicology basics:**
  - **Dose:** amount.
  - **Response:** physiological reaction.
  - **Dose-response curve:** plots increasing dose vs response.
  - **Threshold:** dose below which no response.
  - **LD50:** dose lethal to 50% of test population.
  - **Lower LD50 = more toxic.**
  - **Acute vs chronic exposure.**
- **Bioaccumulation/biomagnification (review):**
  - **Bioaccumulation:** chemicals build up in individual.
  - **Biomagnification:** concentration increases up food chain.
  - **DDT in eagles** classic.
- **Endocrine disruptors:**
  - **Mimic or interfere with hormones.**
  - **Examples:** BPA (plastics), atrazine (herbicide), some pharmaceuticals in waterways.
  - **Effects:** developmental, reproductive issues; feminization of fish.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why dead zones:**
  - **Gulf of Mexico dead zone:** Mississippi watershed agriculture → nutrients → Gulf eutrophication.
  - **Chesapeake Bay:** runoff from surrounding states.
  - **Fertilizer runoff dominant** cause.
  - **Solutions:** reduce fertilizer use; buffer strips; cover crops; wastewater treatment.
- **Sewage treatment detail:**
  - **Primary:** screens, settling tanks (50% of solids).
  - **Secondary:** aerobic bacteria break down organic matter (90% removal).
  - **Tertiary:** chemical/physical removal of remaining nutrients, pathogens.
  - **Sludge:** byproduct; can be incinerated, landfilled, or used as fertilizer (with treatment).
- **Plastic pollution:**
  - **Macroplastics:** visible debris (bags, bottles, fishing gear).
  - **Microplastics:** <5mm; from fragmentation, microbeads.
  - **Great Pacific Garbage Patch:** vast accumulation in ocean gyre.
  - **Marine animals ingest;** enter food chain.
- **LD50 examples (rough):**
  - **Botulinum toxin:** ~1 ng/kg (extremely toxic).
  - **DDT:** ~100 mg/kg.
  - **Caffeine:** ~150-200 mg/kg.
  - **Salt:** ~3,000 mg/kg.
- **Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs):**
  - **Long-lived in environment;** bioaccumulate.
  - **Examples:** PCBs, DDT, dioxins.
  - **Stockholm Convention** restricts.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why non-point source hard:**
  - **Many small sources** (every farm, lawn, driveway).
  - **Hard to regulate individuals.**
  - **Solutions:** best management practices, watershed approaches.
- **Why incineration controversial.**
  - **Reduces volume; recovers energy.**
  - **But:** emissions (dioxins, heavy metals); residual ash often hazardous.
  - **Modern incinerators** much cleaner than older.
- **Why endocrine disruptors concerning.**
  - **Effects at very low doses** (unlike traditional toxicants).
  - **Developmental impacts** especially serious.
  - **Hard to detect, regulate.**

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Point vs non-point

For each, identify point or non-point:
(a) Sewage treatment plant outflow.
(b) Fertilizer runoff from cornfields.
(c) Oil refinery wastewater pipe.
(d) Urban stormwater runoff.

- (a) **Point.**
- (b) **Non-point.**
- (c) **Point.**
- (d) **Non-point.**

### Example 2 [3] — Eutrophication trace

How does fertilizer runoff cause a dead zone?
- **Fertilizer (N, P)** runs off fields into rivers.
- **Reaches lake/sea** (e.g., Gulf of Mexico).
- **Algal bloom** explodes.
- **Algae die, decompose.**
- **Decomposers consume oxygen.**
- **Hypoxia (low O₂)** — fish die.
- **Dead zone** forms.

### Example 3 [4] — Dose-response

Substance A has LD50 of 50 mg/kg; B has LD50 of 5,000 mg/kg. Which is more toxic?
- **A is more toxic** (lower LD50).
- **A** kills 50% of test subjects at 50 mg/kg dose.
- **B** requires 100x higher dose for same effect.

### Example 4 [4] — Sewage treatment

Distinguish primary, secondary, tertiary treatment.
- **Primary:** physical (screens, settling tanks; ~50% of solids).
- **Secondary:** biological (aerobic bacteria break down organic matter; ~90%).
- **Tertiary:** chemical (remove residual nutrients N, P; remove pathogens).
- **Most US plants do primary + secondary; tertiary increasingly common where receiving water sensitive.**

### Example 5 [5] — Solid waste hierarchy

Order solid waste options from most to least preferred.
- **Source reduction (less waste produced).**
- **Reuse.**
- **Recycle.**
- **Compost (organic).**
- **Recover (incinerate with energy capture).**
- **Dispose (landfill).**
- **Reduce > reuse > recycle > recover > dispose.**

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing point and non-point.** Point: identifiable. Non-point: diffuse.
2. **Wrong eutrophication mechanism.** Nutrients → algal bloom → die-off → decomposition → O₂ depletion → fish die.
3. **Confusing LD50 interpretation.** **Lower LD50 = more toxic.**
4. **Wrong sewage treatment levels.** Primary (physical), secondary (biological), tertiary (chemical, advanced).
5. **Confusing bioaccumulation and biomagnification.** Bioaccumulation: in individual over time. Biomagnification: up food chain.
6. **Forgetting source reduction.** Top of waste hierarchy.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For pollution questions:**
- Identify type (water, soil, etc.).
- Identify source (point or non-point).
- Address effects.

**For toxicology questions:**
- Compare LD50 values.
- Address acute vs chronic.

**For waste questions:**
- Identify management option.
- Address tradeoffs.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Point source pollution comes from identifiable origins (sewage outfall, factory pipe) — easier to regulate. Non-point source pollution is diffuse (agricultural runoff, urban stormwater) — much harder to control because it requires changing many small actions."
2. "Cultural eutrophication occurs when nutrient pollution (especially nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizer and sewage) triggers algal blooms; subsequent algal die-off and decomposition consume dissolved oxygen, creating dead zones (Gulf of Mexico, Chesapeake Bay) where fish cannot survive."
3. "Sewage treatment progresses through primary (physical removal of solids), secondary (biological breakdown of organics by aerobic bacteria), and tertiary (chemical removal of nutrients and pathogens) — though tertiary remains less common in much of the world."
4. "LD50 — the dose lethal to 50% of test subjects — is inversely related to toxicity: a substance with LD50 of 5 mg/kg is far more toxic than one with LD50 of 5,000 mg/kg."
5. "The waste hierarchy ranks options from most to least preferred: reduce (source reduction), reuse, recycle, recover (energy from waste), dispose. Source reduction is most effective but requires changing consumption patterns."
