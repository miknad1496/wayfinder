# AP African American Studies — Unit 2: Freedom, Enslavement, and Resistance — Wayfinder Teaching Brain

> Surfaces in chat when student mentions topics in this unit. Use to ground custom teaching responses + on-demand teaching guide generation.

## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** ~25% of the AP African American Studies exam
- **Sub-topics covered:** development of slavery in colonies/US; daily life under slavery; resistance and rebellion; abolition movement; Black abolitionists (Douglass, Tubman, Truth); free Black communities.
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Slave codes, family/community life, resistance forms (everyday + organized), key abolitionists and movements.

## Big Ideas

1. **Race-based chattel slavery** developed in 1600s American colonies.
2. **Slave codes** legally defined and brutally enforced enslaved people's status.
3. **Resistance** took many forms: from work slowdowns to running away to outright rebellion.
4. **Black abolitionists** drove the movement (Douglass, Tubman, Truth, Walker).
5. **Free Black communities** built institutions, churches, schools.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Development of slavery in colonies:**
  - **First African captives** to British North America: 1619 (Jamestown).
  - **Transition from indentured servitude to chattel slavery** (1600s-early 1700s).
  - **Bacon's Rebellion (1676):** elite turn to enslaved Africans over poor whites.
  - **Slave codes** (Virginia, South Carolina) made slavery hereditary, racialized.
- **Geography of slavery:**
  - **South:** plantation slavery (cotton, tobacco, rice, sugar).
  - **North:** smaller-scale; abolished gradually post-Revolution.
  - **Cotton South (post-Whitney's gin, 1793):** "Cotton Kingdom."
- **Daily life under slavery:**
  - **Brutal labor regimes;** sunup to sundown.
  - **Family separations** common.
  - **Sexual violence** by enslavers.
  - **Limited material goods, harsh punishment.**
  - **BUT also:** community, music, religion, family preservation despite obstacles.
- **Resistance forms:**
  - **Everyday resistance:** work slowdowns, sabotage, faking illness, breaking tools.
  - **Running away:** Underground Railroad (Tubman, Vigilance Committees).
  - **Organized rebellions:** Stono Rebellion (1739, SC), Gabriel's Rebellion (1800, VA, foiled), Denmark Vesey (1822, SC, foiled), Nat Turner (1831, VA).
- **Black abolitionists:**
  - **Frederick Douglass:** escaped enslavement; *Narrative* (1845); orator; newspaper publisher; advised Lincoln.
  - **Harriet Tubman:** "Moses"; led ~70 to freedom via Underground Railroad; Civil War spy/scout.
  - **Sojourner Truth:** "Ain't I a Woman?" (1851); women's rights + abolition.
  - **David Walker:** *Appeal to the Coloured Citizens of the World* (1829); militant call.
  - **Henry Highland Garnet, Maria Stewart, Martin Delany.**
- **Free Black communities:**
  - **AME Church (1816, Richard Allen).**
  - **Black newspapers:** Freedom's Journal (1827), North Star (Douglass, 1847).
  - **Schools, mutual aid societies.**
  - **Free Black population** in 1860: ~488,000 (~11% of total Black population).

### Adds for [4]

- **Slave codes (Virginia 1660s+):**
  - Slavery hereditary through mother (1662).
  - Conversion to Christianity does not free.
  - Killing slave during punishment not murder.
- **Atlantic creole identities:** earliest Africans in Americas often had multilingual/multicultural identity.
- **Underground Railroad:**
  - **Network of safe houses** to free states/Canada.
  - **Conductors:** Black and white abolitionists.
  - **~30,000-100,000 escaped** total (estimates vary).
- **Why Nat Turner's Rebellion (1831) significant:**
  - **Killed ~55-60 white people.**
  - **Triggered massive white violence** in retaliation.
  - **Tightened slave codes;** restrictions on Black education, assembly.
  - **Ended Southern reform talk** about gradual emancipation.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why "peculiar institution" misnomer:**
  - **Slavery wasn't peculiar — it was central** to US economy and politics.
  - **Constitutional compromises** (3/5, fugitive slave clause) entrenched it.
- **Why white abolitionists not the main story:**
  - **Black abolitionists** drove the movement, often risking their lives.
  - **William Lloyd Garrison's Liberator** important but reflected/amplified Black voices.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Resistance forms

Identify three forms of enslaved resistance.
- **Everyday resistance:** work slowdowns, sabotage, breaking tools.
- **Running away:** Underground Railroad.
- **Armed rebellion:** Nat Turner (1831), Stono (1739), Denmark Vesey (1822, foiled).

### Example 2 [3] — Black abolitionists

Identify three Black abolitionists and their contributions.
- **Frederick Douglass:** *Narrative* (1845); orator; newspaper.
- **Harriet Tubman:** Underground Railroad conductor; Civil War spy.
- **Sojourner Truth:** "Ain't I a Woman?" speech; women's rights + abolition.

### Example 3 [4] — Nat Turner

What happened in Nat Turner's Rebellion (1831)?
- **Enslaved preacher** in Southampton County, VA.
- **Led rebellion** that killed ~55-60 whites.
- **Suppressed within days;** Turner captured, executed.
- **Massive white retaliation** killed ~120-200+ Black people.
- **Aftermath:** stricter slave codes; restrictions on Black education and assembly.
- **Ended Southern reform conversations** about emancipation.

### Example 4 [4] — Underground Railroad

What was the Underground Railroad?
- **Network of safe houses** and routes.
- **Helped enslaved people escape** to free states or Canada.
- **Conductors** included Tubman, white abolitionists.
- **~30,000-100,000** escaped via UGRR (estimates).
- **Stations** in homes, churches, businesses.
- **Code language:** "passengers," "stations," etc.

### Example 5 [5] — Free Black community

How did free Blacks build community institutions before Civil War?
- **Churches:** AME (Richard Allen, 1816); other Black-led denominations.
- **Newspapers:** *Freedom's Journal* (1827); *North Star* (Douglass, 1847).
- **Schools:** community-funded; often outlawed in South.
- **Mutual aid societies:** insurance against death, disability.
- **Conventions:** Negro Conventions (1830+) discussed strategy.
- **Free Black population** in 1860: ~488,000.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **First African captives 1619** at Jamestown; not 1620.
2. **Slavery wasn't always racialized** — emerged through 1600s.
3. **Cotton gin (1793) revived slavery,** not eliminated it.
4. **Resistance broader than rebellion** — everyday acts mattered too.
5. **Black abolitionists central,** not peripheral, to movement.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For resistance questions:** identify range (everyday → rebellion).
**For abolitionist questions:** name and specific contribution.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Race-based chattel slavery emerged in British North American colonies through the 17th century, codified by Virginia slave codes (1660s+) that made enslavement hereditary through the mother (1662) and severed the link between Christianity and freedom."
2. "Enslaved African Americans resisted in many forms — from everyday acts (work slowdowns, sabotage, breaking tools) to running away (Underground Railroad) to organized rebellions (Stono 1739, Gabriel's 1800, Vesey 1822, Nat Turner 1831)."
3. "Frederick Douglass — formerly enslaved, escaped 1838 — became the most prominent Black abolitionist of the 19th century: published the *Narrative* (1845), edited the *North Star* newspaper, advised Lincoln, and continued advocacy through Reconstruction."
4. "Harriet Tubman ('Moses') escaped slavery in 1849 and returned ~13 times to lead approximately 70 enslaved people to freedom via the Underground Railroad; she later served as a Union spy and scout in the Civil War."
5. "Free Black communities built institutional foundations: the African Methodist Episcopal Church (Richard Allen, 1816), Black newspapers (*Freedom's Journal* 1827, *North Star* 1847), schools, mutual aid societies, and Negro Conventions (1830+)."

## If You Do Nothing Else for This Unit

*Master development of chattel slavery and slave codes. Master forms of resistance (everyday, running, rebellion). Master key abolitionists (Douglass, Tubman, Truth, Walker). Master free Black community institutions.*
