- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** ~5–7 MCQs every year. PES is one of the College Board's favorite FRQ vehicles — at least one PES interpretation question appears in roughly half of recent exams. Mass-spec and average-atomic-mass questions are nearly guaranteed in the MCQ section. Successive-IE-table problems show up frequently as MCQ or as a sub-part of a longer FRQ. The mole work in 1.1 is invisible but everywhere — you cannot do limiting reagent, gas law, titration, or thermo problems without it.

## Big Ideas

1. **The mole bridges atomic-scale and lab-scale.** Avogadro's number (6.022 × 10²³) is the conversion factor between "stuff you can weigh on a balance" and "stuff that reacts with other stuff atom-by-atom." Every quantitative problem in AP Chem flows through mole conversions. Get this rusty and your errors cascade into every other unit.
2. **Coulomb's law explains everything periodic.** F ∝ q₁q₂/r². Larger effective nuclear charge (Z_eff) and/or smaller radius means stronger nucleus-to-electron attraction, which means harder to ionize, smaller radius, more electronegative. This single relationship justifies every periodic trend you'll be asked about.
3. **Electron configuration governs reactivity.** Valence (outermost) electrons dictate ions formed, bonds made, and chemistry observed. Core electrons shield the nucleus from the valence layer. The split between valence and core is where periodic chemistry happens.
4. **Spectroscopy is the experimental window into atoms.** Mass spectroscopy reveals isotope identity and abundance, which build up to the average atomic mass on the periodic table. Photoelectron spectroscopy maps the energy of each subshell directly — it is the experimental confirmation of the electron-configuration model.
5. **Periodic trends are ratios, not memorization.** Atomic radius decreases left-to-right across a period (more protons pulling the same shell inward) and increases top-to-bottom down a group (electrons added to a new, higher principal energy level). Memorize the WHY; the directions follow.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

A 3-target student studies this section, the [3] worked examples, and the [3] traps. That is enough to clear a 3.

- **Avogadro's number:** 6.022 × 10²³ entities per mole. "Entities" can mean atoms, molecules, ions, or formula units depending on the substance.
- **Molar mass:** atomic mass from the periodic table, expressed in g/mol. Add up component atomic masses for a compound (H₂O = 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol).
- **Conversion chain:** mass ↔ moles ↔ particles. (mass/molar mass = moles; moles × Avogadro = particles).
- **Atomic number Z** = number of protons. Defines the element.
- **Mass number A** = protons + neutrons. Determines the isotope.
- **Isotope notation:** ⁵⁶Fe means iron with mass number 56. Same Z (26 protons), different A (different neutron counts).
- **Average atomic mass** = weighted average of isotope masses, weighted by natural abundance. This is the number on the periodic table.
- **Aufbau, Pauli, Hund** — fill orbitals in order of increasing energy, no two electrons in the same orbital with same spin, fill degenerate orbitals singly before pairing.
- **Electron-configuration order:** 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p (use the diagonal rule diagram).
- **Two famous exceptions** to Aufbau: chromium [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and copper [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. Half-filled and fully-filled 3d subshells are extra stable.
- **Valence electrons** = group number for main-group elements (Group 1 = 1, Group 17 = 7).
- **Periodic-trend directions you must know cold:**
  - Atomic radius: increases down a group, decreases across a period (left to right).
  - Ionization energy (1st): decreases down a group, increases across a period.
  - Electronegativity: same direction as IE — increases up and to the right. F is highest.
  - Metallic character: opposite of EN — increases down and to the left. Cs is the most metallic stable element.
- **Mass spectrometry mechanics:** sample is ionized → accelerated → bent in a magnetic field by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) → lighter ions bend more, heavier less → detector records relative abundance as peak height.
- **Photoelectron spectroscopy mechanics:** high-energy photons knock electrons out of the atom; the detector measures their kinetic energy. By energy conservation, binding energy = photon energy − kinetic energy. Each peak in the spectrum corresponds to electrons coming out of one subshell.
- **PES axis convention (memorize this — it's the #1 source of confusion):** binding energy on the x-axis INCREASES from RIGHT to LEFT. The leftmost peak corresponds to the most tightly held electrons (the 1s).
- **PES peak heights** are proportional to the number of electrons in that subshell. So for a complete 2p subshell (6 electrons), the peak is 3× as tall as a complete 2s peak (2 electrons).

### Adds for [4]

- **Coulomb's law applied to atoms:** the binding energy of an electron is proportional to (Z_eff × e²) / r. More protons pulling on the same shell raises Z_eff. A smaller r raises BE. Everything else follows.
- **Effective nuclear charge:** Z_eff ≈ Z − S, where S is the shielding contribution. Core electrons shield very efficiently (≈1.0 per electron). Same-shell electrons shield poorly (≈0.35 per electron). You don't need to memorize Slater's rules for AP, but you must say things like "the added 3p electron in chlorine doesn't fully shield the increased nuclear charge, so Z_eff and BE both rise."
- **Penetration:** s orbitals penetrate closer to the nucleus than p orbitals at the same n. This is WHY 2s electrons have higher binding energy than 2p electrons even though they share the same principal energy level. p > d > f for penetration at the same n. This is the [4] move that explains within-shell PES splittings.
- **Successive ionization energies:** look for the BIG JUMP. The jump tells you when you are about to dig into a noble-gas core. The number of electrons removed BEFORE the jump = the group number. So an element with IE₁ small, IE₂ small, IE₃ enormous belongs to Group 2.
- **Cation electron configurations:** for transition metals, the 4s electrons leave BEFORE the 3d electrons. Fe (1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s²3d⁶) → Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶ (NOT [Ar]4s²3d⁴) → Fe³⁺ is [Ar]3d⁵ (a stable half-filled 3d).
- **Anion electron configurations:** electrons add to the next available orbital following Aufbau. Cl (1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁵) → Cl⁻ = [Ar].
- **Isoelectronic series:** species with the same electron count but different proton count. The species with the most protons is the smallest because it has the strongest pull on the same number of electrons. Example: O²⁻ > F⁻ > Ne > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺ in size.
- **PES interpretation routine:**
  1. Sum all peak heights → total electrons → identifies the element (assuming neutral).
  2. Order peaks by binding energy (left = high BE = inner shells).
  3. Map peaks to subshells from inside out: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, etc.
  4. Compare BEs across elements with the same shell to argue Z_eff differences.
- **Empirical vs molecular formula:** empirical is the smallest whole-number atom ratio (CH₂O for glucose). To get the molecular formula, you need the molar mass; divide it by the empirical formula mass to get the multiplier (180.16 / 30.03 = 6 → C₆H₁₂O₆).
- **Mixture analysis:** mass percent of each component is calculated independently. Be careful: in a mixture, the masses don't have to come out to nice whole-number ratios — that only happens for pure compounds.
- **Mass spec for molecules** (rare on AP but appears in some FRQs): you see a parent ion peak at the molecular mass plus fragmentation peaks. For elements, the mass spec is just isotope abundances.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why 4s fills before 3d at the neutral atom but 3d sits below 4s once filled.** At the neutral atom of K and Ca, the 4s orbital has slightly lower energy than 3d, so it fills first. As soon as 3d electrons are added (Sc onward), the 3d energy drops below 4s. So in cation formation, the higher-energy 4s electrons leave first. Stating this WHY in an FRQ is a sophistication move.
- **Why Mg has higher 1st IE than Al, breaking the across-period trend.** The reason is NOT just "magnesium has a stable filled 3s² configuration." The deeper reason: Al's 1st electron to leave is a 3p electron. Mg's 1st electron to leave is a 3s electron. The 3p subshell is at higher energy than 3s because it penetrates less, so the Al 3p electron is held more weakly than Mg's 3s. Different subshell, not different stability.
- **Why P has higher 1st IE than S, the second across-period exception.** P has a half-filled 3p³ configuration; each 3p electron sits in its own orbital, no electron-electron repulsion within an orbital. S adds the fourth 3p electron, which must pair up with one already there — the resulting electron-electron repulsion makes that paired electron easier to remove. So removing an electron from S relieves repulsion, lowering the IE relative to expectation.
- **The same exception appears at N vs O** for the same reason. N is half-filled 2p³, O has the first paired 2p electron.
- **Coulombic justification, written like an AP grader expects:** "The 2p electron in carbon is held less tightly than the 2s electron because the 2s orbital has greater penetration toward the nucleus. This greater penetration means the 2s electron experiences a larger effective nuclear charge. By Coulomb's law, the force of attraction (F ∝ q₁q₂/r²) is stronger when the electron's effective distance from the nucleus is smaller and Z_eff is larger, so the 2s binding energy exceeds the 2p binding energy."
- **m/z subtlety:** the mass spectrometer measures mass-to-charge ratio. For atomic mass spec, charge is almost always +1, so m/z ≈ m. For molecular fragments with higher charge, you must divide.
- **PES log-scale subtlety:** binding energies span ~3 orders of magnitude across a single periodic table. The 1s of Ne is around 84 MJ/mol; the 3s of Na is about 0.5 MJ/mol. When comparing two PES spectra, a 2× change in BE doesn't correspond to a 2× change in r — Coulomb's r is in the denominator squared.
- **PES of an ion vs neutral atom.** A cation has fewer electrons, so peak heights drop accordingly. The remaining peaks SHIFT to higher BE because shielding has decreased — the same Z now pulls fewer electrons, so each remaining electron is held more tightly.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Average atomic mass from mass spec

Magnesium has three stable isotopes: ²⁴Mg (78.99% abundance, mass 23.985), ²⁵Mg (10.00%, mass 24.986), ²⁶Mg (11.01%, mass 25.983). Find the average atomic mass.

- **Step 1.** Convert each percentage to a decimal: 0.7899, 0.1000, 0.1101.
- **Step 2.** Multiply each isotope's mass by its abundance: (23.985)(0.7899) = 18.945; (24.986)(0.1000) = 2.499; (25.983)(0.1101) = 2.861.
- **Step 3.** Sum: 18.945 + 2.499 + 2.861 = 24.305 g/mol.
- **Sanity check:** the periodic table lists Mg at 24.31. Match.
- **Trap to avoid:** the average is NOT 24 (the most abundant peak's mass). The weighted average accounts for all isotopes proportionally.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Electron configuration of Fe and its cations

Write the full electron configurations of Fe, Fe²⁺, and Fe³⁺.

- Fe (Z = 26): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶ → in noble-gas notation: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶.
- Fe²⁺: remove the two 4s electrons FIRST (not the 3d). Result: [Ar] 3d⁶.
- Fe³⁺: remove both 4s and one 3d electron. Result: [Ar] 3d⁵.
- **[4] insight:** Fe³⁺ is unusually stable because [Ar]3d⁵ is a half-filled subshell — every 3d orbital has one unpaired electron (Hund's), minimizing electron-electron repulsion. This is partly why iron-3 chemistry (rust, hemoglobin oxidation states) is so important biologically and industrially.
- **Common error:** writing Fe³⁺ as [Ar] 3d³ 4s² (removing 3d first). Wrong. Once 3d is partially filled, its energy drops below 4s, so 4s electrons leave first in ionization.

### Example 3 [4][5] — PES interpretation

A photoelectron spectrum has five peaks at the following binding energies (MJ/mol) with the indicated relative heights:
- Peak A: 309 MJ/mol, height 2
- Peak B: 31.5 MJ/mol, height 2
- Peak C: 24.1 MJ/mol, height 6
- Peak D: 2.82 MJ/mol, height 2
- Peak E: 1.52 MJ/mol, height 6

Identify the element and explain the peak ordering.

- **Step 1.** Sum the heights: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 = 18 electrons. This is a neutral atom of element 18: argon.
- **Step 2.** Map peaks to subshells from inside out (highest BE = innermost):
  - Peak A (309) = 1s²
  - Peak B (31.5) = 2s²
  - Peak C (24.1) = 2p⁶
  - Peak D (2.82) = 3s²
  - Peak E (1.52) = 3p⁶
  - This matches the configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p⁶ for argon.
- **[4] explanation: why is 2s at higher BE than 2p?** Both are in the second principal energy level (n = 2), but the 2s orbital has greater penetration toward the nucleus — its electron density extends closer in. This means a 2s electron experiences a larger effective nuclear charge than a 2p electron, which is screened slightly by the 2s. Greater Z_eff → stronger Coulombic attraction → higher binding energy.
- **[5] reasoning chain to write on an FRQ:** "The 2s electrons exhibit greater penetration toward the nucleus than the 2p electrons because the s-orbital has nonzero electron density at r = 0. As a consequence, 2s electrons experience a larger effective nuclear charge than 2p electrons, which are partially shielded by the 2s. Per Coulomb's law (F ∝ q₁q₂/r²), greater Z_eff at comparable r yields a stronger force of attraction and therefore higher binding energy, which is why peak B (2s) appears at higher BE than peak C (2p)."

### Example 4 [4] — Successive ionization energies identify the element

An element X has the following successive ionization energies in MJ/mol: IE₁ = 0.74, IE₂ = 1.45, IE₃ = 7.73, IE₄ = 10.5, IE₅ = 13.6. Identify the group of element X.

- **Step 1.** Locate the BIG jump. It's between IE₂ and IE₃ — the value jumps by a factor of more than 5×.
- **Step 2.** Two electrons can be removed before hitting the noble-gas core. Element X has 2 valence electrons → Group 2 (alkaline earth metals: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba).
- **Step 3.** The magnitudes (IE₁ ≈ 0.74) are consistent with magnesium specifically.
- **AP grader expectation:** state explicitly that the jump indicates breaking into a closed noble-gas (Ne) core. "The discontinuity between IE₂ and IE₃ corresponds to removing an electron from the [Ne] core, which requires significantly more energy than removing the valence 3s electrons."

### Example 5 [3][4] — Empirical formula from mass percent

A 4.50 g sample of an iron oxide contains 3.27 g of iron and 1.23 g of oxygen. Find the empirical formula.

- **Step 1.** Convert grams to moles using molar mass:
  - mol Fe = 3.27 g / 55.85 g/mol = 0.0586 mol
  - mol O = 1.23 g / 16.00 g/mol = 0.0769 mol
- **Step 2.** Divide both by the smaller value to get a mole ratio:
  - Fe: 0.0586 / 0.0586 = 1.000
  - O: 0.0769 / 0.0586 = 1.312
- **Step 3.** Convert to whole numbers. 1.312 ≈ 4/3, so multiply both by 3:
  - Fe: 3.00
  - O: 3.94 ≈ 4
- **Step 4.** Empirical formula: Fe₃O₄ (magnetite — a real mineral).
- **[4] note on rounding:** if the ratio is 1.5, multiply by 2. If 1.33, multiply by 3. If 1.25, multiply by 4. If you round 1.31 down to 1, you'd write FeO (wüstite — a different real mineral) — but the data is closer to 1.33 here, which means the answer is the spinel structure Fe₃O₄. Don't round prematurely.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Mass spec tallest peak is NOT the atomic mass.** The tallest peak shows the most abundant isotope, not the weighted average. Chlorine's tallest peak is at 35 (³⁵Cl), but its average atomic mass is 35.45 because of the ²⁵% abundance of ³⁷Cl.
2. **PES peak height counts electrons, not energy.** A taller peak means more electrons live in that subshell. The peak's HORIZONTAL POSITION on the BE axis tells you energy. Don't conflate.
3. **PES binding energy axis runs LEFT for higher BE.** Counterintuitive but standardized. The leftmost peak is always the 1s (most tightly bound). Many students lose easy points by reading the axis backward.
4. **Cu and Cr break Aufbau.** Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹. AP loves to ask you about unpaired electrons in Cr (the answer is 6: five 3d electrons plus one 4s electron, each unpaired).
5. **Cations: 4s leaves before 3d.** Always. Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶, never [Ar]4s²3d⁴. Co³⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶, not [Ar]4s²3d⁴.
6. **"Smaller atom = more reactive" is circular.** On any justification, invoke Coulomb's law. Stronger nucleus-electron attraction at smaller r and/or larger Z_eff is the WHY.
7. **The Mg-vs-Al IE exception is about subshell, not stability.** Al's 1st electron to leave is a 3p; Mg's is a 3s. Different subshells have different penetration and energy. Don't say "Mg has stable s² so it's harder to ionize" — say "Al's 3p electron is in a higher-energy subshell."
8. **The N-vs-O / P-vs-S IE exception is about pairing.** Removing one of O's PAIRED 2p electrons relieves intra-orbital repulsion, making it easier than expected.
9. **Z_eff conceptual application beats Slater's rules.** AP almost never asks for a numerical Z_eff. It asks you to reason qualitatively: "more protons, same shell → larger Z_eff" is enough.
10. **Empirical vs molecular formula confusion.** Glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆ (molecular) but its empirical formula is CH₂O. Acetic acid is CH₃COOH = C₂H₄O₂ (molecular), empirical CH₂O. Same empirical formula, different molecules.
11. **Isoelectronic comparisons reversed.** O²⁻ has 8 protons and 10 electrons. Mg²⁺ has 12 protons and 10 electrons. Mg²⁺ is SMALLER because more protons pull the same 10 electrons more tightly. Largest → smallest: O²⁻ > F⁻ > Ne > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺.
12. **Forgetting the "in MJ/mol" unit on PES.** AP uses MJ/mol exclusively. Other textbooks use eV. Convert if a problem mixes units, but don't expect AP to do that.
13. **Treating 1s as part of the valence shell.** 1s electrons are core for everything past hydrogen and helium. Valence electrons live in the outermost principal energy level only.
14. **Forgetting to convert mass to moles in stoichiometry-flavored Unit 1 problems.** Mass percent uses grams. Empirical formula uses moles. The conversion goes through molar mass — never skip the step.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

AP Chemistry FRQs on Unit 1 reward a very specific style of writing. The grader is looking for explicit invocations of named principles, with quantitative or directional comparisons.

**For periodic-trend justifications:**
- The grader wants the words "effective nuclear charge" or "Z_eff."
- The grader wants you to mention shielding by core electrons explicitly.
- The grader wants you to invoke Coulomb's law by name (or in symbol form: F ∝ q₁q₂/r²).
- Vague answers ("stronger attraction," "more reactive," "more stable") earn 0 unless paired with a mechanism. The mechanism is always the Coulombic argument.

**For PES problems:**
- Identify the element from total electrons (always your first move).
- Map peaks to subshells from inside (highest BE) out (lowest BE).
- When asked to compare two elements' PES, compare a SPECIFIC peak (e.g., "the 1s peak in Ne is at higher BE than the 1s peak in Li because Ne has more protons exerting a stronger pull on the same n=1 shell").
- When asked about within-element splittings (e.g., 2s vs 2p), invoke penetration.

**For mass-spec problems:**
- The weighted-average calculation must show all three isotope-mass × abundance products, then summed.
- If asked to interpret peaks, identify which peak corresponds to which isotope by mass and explain that peak height is proportional to natural abundance.

**For successive-IE problems:**
- The grader wants you to NAME THE JUMP. "There is a large jump between IE_n and IE_(n+1), indicating that the (n+1)th electron is removed from a noble-gas core."
- State the group number explicitly. Naming the specific element is a bonus.

**For empirical-formula problems:**
- Show the mole conversion explicitly. "Moles of X = grams / molar mass = ___."
- Show the divide-by-smallest step.
- Justify any multiplier you apply at the end ("since the ratio is 1.5, multiply by 2 to obtain whole numbers").

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The effective nuclear charge (Z_eff) experienced by the valence electron in [element] is larger because [more protons in the same principal energy level / fewer shielding core electrons]."
2. "Per Coulomb's law (F ∝ q₁q₂/r²), the smaller atomic radius and/or larger Z_eff in [element] yields a stronger nucleus-electron attraction, requiring more energy to remove the electron."
3. "The 2s electron has greater penetration toward the nucleus than the 2p electron, so it experiences a larger Z_eff and is held with greater binding energy."
4. "The successive ionization energies show a large jump between IE_n and IE_(n+1), indicating that the (n+1)th electron is being removed from a noble-gas core."
5. "These species are isoelectronic; the species with the greatest nuclear charge has the smallest radius because its protons exert the strongest Coulombic attraction on the same number of electrons."
6. "The PES peak at the highest binding energy corresponds to the 1s electrons, which are closest to the nucleus and experience the largest effective nuclear charge."
7. "Cr and Cu are exceptions to the Aufbau principle: their configurations [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ reflect the extra stability of half-filled and fully-filled 3d subshells."

## If You Do Nothing Else for This Unit

*Internalize this single chain: more protons (in the same shell) → larger effective nuclear charge → stronger Coulombic attraction → smaller radius → higher binding energy → harder to ionize → higher electronegativity. Every periodic-trend justification on the AP Chemistry exam derives from this one line of reasoning.*
