- **Sub-topics covered:**

  - 3.4 Ideal Gas Law (PV = nRT)

- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Particulate diagrams of IMFs are the most-tested concept on the entire exam. Beer-Lambert problems appear in roughly 1 in 2 FRQs as a sub-question. Gas law calculations (especially using ideal gas law to find molar mass or to combine with stoichiometry) appear in nearly every exam. KMT-based comparisons of average kinetic energy and speed at different temperatures show up as MCQ regularly. Particulate diagrams of solutions (which dissolves, what the resulting solution looks like) appear as FRQ scaffolding for thermodynamics and equilibrium questions in later units.

## Big Ideas

1. **IMFs determine all bulk physical properties.** Boiling point, melting point, vapor pressure, solubility, viscosity, surface tension — all depend on how strongly molecules attract each other. Get IMF ranking right and you get half the unit.
2. **The IMF strength hierarchy:** ion-dipole > hydrogen bond > dipole-dipole > London dispersion. But London dispersion grows with molecular size and can outweigh other forces in large molecules.
3. **Gases obey PV = nRT until they don't.** Real gases deviate when (a) pressure is high (molecules occupy non-negligible volume) or (b) temperature is low (intermolecular attractions become significant). KMT explains both deviations mechanistically.
4. **At the same temperature, all gases have the same average kinetic energy** — but lighter molecules move faster (KE = ½mv²). This is why H₂ effuses faster than O₂ at the same temperature.
5. **Beer-Lambert (A = εbc) is the workhorse of solution analysis.** Absorbance is directly proportional to concentration when path length and molar absorptivity are constant. AP loves Beer-Lambert problems because they test linear relationships, dilution math, and graph reading all at once.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **The four IMF types in increasing strength** (within neutral molecules):
  - **London dispersion forces (LDF)** — present in ALL molecules. Caused by instantaneous dipoles in electron cloud. Stronger for larger, more polarizable atoms.
  - **Dipole-dipole** — between polar molecules. Permanent partial charges attract.
  - **Hydrogen bonding** — special strong dipole-dipole when H is bonded to N, O, or F. The "FON rule."
  - **Ion-dipole** — between an ion and a polar molecule. Strongest of the IMFs (used to be classified as part of solvation).
- **Ion-ion is NOT an IMF** — that's an intramolecular ionic bond inside a salt lattice. AP distinguishes intra (within) from inter (between) carefully.
- **Properties controlled by IMFs:**
  - Higher IMF → higher boiling point, higher melting point, lower vapor pressure, higher viscosity, higher surface tension.
  - Lower IMF → opposite.
- **States of matter:**
  - Solid: particles in fixed positions, vibrating. Dense, definite shape and volume.
  - Liquid: particles touching but flowing. Dense, definite volume but takes container shape.
  - Gas: particles far apart, moving freely. Low density, fills container.
- **Ideal gas law:** PV = nRT. R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) = 8.314 J/(mol·K). Always use absolute temperature in Kelvin.
- **STP conventions:** standard pressure = 1 atm (or 1 bar in some newer references — AP currently uses 1 atm). Standard temperature = 273.15 K (0°C). At STP, 1 mole of ideal gas occupies 22.4 L.
- **Combined gas law:** P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ when n is constant.
- **Dalton's law of partial pressures:** P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + ... Each gas exerts pressure independently. Mole fraction × total pressure = partial pressure.
- **KMT postulates** (memorize):
  1. Gas particles are in constant random motion.
  2. Particle volume is negligible compared to container volume.
  3. No intermolecular forces.
  4. Collisions are elastic (no kinetic energy lost).
  5. Average KE is proportional to absolute temperature.
- **Beer-Lambert law:** A = εbc, where A = absorbance (unitless), ε = molar absorptivity (M⁻¹cm⁻¹), b = path length (cm), c = concentration (M).
- **Spectroscopy basics:** electromagnetic spectrum from radio (low E) to gamma (high E). E = hν = hc/λ. Higher frequency = shorter wavelength = higher energy.
- **Concentration:** molarity (M) = moles solute / liters solution. Molality (m) = moles solute / kg solvent (less common on AP).
- **Dilution:** M₁V₁ = M₂V₂.
- **Mass percent of a solute:** (mass solute / mass solution) × 100%.

### Adds for [4]

- **IMF ranking with worked examples:**
  - H₂O has stronger IMFs than HF or NH₃ despite all having H-bonding, because each H₂O can form 4 H-bonds (2 donors + 2 acceptors), while NH₃ averages 1 (3 donors but only 1 acceptor lone pair) and HF averages 2.
  - Acetic acid (CH₃COOH) DIMERIZES via two hydrogen bonds, so its boiling point is anomalously high relative to its molar mass.
- **London dispersion grows with molar mass and surface area.** Br₂ (gas at high temp) > Cl₂ (gas) > F₂ (gas) only by virtue of size. Pentane has higher BP than 2,2-dimethylpropane (same formula, less surface contact in branched structure).
- **Polarizability** is the underlying property: larger electron clouds are more easily distorted, giving stronger temporary dipoles. Heavier halogens, larger noble gases, longer carbon chains all have higher polarizability.
- **Solid types and properties:**
  - Ionic: high MP, brittle, conducts when molten/dissolved (NaCl, MgO).
  - Molecular: low MP, soft, doesn't conduct (sugar, ice).
  - Network covalent: very high MP, hard, doesn't conduct (diamond, SiO₂); exception: graphite (conducts within sheets).
  - Metallic: variable MP, malleable, conducts (Cu, Fe).
- **Why ionic solids are brittle:** when planes shift, like-charged ions align → repulsion → fracture. Metallic solids stay malleable because the sea of electrons cushions the cations.
- **Real-gas deviations (van der Waals corrections):**
  - At HIGH pressure: molecular volume becomes non-negligible. Real V > ideal V predicted (because particles take up space).
  - At LOW temperature: intermolecular attractions matter. Real P < ideal P predicted (attractions slow molecules and reduce wall impacts).
  - Largest deviations: gases with strong IMFs (NH₃, H₂O vapor, CO₂) and at conditions near liquefaction.
- **Maxwell-Boltzmann distributions:** at higher T, the curve flattens and shifts right (more molecules with higher speeds, broader spread). All gases at the SAME T have the same average KE.
- **Effusion / Graham's law:** rate₁/rate₂ = √(M₂/M₁). Lighter gas effuses faster. This is the only AP-relevant Graham's-law application.
- **KMT and pressure:** pressure comes from collisions of particles with container walls. More particles, faster particles, or smaller container → more or harder collisions → higher P.
- **Beer-Lambert problem patterns:**
  - Find ε from a calibration curve (slope of A vs c).
  - Find unknown c from absorbance and known ε, b.
  - Account for dilutions before reporting original concentration.
  - Series dilutions: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ at each step.
- **Photoelectric effect** (the [4] piece on spectroscopy): light hitting a metal ejects electrons only if photon energy exceeds the work function (threshold). Below threshold = no electrons regardless of intensity. Above threshold = ejected electrons have KE = hν − work function. Confirmed quantum nature of light.
- **Particulate diagrams** are AP's most distinctive question type. You may be asked to:
  - Draw water molecules around a dissolved Na⁺ ion (oxygen ends pointing in, hydrogens out).
  - Draw the orientation of polar molecules in a solution.
  - Distinguish "before mixing" from "after mixing" particulate views.
- **Henry's law** (gas solubility): solubility of a gas ∝ partial pressure of that gas above the solution. Soda goes flat when CO₂ partial pressure drops.
- **Solubility rules to know:**
  - "Like dissolves like" — polar dissolves polar, nonpolar dissolves nonpolar.
  - All Group 1 and ammonium (NH₄⁺) salts are soluble.
  - All nitrates (NO₃⁻) are soluble.
  - Most chlorides are soluble (except AgCl, PbCl₂, Hg₂Cl₂).
  - Most sulfates are soluble (except BaSO₄, PbSO₄, CaSO₄).
  - Most carbonates, phosphates, hydroxides are insoluble (except Group 1 and ammonium).

### Adds for [5]

- **Why H₂O has the highest BP of the hydrides H₂O, NH₃, HF.** All three H-bond, but water has TWO lone pairs AND two H atoms, so each molecule can both donate and accept two H-bonds — total network of 4 per molecule. NH₃ has one lone pair (1 acceptor + 3 donors); HF has 3 lone pairs but only 1 H (1 donor + 3 potential acceptors). So water has the most hydrogen bonds per molecule, hence highest BP.
- **The water density anomaly.** Solid water (ice) is LESS dense than liquid water because the H-bond network forces water molecules into an open hexagonal lattice. Above 4°C, increasing T disrupts the lattice and density drops as expected; below 4°C, the lattice is forming and density rises. The maximum density is at 4°C.
- **van der Waals equation.** (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. The "a" term corrects for intermolecular attractions (subtracts from real pressure as if attractions were absent); the "b" term corrects for finite molecular volume. You don't need to memorize this for AP, but recognizing the WHY for each correction is a [5] move.
- **Surface tension and viscosity in molecular terms.** Surface tension comes from molecules at the surface having no neighbors above — net inward pull. Higher IMFs → higher surface tension. Water beads up; alcohols spread because lower IMFs.
- **Capillary action:** balance of cohesion (molecule-to-molecule) vs adhesion (molecule-to-container). Water rises in a glass tube (adhesion to glass > cohesion); mercury depresses (cohesion > adhesion).
- **Phase diagrams** (P vs T): triple point (all three phases coexist), critical point (above which gas and liquid become indistinguishable), supercritical fluid region. The slope of the solid-liquid line is positive for most substances; water has a NEGATIVE slope (rare) — explained by ice being less dense than liquid water.
- **Henry's law constants** depend on temperature. Solubility of gases in water DECREASES with temperature (warm soda goes flat faster). Solubility of solids in liquids USUALLY increases with temperature (sugar dissolves better in hot water).
- **Beer-Lambert calibration curves:** plot A vs c at multiple known concentrations to extract ε from the slope (slope = εb when path length is fixed). The line should pass through the origin (zero concentration → zero absorbance) — non-zero intercept signals systematic error.
- **Spectroscopy region identifications:**
  - UV/Vis (~200–800 nm) — electronic transitions; used for transition metal complexes, conjugated organics. Most Beer-Lambert AP problems use UV/Vis.
  - IR (~2500–25000 nm) — vibrational transitions; used to identify functional groups.
  - Microwave — rotational transitions.
  - X-ray — core electron transitions.
- **Why some metal complexes are colored:** d-d electronic transitions in the visible range. The complementary color of the absorbed wavelength is what we see (Cu²⁺ absorbs orange/red, appears blue).
- **Particulate-diagram FRQ rubric expectations:**
  - Show the correct number of particles for the chemical amount given.
  - Show the correct orientation (polar molecules' positive ends near anions, etc.).
  - Show ONLY the particles that exist post-process (don't draw "before" particles in an "after" diagram).
  - Distinguish ions from molecules (charge labels matter).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Ranking boiling points by IMF

Rank the following by increasing boiling point: CH₄, CH₃OH, NaCl, He.

- **Step 1.** Identify the dominant IMF in each:
  - He — atomic, only LDF (very weak; small atom).
  - CH₄ — nonpolar molecular, only LDF (slightly larger than He).
  - CH₃OH — polar with H-bonding (O–H present).
  - NaCl — ionic solid; ionic bonds (intramolecular forces in the lattice are extremely strong).
- **Step 2.** Rank: He < CH₄ < CH₃OH < NaCl.
- **Step 3.** Justification: stronger interparticle attraction → more energy needed to break apart particles → higher BP. Ionic > H-bonding > LDF (with size differences).
- **AP-style answer:** "Helium has only weak London dispersion forces; methane has slightly stronger LDF due to greater electron count; methanol exhibits hydrogen bonding via its O–H group, which is much stronger than dipole-dipole or LDF; sodium chloride is held together by full ionic bonds, requiring the most energy to break."

### Example 2 [3][4] — Ideal gas law with molar mass

A 0.500 g sample of an unknown gas occupies 250 mL at 25°C and 1.00 atm. Find its molar mass.

- **Step 1.** Convert: V = 0.250 L, T = 298.15 K, P = 1.00 atm.
- **Step 2.** Use PV = nRT → n = PV/RT = (1.00)(0.250) / (0.08206)(298.15) = 0.01022 mol.
- **Step 3.** Molar mass = mass/moles = 0.500 g / 0.01022 mol = 48.9 g/mol.
- **[4] note:** this is close to 48 g/mol, which suggests ozone (O₃) or possibly sulfur monoxide (SO, less common). On AP, you'd be given a list of candidates; pick the closest.

### Example 3 [4] — Beer-Lambert with calibration

A standard solution of 1.00 × 10⁻⁴ M of a colored compound has absorbance 0.250 at 530 nm in a 1.00 cm cell. An unknown sample has absorbance 0.625 in the same cell. What is its concentration?

- **Step 1.** From the standard: ε = A/(bc) = 0.250 / [(1.00)(1.00 × 10⁻⁴)] = 2500 M⁻¹cm⁻¹.
- **Step 2.** For the unknown: c = A/(εb) = 0.625 / [(2500)(1.00)] = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ M.
- **Sanity check:** absorbance 2.5× higher → concentration 2.5× higher. Linear, as expected.
- **[4] explanation:** "Because Beer-Lambert is linear (A = εbc), the ratio of unknown to standard concentration equals the ratio of their absorbances when path length and ε are constant: c_unknown = c_standard × (A_unknown / A_standard) = (1.00 × 10⁻⁴)(0.625/0.250) = 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ M."

### Example 4 [4] — Particulate diagram of NaCl in water

Draw a particulate diagram showing what happens when 1 formula unit of NaCl dissolves in water. Label all particles.

- **Step 1.** NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
- **Step 2.** Diagram should show:
  - 1 Na⁺ ion (labeled with + charge)
  - 1 Cl⁻ ion (labeled with − charge)
  - Multiple H₂O molecules (label as bent shape with O and 2 H's)
- **Step 3.** Orientation rules:
  - Around Na⁺: water O ends (partially negative) point TOWARD the cation.
  - Around Cl⁻: water H ends (partially positive) point TOWARD the anion.
- **Why this earns the rubric point:** the diagram explicitly demonstrates ion-dipole interactions, which is the [4]-level mechanism for ionic dissolution.
- **Common loss:** drawing both Na⁺ and Cl⁻ touching (no water in between). The dissolution process means water has come between the ions, separating them.

### Example 5 [4][5] — KMT comparison

A sample contains equal moles of He and Ne gas in the same container at 300 K. Compare (a) average kinetic energy, (b) average speed, (c) partial pressures.

- **Step 1.** Average KE depends ONLY on T. Both gases at 300 K → identical average KE = (3/2)k_B T per molecule.
- **Step 2.** KE = ½mv², so v_avg = √(KE × 2/m). Lighter gas (He, M=4) has higher speed than heavier gas (Ne, M=20). Specifically: v_He / v_Ne = √(M_Ne/M_He) = √(20/4) = √5 ≈ 2.24.
- **Step 3.** Partial pressures: equal moles in same V at same T → equal partial pressures (P = nRT/V).
- **[5] phrase:** "All gases at the same temperature have identical average translational kinetic energies because KE_avg = (3/2)k_B T depends only on temperature. Since helium has a smaller molar mass than neon, equating ½m₁v₁² = ½m₂v₂² requires v_He > v_Ne. Specifically, v_He = v_Ne × √(M_Ne/M_He) = v_Ne × √5."

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing ion-ion (intramolecular, in lattice) with ion-dipole (intermolecular, in solution).** AP carefully distinguishes them.
2. **Calling London dispersion "weak" without qualification.** LDF can dominate when molecules are large (heavy halogens, long alkanes). Br₂ is liquid at room T; F₂ is gas — both are nonpolar; the difference is LDF magnitude.
3. **Hydrogen bonding scope confusion.** H-bonds form ONLY when H is bonded to N, O, or F (the "FON rule"). C–H bonds do NOT hydrogen-bond. Methanol H-bonds through its O–H, not its C–H bonds.
4. **Forgetting to convert °C to K in PV=nRT.** Always Kelvin in gas law calculations.
5. **Wrong R value units.** R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) for P in atm and V in L. R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) for energy contexts. Pick the right one.
6. **Volume in mL when using R = 0.08206.** Convert mL to L (divide by 1000).
7. **Treating gases as having the same average speed at the same T.** They have the same KINETIC ENERGY, not the same speed. Lighter molecules move faster.
8. **Real-gas deviation direction confusion.** At high P → real V is LARGER than ideal predicts (molecular volume matters). At low T → real P is LOWER than ideal predicts (attractions slow particles).
9. **Beer-Lambert non-linearity assumption.** Beer-Lambert is only linear at low concentrations (typically c < 0.01 M). At high concentrations, deviations occur from molecular interactions.
10. **Mixing molarity and grams.** M = mol/L, NOT g/L. Convert through molar mass.
11. **Forgetting dilution factor when reporting original concentration.** If you diluted a sample 10× before measuring, multiply your Beer-Lambert result by 10 to report the original concentration.
12. **Particulate diagram missing solvent.** When showing a dissolved species, the water (or other solvent) molecules MUST be drawn surrounding the dissolved particles, properly oriented.
13. **Drawing molecular gas as touching balls.** Gas particles in a particulate diagram should be far apart (with the volume of the molecules being a tiny fraction of the container volume).
14. **Calling a molecule "polar" without naming the asymmetry.** AP wants a geometric reason: "the molecule is polar because the bent geometry prevents the bond dipoles from canceling."
15. **"Stronger IMFs = more reactive" confusion.** IMFs determine PHYSICAL properties (BP, MP, viscosity), not chemical reactivity. Reactive sites are determined by bonding patterns.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For IMF identification:**
- Name the SPECIFIC IMF type (London dispersion, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding, ion-dipole).
- Justify by the bond/molecule features that produce that IMF (electronegativity differences, presence of H–F/O/N, ion charges).

**For BP/MP/vapor pressure rankings:**
- Identify the dominant IMF in each substance.
- Rank by IMF strength.
- For LDF-dominant comparisons, also consider molecular size and shape.

**For ideal gas problems:**
- Show the formula PV = nRT (or rearranged form).
- Convert all units to match R.
- Solve for the requested quantity.

**For Beer-Lambert problems:**
- Show A = εbc and identify each variable.
- For calibration curves, show that ε = slope.
- Account for dilutions explicitly.

**For particulate diagrams:**
- Draw the correct number and identity of particles for the post-process state.
- Show orientation of polar molecules (which end faces ion or polar partner).
- Don't draw bonds where there shouldn't be any (e.g., no covalent line between Na⁺ and water).

**For solubility / "like dissolves like" arguments:**
- Identify the polarity of solute and solvent.
- Match polar with polar, nonpolar with nonpolar.
- For ionic dissolution, name the ion-dipole interactions explicitly.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The dominant intermolecular force in [substance] is [IMF type], because [structural justification: presence of O–H bond enables hydrogen bonding / molecule has permanent dipole / largest London dispersion due to high polarizability]."
2. "Substance X has a higher boiling point than substance Y because [X exhibits stronger IMF type than Y], requiring more thermal energy to overcome the attractions and convert from liquid to gas."
3. "The deviation from ideal gas behavior occurs at high pressure because the volume of the gas molecules themselves becomes a non-negligible fraction of the container volume, and at low temperature because intermolecular attractions become significant relative to particle kinetic energy."
4. "All gases at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy. Since KE = ½mv², the lighter molecule (lower molar mass) has a higher average speed."
5. "By Beer-Lambert (A = εbc), absorbance is directly proportional to concentration when path length and molar absorptivity are held constant. The slope of the A-vs-c calibration curve gives the product εb."
6. "When NaCl(s) dissolves in water, the polar water molecules orient with their oxygen ends toward Na⁺ and their hydrogen ends toward Cl⁻, forming ion-dipole interactions that overcome the lattice energy of the salt."
7. "Like dissolves like: [polar/nonpolar] solvent X dissolves [polar/nonpolar] solute Y because the IMFs between solute and solvent are comparable in strength to those within each pure substance, allowing favorable mixing."
