## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 7–9% of the AP Chemistry exam
- **Sub-topics covered:**
  - 4.1 Introduction to Reactions (chemical vs physical change)
  - 4.2 Net Ionic Equations
  - 4.3 Representations of Reactions (particulate, symbolic, macroscopic)
  - 4.4 Physical and Chemical Changes
  - 4.5 Stoichiometry
  - 4.6 Introduction to Titration
  - 4.7 Types of Chemical Reactions (synthesis, decomposition, single replacement, double replacement, combustion)
  - 4.8 Introduction to Acid-Base Reactions
  - 4.9 Oxidation-Reduction (Redox) Reactions
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Net ionic equations are the single most-tested skill in this unit — they appear in nearly every FRQ either directly or as a required intermediate step. Stoichiometry is the foundation of every quantitative FRQ on the exam, including thermodynamics, equilibrium, and acids/bases. Identifying redox reactions and assigning oxidation numbers shows up as MCQ regularly. Titration mechanics come back HARD in Unit 8 (acids/bases) but the foundational ideas are introduced here.

## Big Ideas

1. **Mass and atoms are conserved.** Every balanced equation must have the same number of atoms of each element on both sides AND the same total charge. Stoichiometry is the bookkeeping of this conservation.
2. **Net ionic equations strip away spectators.** A complete ionic equation shows every dissolved species; the net ionic equation removes ions that appear unchanged on both sides. The remaining species are the only ones that actually react.
3. **Reactions fall into a small number of categories.** Synthesis (A + B → AB), decomposition (AB → A + B), single replacement (A + BC → AC + B), double replacement (AB + CD → AD + CB), combustion (CₓHᵧ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O), acid-base (H⁺ transfer), redox (e⁻ transfer). Recognize the type and the products often write themselves.
4. **Acids donate protons, bases accept them.** This Brønsted-Lowry framing covers nearly every acid-base reaction on AP. Strong acids/bases dissociate completely; weak ones partially.
5. **Redox is electron transfer.** OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). The substance oxidized is the reducing agent; the substance reduced is the oxidizing agent.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Balancing equations:** balance atoms first, then charge if necessary. Common pattern: balance metals, then nonmetals, then H, then O, then check charge.
- **Stoichiometry pipeline:**
  1. Convert given (mass, volume of gas, volume of solution) to MOLES.
  2. Use mole ratio from balanced equation.
  3. Convert moles of target back to whatever units the question wants.
- **Limiting reagent:** the reactant that runs out first determines maximum product. To find it, calculate how much product each reactant could individually produce; the smaller answer wins, and that reactant is limiting.
- **Percent yield:** (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%.
- **Solubility rules** (memorize these; you'll use them constantly):
  - All Group 1 (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, etc.) and ammonium (NH₄⁺) salts → SOLUBLE.
  - All nitrates (NO₃⁻), acetates (CH₃COO⁻) → SOLUBLE.
  - Most chlorides, bromides, iodides → SOLUBLE except Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺.
  - Most sulfates → SOLUBLE except Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺ (slightly).
  - Most carbonates, phosphates, hydroxides, sulfides → INSOLUBLE except Group 1 and ammonium.
- **Strong acids** (memorize all 7): HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄, HClO₃.
- **Strong bases** (memorize): all Group 1 hydroxides (LiOH, NaOH, KOH, etc.) and heavy Group 2 hydroxides (Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂).
- **Net ionic equation procedure:**
  1. Write the balanced molecular equation.
  2. Write the COMPLETE ionic equation: dissociate every soluble strong electrolyte into its ions.
  3. Cancel SPECTATOR ions (those appearing unchanged on both sides).
  4. What remains is the net ionic equation.
- **Spectators are ions present in solution but not participating in the reaction.** Common spectators: Na⁺, K⁺, NO₃⁻, ClO₄⁻ (almost always spectators because they don't form precipitates or weak acids/bases easily).
- **Reaction types:**
  - Synthesis (combination): A + B → AB. Example: 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O.
  - Decomposition: AB → A + B. Example: 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂.
  - Single replacement: A + BC → AC + B. Example: Zn + 2 HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂.
  - Double replacement (metathesis): AB + CD → AD + CB. Example: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃.
  - Combustion: CₓHᵧ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
  - Acid-base (neutralization): acid + base → salt + water.
- **Oxidation numbers** (memorize the rules):
  - Free elements: 0.
  - Monatomic ion: equal to its charge.
  - O is usually −2 (except in peroxides where it's −1, and OF₂ where it's +2).
  - H is usually +1 (except in metal hydrides where it's −1).
  - F is always −1.
  - Sum in a neutral compound = 0; in a polyatomic ion = ion's charge.
- **OIL RIG:** Oxidation Is Loss (of e⁻), Reduction Is Gain (of e⁻).
- **Half-reactions:** split a redox into separate oxidation and reduction halves.
- **Titration basics:** at the equivalence point, moles of acid = moles of base. M_acid × V_acid = M_base × V_base (for monoprotic acid + monobasic base in 1:1 ratio).

### Adds for [4]

- **Net ionic for a precipitation reaction worked through:**
  - Molecular: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)
  - Complete ionic: Ag⁺ + NO₃⁻ + Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s) + Na⁺ + NO₃⁻
  - Net ionic: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)
  - Notice: Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectators and cancel out.
- **Net ionic for acid-base (strong acid + strong base):**
  - Molecular: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
  - Net ionic: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)
  - This is the universal net ionic for ANY strong acid + strong base.
- **Net ionic for acid-base (strong acid + weak base, like ammonia):**
  - Molecular: HCl + NH₃ → NH₄Cl
  - Complete ionic: H⁺ + Cl⁻ + NH₃ → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻
  - Net ionic: H⁺(aq) + NH₃(aq) → NH₄⁺(aq)
  - Note: NH₃ stays as a molecule (weak base, doesn't dissociate in solution).
- **Net ionic for weak acid + strong base:**
  - Molecular: CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
  - Net ionic: CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)
  - Note: weak acid stays UNDISSOCIATED in the net ionic; conjugate base appears as ion.
- **The "how to know what stays as molecule vs ion" rule for ionic equations:**
  - SPLIT into ions: strong acids, strong bases, soluble salts.
  - KEEP TOGETHER as molecule: weak acids (HF, CH₃COOH, HCN), weak bases (NH₃), insoluble salts (precipitates), water, gases.
- **Conjugate acid-base pairs:** an acid donates H⁺ to become its conjugate base. HCl → Cl⁻ (HCl/Cl⁻ pair). NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ (NH₃/NH₄⁺ pair).
- **Strong acid HX dissociates fully:** HX(aq) → H⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq). The resulting [H⁺] equals the initial acid concentration (for monoprotic).
- **Identifying redox reactions:** assign oxidation numbers to all atoms. If any atom's oxidation number changes, it's a redox reaction. Common giveaways:
  - Free element on either side (O₂ on reactant side, Cu metal on product side) almost always means redox.
  - Single replacement reactions are ALWAYS redox.
  - Combustion reactions are ALWAYS redox.
  - Double replacement reactions are NEVER redox.
- **Activity series for single-replacement:** more reactive metal displaces less reactive metal from solution. Memorize the relative order: Li > K > Na > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au.
- **Limiting reagent + percent yield combined:**
  - Identify limiting reagent.
  - Calculate theoretical yield from limiting reagent.
  - % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100.
- **Stoichiometric titration calculation:** at equivalence point for HCl + NaOH 1:1, M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. For different stoichiometry like H₂SO₄ + 2 NaOH (1:2), use M_acid × V_acid × 2 = M_base × V_base.
- **Half-reaction balancing in acidic solution:**
  1. Balance all atoms except O and H.
  2. Balance O by adding H₂O.
  3. Balance H by adding H⁺.
  4. Balance charge by adding e⁻.
- **Half-reaction balancing in basic solution:** same procedure as acidic, then add OH⁻ to both sides equal to H⁺ count to neutralize, simplify any H₂O on both sides.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why net ionic equations matter chemically (the [5] framing):** spectator ions don't participate in the chemistry — they're just along for the ride to maintain electrical neutrality. The net ionic captures the actual chemical transformation. When AO grades a "write the net ionic for the reaction" question, they're testing whether you understand WHICH species are reacting, not just whether you can balance.
- **Recognizing reactions from particulate diagrams:** AP often shows you a "before mixing" diagram of two solutions and a "after mixing" diagram, and asks you to write the net ionic. The particulate diagram tells you which species ended up as molecules (precipitates, water, gases) vs which stayed as ions.
- **The [5] move on identifying weak vs strong acids:** if the problem doesn't tell you explicitly, infer from context. Acetic acid (vinegar, CH₃COOH) is the AP's go-to weak acid. HCN, HF, HClO are weak. The 7 strong acids are memorized; everything else is weak by default.
- **Half-reactions and electron balance.** For a balanced redox equation, electrons LOST in oxidation = electrons GAINED in reduction. If the half-reaction electron counts don't match, multiply through to make them equal. Example: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ paired with Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag requires multiplying the Ag half-reaction by 2: 2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag. Combined: Cu + 2Ag⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2Ag.
- **Disproportionation:** a single substance is both oxidized and reduced. Classic example: Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HClO (Cl₂ disproportionates from oxidation state 0 to both −1 and +1).
- **Comproportionation:** two species of the same element in different oxidation states combine to form a single intermediate state. Example: 5 Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻ + 6 H⁺ → 3 Cl₂ + 3 H₂O.
- **The [5] insight on titration stoichiometry:** a polyprotic acid (H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄) requires multiple equivalence points. The titration curve has multiple "jumps." Diprotic acids = 2 equivalence points; triprotic = 3.
- **Mole-to-mole ratios are coefficients in the balanced equation, not the formulas.** N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃ tells you 1 mole N₂ : 3 moles H₂ : 2 moles NH₃. Forgetting the coefficient is the most common stoichiometry error.
- **Reaction prediction (not common on AP but useful):**
  - Acid + carbonate → salt + water + CO₂ (gas).
  - Acid + active metal → salt + H₂ (gas).
  - Two soluble salts → check solubility table for possible precipitate.
  - Strong acid + weak base → conjugate acid (often acidic salt solution).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Stoichiometry with limiting reagent

Given the reaction 2 Al + 3 Cl₂ → 2 AlCl₃, you mix 5.40 g Al with 8.50 g Cl₂. Find the limiting reagent and the theoretical yield of AlCl₃.

- **Step 1.** Convert each reactant to moles:
  - mol Al = 5.40 / 26.98 = 0.200 mol
  - mol Cl₂ = 8.50 / 70.90 = 0.120 mol
- **Step 2.** Calculate moles AlCl₃ each reactant could produce:
  - From Al: 0.200 mol Al × (2 mol AlCl₃ / 2 mol Al) = 0.200 mol AlCl₃
  - From Cl₂: 0.120 mol Cl₂ × (2 mol AlCl₃ / 3 mol Cl₂) = 0.0800 mol AlCl₃
- **Step 3.** Smaller value wins: Cl₂ is the limiting reagent. Maximum AlCl₃ = 0.0800 mol.
- **Step 4.** Convert to grams: 0.0800 mol × (133.33 g/mol) = 10.7 g AlCl₃ (theoretical yield).
- **Common error to avoid:** comparing the mass ratio instead of the mole ratio. Stoichiometry always works in moles.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Writing a net ionic for a precipitation reaction

Mixing aqueous Pb(NO₃)₂ with aqueous KI produces a yellow precipitate. Write the molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations.

- **Step 1.** Predict the products. Swapping partners: Pb(NO₃)₂ + KI → PbI₂ + KNO₃. Check solubility: PbI₂ is INSOLUBLE (lead halides are insoluble exceptions); KNO₃ is soluble. So the precipitate is PbI₂.
- **Step 2.** Balance the molecular equation: Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2 KNO₃(aq).
- **Step 3.** Write the complete ionic. Dissociate all soluble strong electrolytes:
  - Pb²⁺(aq) + 2 NO₃⁻(aq) + 2 K⁺(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2 K⁺(aq) + 2 NO₃⁻(aq).
- **Step 4.** Cancel spectators: 2 NO₃⁻ and 2 K⁺ appear on both sides → cancel.
- **Step 5.** Net ionic: Pb²⁺(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s).
- **AP-style answer:** "The net ionic equation captures the actual chemical transformation: aqueous lead(II) ions and iodide ions combine to form solid lead(II) iodide. The potassium and nitrate ions are spectators and do not participate."

### Example 3 [4] — Net ionic for weak acid + strong base

A solution of acetic acid (CH₃COOH) is neutralized by aqueous KOH. Write the net ionic equation.

- **Step 1.** Molecular: CH₃COOH(aq) + KOH(aq) → CH₃COOK(aq) + H₂O(l).
- **Step 2.** Complete ionic. Acetic acid is WEAK → stays as molecule. KOH is strong → dissociates. Potassium acetate is soluble → ionizes:
  - CH₃COOH(aq) + K⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + K⁺(aq) + H₂O(l).
- **Step 3.** Cancel K⁺ (spectator).
- **Step 4.** Net ionic: CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l).
- **[4] phrase:** "Because acetic acid is a WEAK acid, it remains undissociated in solution and is written as a molecule in the net ionic equation. The conjugate base, acetate, appears as an ion because it dissociates fully as a soluble ionic compound."

### Example 4 [4] — Identify a redox reaction and balance half-reactions

Consider the reaction: Zn(s) + 2 H⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + H₂(g). Identify the species oxidized and reduced. Write the half-reactions.

- **Step 1.** Assign oxidation numbers:
  - Zn(s) = 0
  - H⁺ = +1
  - Zn²⁺ = +2
  - H₂(g) = 0
- **Step 2.** Identify changes:
  - Zn: 0 → +2 (LOST 2 electrons → OXIDIZED → Zn is the reducing agent)
  - H: +1 → 0 (GAINED 1 electron each, 2 total → REDUCED → H⁺ is the oxidizing agent)
- **Step 3.** Half-reactions:
  - Oxidation: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
  - Reduction: 2 H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂
- **Step 4.** Combine (electrons cancel because both sides have 2): Zn + 2 H⁺ → Zn²⁺ + H₂. Matches the original.
- **OIL RIG check:** Zn lost electrons (oxidized); H gained electrons (reduced).

### Example 5 [3] — Titration stoichiometry

How many mL of 0.250 M NaOH are needed to neutralize 25.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl?

- **Step 1.** Stoichiometry: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. Mole ratio is 1:1.
- **Step 2.** Apply M_acid × V_acid = M_base × V_base (valid for 1:1 stoichiometry):
  - (0.100 M)(25.0 mL) = (0.250 M)(V_base)
  - V_base = (0.100)(25.0) / 0.250 = 10.0 mL
- **Sanity check:** the base is more concentrated (0.250 M vs 0.100 M), so we need less of it. 10.0 mL < 25.0 mL ✓
- **Common error to avoid:** if the stoichiometry is NOT 1:1 (e.g., H₂SO₄ + 2 NaOH), include the mole ratio: M_acid × V_acid × 2 = M_base × V_base.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Forgetting to balance equations before doing stoichiometry.** Mole ratios come from coefficients. An unbalanced equation gives wrong ratios.
2. **Mass-based mole ratios instead of mole-based.** Stoichiometry is mole-to-mole, not gram-to-gram.
3. **Not identifying limiting reagent before calculating yield.** The reactant in excess does NOT determine maximum product.
4. **Splitting weak acids in net ionic equations.** Acetic acid stays as CH₃COOH — do NOT write H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻.
5. **Splitting insoluble compounds.** Precipitates stay together. PbI₂(s) does NOT become Pb²⁺ + 2 I⁻ — that's the dissolution direction, not the precipitation.
6. **Splitting water in ionic equations.** H₂O stays as a molecule.
7. **Forgetting state symbols (s, l, g, aq).** AP rubrics often require state symbols on net ionic equations.
8. **Net ionic equation not balanced for charge.** Both sides must have the same total charge.
9. **Wrong oxidation number on H or O.** H is +1 except in metal hydrides (NaH, where H = −1). O is −2 except in peroxides (H₂O₂, where O = −1).
10. **Confusing strong vs weak.** Strong/weak refers to DEGREE OF DISSOCIATION, not concentration. A dilute strong acid is still a strong acid.
11. **Calling a reaction "neutralization" when one or both species are weak.** Strong acid + strong base produces a neutral salt solution; strong acid + weak base produces an acidic salt solution; weak acid + strong base produces a basic salt solution.
12. **Activity series direction confusion.** A more active metal displaces a less active metal. Zn displaces Cu²⁺ from solution because Zn is more active than Cu. Cu does NOT displace Zn²⁺.
13. **Treating combustion as a non-redox reaction.** Combustion is ALWAYS redox: the fuel is oxidized (gains O), O₂ is reduced.
14. **Not multiplying half-reactions to balance electrons.** The electrons lost in oxidation must equal the electrons gained in reduction. Multiply each half-reaction by the appropriate factor before combining.
15. **Stoichiometric titration formula misapplication.** M_acid × V_acid = M_base × V_base ONLY works for 1:1 mole ratio. For polyprotic acids or different ratios, include the stoichiometric coefficient.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For balancing equations:**
- Show the balanced equation explicitly.
- Include state symbols (s, l, g, aq) when the question involves solutions or phases.
- Charge balance applies in net ionic equations.

**For net ionic equations:**
- Cancel spectators visibly (cross them out or just write the net ionic with explanation).
- Show charge conservation.
- Identify weak acids/bases as molecules; strong as ions.
- Identify insoluble compounds (precipitates, water, gases) as molecules.

**For stoichiometry problems:**
- Show the balanced equation.
- Show the mol-to-mol conversion using coefficients explicitly.
- Show the mass-to-mole and mole-to-mass conversions with units.
- Include sig figs appropriate to the data.

**For limiting reagent + percent yield:**
- Calculate theoretical yield from BOTH reactants; identify limiting from smaller answer.
- Show the % yield formula.
- Watch for "actual yield" vs "theoretical yield" wording.

**For redox identification:**
- Assign oxidation numbers to ALL atoms.
- Identify which changes (these are the oxidized/reduced species).
- Name the oxidizing and reducing agents specifically.

**For titration calculations:**
- State the equivalence point relationship.
- Show M × V × stoichiometric ratio = M × V × stoichiometric ratio.
- Convert mL appropriately.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The balanced net ionic equation removes spectator ions, leaving only the species that participate in the chemical transformation: [equation]."
2. "[Substance X] is the limiting reagent because it produces the smaller amount of product when consumed completely. Once X is exhausted, the reaction stops, regardless of how much excess Y remains."
3. "[Substance A] is oxidized: its oxidation number increases from [n₁] to [n₂], indicating loss of electrons. [Substance B] is reduced: its oxidation number decreases from [m₁] to [m₂], indicating gain of electrons. Therefore A is the reducing agent and B is the oxidizing agent."
4. "Because [acetic acid] is a weak acid, it remains undissociated in aqueous solution. In the net ionic equation, it is written as the molecule [CH₃COOH], not as separate ions."
5. "At the equivalence point, the moles of acid equal the moles of base (adjusted for stoichiometric ratio). Applying M_acid × V_acid = M_base × V_base × (ratio): [calculation]."
6. "The yellow precipitate is PbI₂, which forms because the product of the lead and iodide ion concentrations exceeds the solubility limit, driving precipitation."
7. "The reaction is classified as [reaction type] because [specific criterion: e.g., one element displaces another from a compound / a single compound breaks into multiple products]."
