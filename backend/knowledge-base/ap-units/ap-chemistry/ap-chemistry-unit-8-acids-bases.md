## Big Ideas

1. **Acids donate H⁺; bases accept H⁺.** This Brønsted-Lowry framing handles every acid-base reaction on AP. Lewis (electron-pair) framing rarely appears on the exam.
2. **Strong acids/bases dissociate completely; weak acids/bases dissociate partially.** Strong → use stoichiometry directly. Weak → use Ka, Kb, ICE tables.
3. **pH and pOH are negative log scales.** pH = −log[H⁺]; pOH = −log[OH⁻]; pH + pOH = 14 at 25°C. Each unit of pH = 10× change in [H⁺].
4. **Buffers resist pH changes through the conjugate-acid-base pair.** A weak acid HA mixed with its conjugate base A⁻ neutralizes added H⁺ (consumed by A⁻) or added OH⁻ (consumed by HA). Best buffers: pH ≈ pKa of the weak acid.
5. **Titration curves reveal Ka.** The half-equivalence point of a weak-acid + strong-base titration occurs when half the weak acid has been neutralized. At that point, [HA] = [A⁻], so by Henderson-Hasselbalch, pH = pKa. This is the most exploited fact in AP titration questions.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Brønsted-Lowry definitions:**
  - Acid = proton (H⁺) donor.
  - Base = proton acceptor.
  - Conjugate acid: what a base becomes after accepting H⁺.
  - Conjugate base: what an acid becomes after donating H⁺.
  - Example: HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻. HCl is the acid; H₂O is the base. H₃O⁺ is the conjugate acid of water; Cl⁻ is the conjugate base of HCl.
- **Strong acids (memorize all 7):** HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄, HClO₃.
- **Strong bases:** Group 1 hydroxides (LiOH, NaOH, KOH, RbOH, CsOH) and heavy Group 2 hydroxides (Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂).
- **Common weak acids (good to recognize):** acetic acid (CH₃COOH), formic acid (HCOOH), HF, HCN, NH₄⁺, H₂CO₃, H₃PO₄.
- **Common weak bases:** NH₃, methylamine (CH₃NH₂), pyridine, conjugate bases of weak acids (acetate, fluoride, etc.).
- **Self-ionization of water:** 2 H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C.
- **pH definitions:**
  - pH = −log[H⁺]
  - pOH = −log[OH⁻]
  - pKa = −log(Ka)
  - pH + pOH = 14 at 25°C
- **For strong acids:** [H⁺] = initial concentration of the acid (since fully dissociated). pH = −log(concentration).
- **For strong bases:** [OH⁻] = initial concentration × number of OH⁻ per formula unit (e.g., for 0.10 M Ca(OH)₂: [OH⁻] = 0.20 M).
- **For weak acids:** use Ka and ICE table.
  - HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA].
  - With initial [HA] = C and assuming x small: x = √(Ka × C), where x = [H⁺].
- **For weak bases:** use Kb and ICE table similarly.
  - B + H₂O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻, Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B].
- **Ka × Kb = Kw for a conjugate acid-base pair.** So pKa + pKb = 14.
- **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation** (for buffers):
  - pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])
- **Buffer = weak acid + its conjugate base** (or weak base + its conjugate acid).
- **Equivalence point of titration:** moles of acid = moles of base (adjusted for stoichiometry). The point where the indicator changes (the actual experimental "endpoint") should match the equivalence point if the indicator is well-chosen.
- **Half-equivalence point:** halfway to the equivalence point. At this point, exactly half of the original weak acid has been converted to its conjugate base, so [HA] = [A⁻]. By Henderson-Hasselbalch, pH = pKa here.

### Adds for [4]

- **Strength from molecular structure:**
  - Binary acids HX: strength increases down the group (HI > HBr > HCl >> HF). Why: weaker H–X bond = easier to ionize. (Not because of higher electronegativity — F is most EN but HF is weakest binary acid.)
  - Oxyacids: more O atoms / more electronegative central atom → stronger acid. HClO₄ > HClO₃ > HClO₂ > HClO. The extra O atoms stabilize the conjugate base by spreading negative charge over multiple atoms.
- **Conjugate base stability is the key.** A strong acid has a stable (low-energy) conjugate base. The more stable the conjugate base, the stronger the acid. Stabilization mechanisms include resonance, electronegativity, size.
- **Polyprotic acids:**
  - H₂SO₄: first H⁺ is strong (fully dissociates), second is weak (Ka₂ ≈ 0.012).
  - H₃PO₄: all three Ka values — Ka₁ = 7.5 × 10⁻³, Ka₂ = 6.2 × 10⁻⁸, Ka₃ = 4.8 × 10⁻¹³.
  - Each successive ionization is harder (positive charge buildup makes the next H⁺ more reluctant to leave).
- **Salt hydrolysis (predicting solution pH from a salt):**
  - Salt of strong acid + strong base (NaCl, KNO₃) → neutral (pH = 7).
  - Salt of strong acid + weak base (NH₄Cl, NH₄NO₃) → acidic (NH₄⁺ donates H⁺).
  - Salt of weak acid + strong base (NaCH₃COO, NaF) → basic (anion accepts H⁺).
  - Salt of weak acid + weak base (NH₄CH₃COO) → depends on Ka vs Kb of the components.
- **Buffer preparation:** mix a weak acid HA with a salt containing its conjugate base A⁻ (often the sodium salt). Or mix a weak acid with strong base in less-than-stoichiometric amount (so some weak acid is left and some conjugate base is formed).
- **Buffer pH is set by Henderson-Hasselbalch:**
  - Choose weak acid with pKa near desired pH (within ±1 unit).
  - Adjust the [A⁻]/[HA] ratio to fine-tune.
- **Buffer capacity:** the amount of acid or base a buffer can absorb without significant pH change. Larger absolute concentrations of HA and A⁻ → more capacity. Buffers with [A⁻]/[HA] near 1 have maximum capacity.
- **Titration curve features (weak acid + strong base):**
  - Initial pH: depends on Ka and concentration of weak acid (low-pH region).
  - Buffer region: a long flat plateau where pH changes slowly because the buffer system absorbs the added base.
  - Half-equivalence point: pH = pKa.
  - Equivalence point: pH > 7 (because the conjugate base of a weak acid is itself a weak base).
  - Past equivalence: rapid rise to high pH dominated by excess strong base.
- **Titration curve for strong acid + strong base:**
  - Equivalence point: pH = 7 (neutral salt forms).
  - Vertical jump near equivalence point.
  - No buffer region.
- **Indicator choice:** the indicator's pKa (color change pH range) should match the equivalence point pH. For weak acid + strong base titration with equivalence pH ~9, choose phenolphthalein (transitions pH 8–10).
- **Equivalence point pH calculation:**
  - For weak acid + strong base: at equivalence, all HA has converted to A⁻. Solution is a weak base solution. Use Kb of A⁻ (= Kw/Ka) to calculate pH.
  - For weak base + strong acid: at equivalence, all base has converted to BH⁺. Solution is a weak acid. Use Ka of BH⁺ to calculate pH.

### Adds for [5]

- **The "buffer math first, then Henderson-Hasselbalch" workflow** for adding strong acid/base to a buffer:
  1. Set up a stoichiometry table: how much HA and A⁻ are present after the strong acid/base reacts with the buffer.
  2. Apply the neutralization reaction completely (since strong acid/base reacts essentially fully).
  3. Calculate new [HA] and [A⁻].
  4. Apply Henderson-Hasselbalch to find new pH.
  5. NEVER plug into Henderson-Hasselbalch BEFORE doing the stoichiometry — that's a guaranteed wrong answer.
- **Why pH = pKa at the half-equivalence point** (the [5] derivation):
  - At equivalence, n_acid = n_base added. At half-equivalence, half the acid has been neutralized.
  - Stoichiometry: original moles HA = n. After half-neutralization: moles HA = n/2; moles A⁻ = n/2.
  - Concentrations: [HA] = [A⁻] (assuming same volume).
  - Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log(1) = pKa.
  - This is why titration curves of weak acids are used experimentally to determine pKa values.
- **Acid/base properties of metal cations.** Small, highly charged cations (Al³⁺, Fe³⁺) are acidic in water because they polarize coordinated water molecules: Al(H₂O)₆³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ Al(H₂O)₅(OH)²⁺ + H₃O⁺. This is why solutions of AlCl₃ are noticeably acidic.
- **Why a buffer at pH = pKa has maximum capacity.** The slope dpH/dV (volume of strong base added) is minimum when [HA] = [A⁻] — meaning the system absorbs the most strong base for the smallest pH change. Adding base shifts the ratio, but starting from a 1:1 ratio gives the most "room" to shift in either direction.
- **Fraction protonated diagram.** For a weak acid HA, plot the fraction in HA form vs the fraction in A⁻ form as pH varies. At pH = pKa, fractions are equal. At pH > pKa + 1, mostly A⁻. At pH < pKa − 1, mostly HA. This visualization clarifies all titration and buffer questions.
- **Polyprotic titration curves** have multiple equivalence points (one per acidic proton). H₃PO₄ has three. The pH at the half-equivalence point of the n-th ionization equals pKa_n.
- **Common-ion effect on weak acid ionization.** Adding NaA (the salt of the conjugate base) to an HA solution suppresses HA ionization (Le Chatelier — adding product shifts back). This is the buffer mechanism viewed equilibrium-wise.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — pH of strong acid

What is the pH of 0.025 M HCl?

- **Step 1.** HCl is a strong acid → fully dissociates: [H⁺] = 0.025 M.
- **Step 2.** pH = −log[H⁺] = −log(0.025) = 1.60.
- **Sanity check:** strong acid at modest concentration → pH between 1 and 2 ✓.

### Example 2 [3][4] — pH of weak acid

Calculate the pH of 0.10 M acetic acid (CH₃COOH). Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.

- **Step 1.** Set up ICE:
  ```
                 CH₃COOH       H⁺      CH₃COO⁻
  Initial         0.10           0         0
  Change          -x            +x         +x
  Equilibrium     0.10 - x       x         x
  ```
- **Step 2.** Ka = x² / (0.10 − x) = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.
- **Step 3.** Assume x << 0.10: x² / 0.10 = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ → x² = 1.8 × 10⁻⁶ → x = 1.34 × 10⁻³ M.
- **Step 4.** Check: x / 0.10 = 1.34% < 5% — approximation valid.
- **Step 5.** pH = −log(1.34 × 10⁻³) = 2.87.
- **Sanity check:** weak acid → pH higher than equivalent strong acid (which would be pH 1) ✓.

### Example 3 [4] — Buffer pH calculation

A buffer is prepared by mixing 0.20 mol acetic acid and 0.30 mol sodium acetate in 1.00 L of water. Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵. Calculate the pH.

- **Step 1.** Identify HA and A⁻: HA = CH₃COOH (0.20 M); A⁻ = CH₃COO⁻ (0.30 M).
- **Step 2.** pKa = −log(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.74.
- **Step 3.** Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 4.74 + log(0.30/0.20) = 4.74 + log(1.5) = 4.74 + 0.176 = 4.92.
- **Sanity check:** more conjugate base than weak acid → pH > pKa ✓.

### Example 4 [4][5] — Adding strong base to a buffer

To the buffer in Example 3, add 0.05 mol NaOH. Calculate the new pH (assume volume change negligible).

- **Step 1 (stoichiometry FIRST!):** OH⁻ reacts completely with HA to form A⁻ + H₂O. Moles initial: HA = 0.20, A⁻ = 0.30. Add 0.05 mol OH⁻:
  - HA: 0.20 − 0.05 = 0.15 mol
  - A⁻: 0.30 + 0.05 = 0.35 mol
  - OH⁻ remaining: 0 (fully consumed)
- **Step 2 (NOW Henderson-Hasselbalch):** pH = 4.74 + log(0.35/0.15) = 4.74 + log(2.33) = 4.74 + 0.368 = 5.11.
- **Step 3.** Compare to original pH (4.92): pH rose by only 0.19 — that's the buffer working. Without buffer, adding 0.05 mol OH⁻ to 1.00 L would give [OH⁻] = 0.05 M, pOH = 1.30, pH = 12.70 — a huge change. The buffer absorbed essentially all of it.
- **CRITICAL note:** if you skipped step 1 and plugged 0.05 mol OH⁻ directly into Henderson-Hasselbalch, you'd get a wildly wrong answer. ALWAYS do the stoichiometry first.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Titration curve and finding Ka

A 25.0 mL sample of an unknown weak acid (HA) is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. The titration requires 30.0 mL of NaOH to reach the equivalence point. After 15.0 mL of NaOH has been added, the pH is 4.60. Determine Ka of the weak acid.

- **Step 1.** 15.0 mL = half of 30.0 mL → this is the HALF-EQUIVALENCE point.
- **Step 2.** At half-equivalence: pH = pKa.
- **Step 3.** pKa = 4.60.
- **Step 4.** Ka = 10^(−pKa) = 10^(−4.60) = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵.
- **AP-style explanation:** "At the half-equivalence point, exactly half of the original weak acid has been neutralized to its conjugate base, giving [HA] = [A⁻]. Substituting into Henderson-Hasselbalch, the log term vanishes (log 1 = 0), so pH = pKa. The measured pH of 4.60 directly gives pKa = 4.60, hence Ka = 10⁻⁴·⁶⁰ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵."

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Henderson-Hasselbalch BEFORE stoichiometry.** When strong acid/base is added to a buffer, REACT FIRST (consume HA or A⁻ completely), THEN apply Henderson-Hasselbalch with the new amounts. Skipping step 1 gives wrong answers.
2. **Treating weak acids as fully dissociated.** Only the 7 strong acids fully dissociate. Acetic acid, HF, HCN, formic acid, NH₄⁺ do NOT.
3. **Forgetting stoichiometric coefficient for polybasic strong bases.** 0.10 M Ca(OH)₂ gives [OH⁻] = 0.20 M because each formula unit releases 2 OH⁻.
4. **Using log instead of −log for pH.** pH = −log[H⁺]. pH of 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ M H⁺ is 5, not −5.
5. **Misidentifying conjugate acid-base pairs.** Conjugate base of HCl is Cl⁻ (HCl loses H⁺ to become Cl⁻). Conjugate acid of NH₃ is NH₄⁺ (NH₃ gains H⁺ to become NH₄⁺).
6. **Salt hydrolysis prediction errors.** NaCl → neutral. NH₄Cl → acidic (NH₄⁺ is conjugate acid of weak base NH₃). NaF → basic (F⁻ is conjugate base of weak acid HF).
7. **Half-equivalence point confused with equivalence point.** Half-eq: pH = pKa. Equivalence: pH > 7 (for weak acid + strong base) or pH < 7 (for weak base + strong acid).
8. **Equivalence point pH = 7 for weak-strong titrations.** Wrong — only for STRONG + STRONG titrations does the equivalence point sit at exactly pH 7.
9. **Forgetting that polyprotic acids have multiple equivalence points.** H₃PO₄ has 3, with corresponding pH plateaus at pKa₁, pKa₂, pKa₃.
10. **Wrong indicator choice.** Indicator color change should occur within the steepest part of the titration curve (usually within 1 pH unit of the equivalence point). Phenolphthalein for weak acid + strong base; methyl orange for strong acid + weak base.
11. **5% approximation failures.** When Ka is large or initial concentration is small, the assumption x << initial breaks down. Always check.
12. **Confusing Ka with [H⁺].** Ka is a constant; [H⁺] is a concentration. They have different units and meanings. For weak acid HA at concentration C (assuming x small): [H⁺] ≈ √(Ka × C).
13. **Buffer pH outside ±1 of pKa.** Buffers are most effective when pH ≈ pKa. Outside ±1 unit, capacity drops sharply.
14. **Adding water dilutes a buffer's CAPACITY but not its pH.** A buffer's pH is set by the [A⁻]/[HA] RATIO, which is unchanged by dilution. But buffer capacity drops with dilution.
15. **Forgetting Kw at non-25°C temperatures.** Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. At higher T, Kw is larger, so [H⁺] in pure water is higher, and the "neutral" pH is below 7. AP rarely tests this directly but is aware of the principle.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For pH calculations:**
- For strong acid/base: take the log of concentration directly.
- For weak acid/base: set up ICE table, apply Ka or Kb, solve for x = [H⁺] or [OH⁻], take pH.
- Show ALL steps; AP gives partial credit liberally if the setup is right.

**For Henderson-Hasselbalch problems:**
- Identify the conjugate acid-base pair.
- Plug in the concentration ratio.
- For "what happens after adding strong base/acid": stoichiometry FIRST, then Henderson-Hasselbalch with new concentrations.

**For titration curve problems:**
- Identify regions: initial (weak acid alone), buffer region, half-eq (pH = pKa), equivalence point, beyond equivalence.
- For half-equivalence: pH = pKa, no further calculation needed.
- For equivalence point: identify what's left in solution (conjugate base for weak-strong, conjugate acid for strong-weak), use that species' Kb or Ka.

**For buffer questions:**
- State the conjugate pair.
- Apply Henderson-Hasselbalch.
- For capacity questions, note that more concentrated buffers absorb more strong acid/base before significant pH change.

**For acid strength comparison:**
- Use molecular structure: more electronegative atoms near the H, more O atoms in oxyacid → stronger.
- Use bond strength: weaker H–X bond → stronger acid (binary acids).
- Use conjugate base stability: more stable conjugate base → stronger acid.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Acetic acid is a weak acid; in solution, only a small fraction of CH₃COOH ionizes to H⁺ and CH₃COO⁻. The equilibrium concentration of H⁺ is found by setting up an ICE table with Ka = [H⁺][CH₃COO⁻]/[CH₃COOH] = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵."
2. "By Henderson-Hasselbalch, pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) = [calculation]."
3. "When NaOH is added to this buffer, the strong base reacts completely with the weak acid HA to form additional A⁻. After accounting for the stoichiometry, the new concentrations are [HA] = [...] and [A⁻] = [...]. Substituting into Henderson-Hasselbalch yields pH = [...]."
4. "At the half-equivalence point, exactly half of the original weak acid has been neutralized to its conjugate base, so [HA] = [A⁻]. By Henderson-Hasselbalch, pH = pKa + log(1) = pKa."
5. "At the equivalence point of a weak acid + strong base titration, all the weak acid has been converted to its conjugate base. The solution contains only A⁻, which is a weak base. The pH is greater than 7, calculated using Kb = Kw/Ka of the conjugate base."
6. "The conjugate base of a strong acid is so weak that it does not affect pH; the conjugate base of a weak acid is itself a weak base and produces a basic solution."
7. "Among oxyacids of chlorine, HClO₄ > HClO₃ > HClO₂ > HClO in acid strength because more terminal oxygens stabilize the conjugate base by spreading the negative charge over multiple electronegative atoms via resonance."
