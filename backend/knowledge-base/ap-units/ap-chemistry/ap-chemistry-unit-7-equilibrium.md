## Big Ideas

1. **Equilibrium is dynamic, not static.** Forward and reverse reactions are still occurring at equilibrium — they're just occurring at the same rate. Macroscopic concentrations are constant; microscopic processes continue.
2. **K is a fixed number at a given temperature.** Adding more reactant doesn't change K; raising temperature does. This is the single most important conceptual point about equilibrium.
3. **Q vs K tells you direction.** Calculate Q from current concentrations. Compare to K: Q < K → reaction proceeds forward; Q > K → reverse; Q = K → at equilibrium.
4. **Le Chatelier predicts response to disturbance.** A system at equilibrium that is disturbed will shift to partially counteract the disturbance. Add reactant → shifts toward products; increase pressure on a gas system with more moles on one side → shifts toward fewer moles; add heat to exothermic → shifts toward reactants.
5. **K and ΔG are linked: ΔG° = −RT·ln(K).** Large K means thermodynamically favorable forward reaction. Small K means favorable reverse. K = 1 means ΔG° = 0 (equally favorable).

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Equilibrium expression for a A + b B ⇌ c C + d D:**
  - K = [C]^c[D]^d / ([A]^a[B]^b)
  - Concentrations of products in numerator, reactants in denominator, each raised to its coefficient.
- **Pure solids and pure liquids do NOT appear** in K expressions. Their "concentrations" are constant and absorbed into K.
- **Kc vs Kp:**
  - Kc uses molar concentrations (M).
  - Kp uses partial pressures (atm).
  - For gas reactions, you can use either; for solution reactions, use Kc.
- **Reaction quotient Q:** same expression as K but uses CURRENT (non-equilibrium) concentrations. Compare Q to K to predict direction.
- **Le Chatelier's principle:**
  - Add reactant → shift toward products.
  - Add product → shift toward reactants.
  - Remove reactant → shift toward reactants.
  - Remove product → shift toward products.
  - Increase pressure (for gas system) → shift toward side with FEWER moles of gas.
  - Decrease pressure → shift toward side with MORE moles of gas.
  - For exothermic reaction: add heat → shifts toward REACTANTS (heat is a "product"). Remove heat (cool) → shifts toward products.
  - For endothermic reaction: opposite.
- **Catalysts do NOT shift equilibrium.** They speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally; the system reaches equilibrium faster but at the same composition.
- **Inert (non-reacting) gases added at constant volume do NOT shift equilibrium** because they don't change the partial pressures of the reacting species.
- **Magnitude of K:**
  - K >> 1 (e.g., 10⁵): equilibrium favors PRODUCTS. Reaction goes nearly to completion.
  - K << 1 (e.g., 10⁻⁵): equilibrium favors REACTANTS. Very little product forms.
  - K ≈ 1: comparable amounts of reactants and products at equilibrium.
- **ICE table** (Initial, Change, Equilibrium): the standard tool for solving equilibrium concentration problems.
  1. Initial row: starting concentrations.
  2. Change row: how each species changes by the time equilibrium is reached (use stoichiometric ratios; reactants lose −x, products gain +x).
  3. Equilibrium row: I + C.
  4. Plug equilibrium values into K expression and solve for x.
- **Solubility product Ksp:** equilibrium constant for the dissolution of a sparingly soluble ionic compound.
  - For MX(s) ⇌ M⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq): Ksp = [M⁺][X⁻].
  - For M₂X(s) ⇌ 2 M⁺(aq) + X²⁻(aq): Ksp = [M⁺]²[X²⁻].
- **Molar solubility (s):** moles of compound dissolving per liter at saturation. Related to Ksp through the stoichiometry.
  - For MX: s² = Ksp → s = √Ksp.
  - For M₂X: (2s)²(s) = 4s³ = Ksp → s = (Ksp/4)^(1/3).
- **Common-ion effect:** if one of the ions in a Ksp expression is already present in solution from another source, the solubility of the salt DECREASES (Le Chatelier — adding product shifts dissolution backward).

### Adds for [4]

- **K as the position of equilibrium, not the rate.**
  - K is determined by ΔG (thermodynamics).
  - The rate at which equilibrium is approached is determined by Eₐ (kinetics).
  - These are independent: a reaction can be highly favorable (large K) but very slow (high Eₐ), or vice versa.
- **Effect of changes on K:**
  - Temperature change → DOES change K. Increasing T shifts the equilibrium for exothermic reactions backward (K decreases) and for endothermic reactions forward (K increases). This is the only way to change K's value.
  - Concentration changes → do NOT change K. They only shift Q to a new equilibrium position with the same K.
  - Pressure changes → do NOT change Kc but may change which direction the system shifts to remain at K.
  - Catalyst → does NOT change K.
- **Ice table with strong assumption:** when K is small (<10⁻⁴) and initial reactant concentrations are reasonable, you can often approximate "x is small" — i.e., assume initial concentration ≈ equilibrium concentration. Verify by checking that x < 5% of initial.
- **Q-K decision tree:**
  - Q < K: not enough product → reaction proceeds FORWARD.
  - Q > K: too much product → reaction proceeds REVERSE.
  - Q = K: at equilibrium, no net change.
- **Volume change in gas-phase equilibrium.** Decreasing volume increases all partial pressures (and concentrations). The system shifts to reduce pressure by going toward fewer moles of gas. Increasing volume: opposite.
- **Reversing a reaction:** K_reverse = 1/K_forward.
- **Multiplying a reaction by n:** K_new = K^n.
- **Adding two reactions:** K_total = K₁ × K₂.
- **Common-ion effect quantitative:** for AgCl in 0.10 M NaCl solution, Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻]. With [Cl⁻] effectively = 0.10 (from NaCl, dominant over the tiny amount from AgCl), molar solubility s = Ksp / 0.10. Compare to s = √Ksp in pure water — much smaller in NaCl solution.
- **pH and solubility:** for salts of weak acids (carbonates, phosphates, hydroxides), solubility INCREASES at low pH because H⁺ removes the anion (e.g., CO₃²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO₃⁻), shifting the dissolution equilibrium forward.
- **Particulate diagrams of equilibrium:**
  - Show both reactant and product particles in proportions reflecting K.
  - Large K → mostly products in the diagram.
  - Small K → mostly reactants.
  - Compare "before disturbance" and "after re-equilibration" to show shifts.

### Adds for [5]

- **The deeper "why K doesn't change with concentration" argument.** K is a ratio of activities (or concentrations) at equilibrium, defined by the thermodynamic minimum of free energy. Adding reactant temporarily makes Q < K, but the system relaxes back to a new state where the ratio (with new concentrations) once again equals K. The total amounts of each species change; the ratio doesn't.
- **The connection to ΔG.** ΔG = ΔG° + RT·ln(Q). At equilibrium, ΔG = 0, so ΔG° = −RT·ln(K). This means the standard free energy change directly determines K. A reaction with ΔG° = −10 kJ/mol at 298 K has K ≈ 56; ΔG° = +10 kJ/mol gives K ≈ 0.018.
- **Ksp for "less common" stoichiometries.** For Ca₃(PO₄)₂(s) ⇌ 3 Ca²⁺ + 2 PO₄³⁻: Ksp = [Ca²⁺]³[PO₄³⁻]². With molar solubility s: [Ca²⁺] = 3s, [PO₄³⁻] = 2s, so Ksp = (3s)³(2s)² = 108·s⁵, hence s = (Ksp/108)^(1/5).
- **Ksp temperature dependence.** Ksp values are tabulated at 25°C. For most ionic salts, dissolution is endothermic (positive ΔH), so Ksp increases with temperature — salts dissolve more in hot water. (A few exceptions: Ce₂(SO₄)₃ has retrograde solubility.)
- **Selective precipitation.** When two cations can both precipitate with a common anion, the one with the smaller Ksp (lower solubility) precipitates first. For example, in a solution containing Ag⁺ and Pb²⁺, adding Cl⁻ slowly precipitates AgCl first (Ksp ≈ 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰) before PbCl₂ (Ksp ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻⁵). The exact threshold depends on the precise [Cl⁻].
- **Molar solubility units check.** Always express s in mol/L. To convert to grams per 100 mL or other units, multiply by molar mass and adjust volume.
- **The 5% approximation rule** (when to NOT use the simplifying assumption): when Ka, Kb, Ksp, etc. are large enough that x is significant (>5% of initial concentration), you must solve the full quadratic. Common cutoffs:
  - Initial / K > 1000 → safe to approximate.
  - Initial / K < 1000 → consider the full quadratic.
- **Ksp predictions get harder with hydrolysis.** For salts of weak acids (like CaCO₃), the dissolved anion (CO₃²⁻) is itself a weak base and reacts with water (CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻), pulling more CaCO₃ into solution than the simple Ksp expression predicts. AP rarely tests this quantitatively but expects qualitative awareness.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Setting up K and identifying direction

For the reaction N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) ⇌ 2 NH₃(g), Kc = 0.500 at some temperature. A reaction mixture contains [N₂] = 0.10 M, [H₂] = 0.20 M, [NH₃] = 0.40 M. (a) Calculate Q. (b) Determine the direction the reaction will proceed.

- **Step 1.** Write the K expression: K = [NH₃]² / ([N₂][H₂]³).
- **Step 2.** Calculate Q with current concentrations:
  - Q = (0.40)² / [(0.10)(0.20)³] = 0.16 / (0.10 × 0.008) = 0.16 / 0.0008 = 200.
- **Step 3.** Compare Q to K. Q = 200; K = 0.500. Q >> K.
- **Step 4.** Since Q > K, there's too much product. Reaction proceeds in REVERSE direction (toward reactants).
- **AP-style answer:** "Q (200) is much greater than K (0.500), indicating that current product concentrations exceed equilibrium values. The system will shift toward reactants (reverse direction) until Q decreases to equal K."

### Example 2 [3][4] — ICE table with small K

For the reaction A ⇌ 2 B, K = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵. If [A]₀ = 1.00 M and [B]₀ = 0, find [A] and [B] at equilibrium.

- **Step 1.** Set up ICE table:
  ```
                A           2B
  Initial      1.00         0
  Change       -x          +2x
  Equilibrium  1.00 - x     2x
  ```
- **Step 2.** K expression: K = [B]² / [A] = (2x)² / (1.00 − x) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵.
- **Step 3.** Since K is very small, assume x << 1.00 → 1.00 − x ≈ 1.00:
  - 4x² / 1.00 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵
  - x² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵
  - x = 3.16 × 10⁻³ M
- **Step 4.** Check approximation: x = 0.00316 vs 1.00 → 0.32% — well below 5%, approximation valid.
- **Step 5.** Equilibrium concentrations: [A] = 1.00 − 0.00316 ≈ 0.997 M; [B] = 2(0.00316) = 6.32 × 10⁻³ M.

### Example 3 [3][4] — Le Chatelier prediction

The reaction 2 SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2 SO₃(g) is exothermic (ΔH = −198 kJ). Predict the direction of shift for each disturbance: (a) add SO₂; (b) decrease the volume of the container; (c) increase the temperature; (d) add a catalyst; (e) add helium gas at constant volume.

- **(a) Add SO₂ (a reactant):** Q decreases (less product / more reactant ratio). Q < K → shift toward PRODUCTS.
- **(b) Decrease volume (gas system):** all partial pressures increase; system shifts toward the side with FEWER moles of gas. Reactants: 2 SO₂ + O₂ = 3 moles. Products: 2 SO₃ = 2 moles. Shift toward PRODUCTS.
- **(c) Increase temperature (exothermic reaction):** treat heat as a "product" — adding heat shifts equilibrium AWAY from heat (toward reactants). Shift toward REACTANTS. (And K decreases.)
- **(d) Add catalyst:** speeds up both directions equally. NO SHIFT in equilibrium position.
- **(e) Add He at constant V:** He doesn't react. Partial pressures of SO₂, O₂, SO₃ unchanged. NO SHIFT.
- **AP-style phrasing example for (c):** "Increasing the temperature of an exothermic reaction shifts the equilibrium toward reactants because the system absorbs the added heat by reversing the exothermic forward reaction. Both the equilibrium position shifts left AND the value of K decreases."

### Example 4 [3][4] — Ksp and molar solubility

The Ksp of CaF₂ is 3.9 × 10⁻¹¹. (a) Calculate the molar solubility of CaF₂ in pure water. (b) Calculate the molar solubility of CaF₂ in 0.10 M NaF.

- **(a) In pure water:**
  - CaF₂(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 F⁻(aq)
  - If s mol/L dissolves, then [Ca²⁺] = s and [F⁻] = 2s.
  - Ksp = [Ca²⁺][F⁻]² = s × (2s)² = 4s³ = 3.9 × 10⁻¹¹
  - s³ = 9.75 × 10⁻¹² → s = 2.13 × 10⁻⁴ M.
- **(b) In 0.10 M NaF:**
  - [F⁻] is dominated by NaF: [F⁻] ≈ 0.10 M (the small contribution from CaF₂ dissolving is negligible).
  - Ksp = [Ca²⁺](0.10)² = 3.9 × 10⁻¹¹
  - [Ca²⁺] = 3.9 × 10⁻⁹ M = molar solubility of CaF₂ in this solution.
- **Comparison:** solubility dropped from 2.13 × 10⁻⁴ M to 3.9 × 10⁻⁹ M — about 5 orders of magnitude lower. Common-ion effect at work.
- **[4] phrase:** "By Le Chatelier, adding fluoride ion (a product of dissolution) shifts the equilibrium back toward solid CaF₂, reducing solubility. Quantitatively, [F⁻] is set by the dominant NaF source, so [Ca²⁺] = Ksp / [F⁻]² is dramatically smaller than the pure-water value."

### Example 5 [4][5] — Manipulating K for combined reactions

Given:
- (1) 2 NO(g) + Br₂(g) ⇌ 2 NOBr(g), K₁ = 0.014
- (2) 2 NO(g) ⇌ N₂(g) + O₂(g), K₂ = 2.4 × 10³⁰

Find K for: N₂(g) + O₂(g) + Br₂(g) ⇌ 2 NOBr(g).

- **Step 1.** Strategy: combine equations to get the target.
- **Step 2.** Reverse equation (2): N₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2 NO(g), K₂_reverse = 1/K₂ = 4.17 × 10⁻³¹.
- **Step 3.** Add reversed (2) and (1):
  - N₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2 NO   (K = 4.17 × 10⁻³¹)
  - 2 NO + Br₂ ⇌ 2 NOBr   (K = 0.014)
  - Sum: N₂ + O₂ + Br₂ ⇌ 2 NOBr ✓
- **Step 4.** When adding reactions, multiply K's: K_target = (4.17 × 10⁻³¹)(0.014) = 5.83 × 10⁻³³.
- **Sanity check:** K is extraordinarily small — reasonable, because forming NOBr from N₂ requires breaking the very strong N≡N bond, which dominates everything else.
- **[5] phrase:** "When two equilibrium reactions are added to give a third, the equilibrium constant of the combined reaction is the PRODUCT of the constants of the original reactions. Reversing a reaction inverts its K. Multiplying a reaction by a coefficient n raises its K to the n-th power."

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Including pure solids or liquids in the K expression.** They have constant activity = 1. Only gases and aqueous species appear in K.
2. **Wrong direction prediction from Q vs K.** Q < K → forward (toward products). Q > K → reverse (toward reactants). Easy to flip.
3. **Saying "K changes when concentration changes."** K depends only on TEMPERATURE. Adding reactant changes Q, not K.
4. **Adding inert gas thinking it shifts equilibrium.** At constant volume, inert gas doesn't change reacting partial pressures → no shift. At constant pressure (different scenario), it does.
5. **Le Chatelier for temperature backward.** For exothermic, INCREASING T shifts toward REACTANTS (heat is a product, system relieves added heat by going backward). Endothermic: opposite.
6. **Not accounting for stoichiometry in ICE tables.** If the equation is A ⇌ 2 B, then for every −x of A, +2x of B forms.
7. **Approximating x small when K isn't small.** The "x is small" approximation fails when K is large or initial concentration is small. Always check that x < 5% of initial.
8. **Missing the 5% check.** AP rubrics may require the explicit verification statement "x is less than 5% of initial concentration, so the approximation is valid."
9. **Treating Ksp as a concentration.** Ksp is a CONSTANT (no units, or sometimes M^(total exponents)). Don't compare it directly to a concentration without computing molar solubility first.
10. **Using molar solubility as Ksp.** s and Ksp are related but not equal. For MX: Ksp = s². For M₂X: Ksp = 4s³.
11. **Ignoring the common-ion source dominance.** When NaCl is added to AgCl, [Cl⁻] from NaCl swamps the trace from AgCl dissolution.
12. **Catalyst confusion in Le Chatelier.** Catalysts NEVER shift equilibrium. They only speed up the approach.
13. **Forgetting to compute Q before assuming the reaction goes forward.** When given non-equilibrium starting concentrations, ALWAYS calculate Q first; the direction depends on Q vs K.
14. **Multiplying K by a coefficient instead of raising it to the power.** When you multiply a reaction by n, K_new = K^n, NOT n × K.
15. **Q expression with current values not normalized.** Q has the same form as K but uses current concentrations. Don't accidentally use equilibrium values.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For ICE table problems:**
- Set up the table explicitly with all three rows (Initial, Change, Equilibrium).
- Use stoichiometric coefficients in the change row (−ax for reactant, +bx for product where stoichiometry is a:b).
- Apply assumption when justified, but state it: "assume x is small, so [A]₀ − x ≈ [A]₀."
- Verify the approximation: state the percent and confirm < 5%.
- Solve for x and back-substitute for equilibrium concentrations.

**For Le Chatelier predictions:**
- State which direction the equilibrium shifts.
- Justify with reference to Q vs K (after the disturbance) OR with the "system counteracts the disturbance" framing.
- For temperature changes, also state whether K itself changes.

**For Q vs K comparisons:**
- Calculate Q explicitly with current concentrations.
- Compare to K numerically.
- State the direction of shift with reasoning.

**For Ksp problems:**
- Write the dissolution equilibrium and Ksp expression with correct stoichiometric exponents.
- Define molar solubility s and relate ion concentrations to s.
- For common-ion problems, identify the dominant source of the common ion.

**For combined-reaction K problems:**
- Manipulate equations (reverse, multiply) to get the target.
- Apply the K rules: reverse → 1/K; multiply by n → K^n; add → multiply K's.
- Show each step of the manipulation.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Q ([value]) is [less than / greater than / equal to] K ([value]). Therefore the reaction proceeds in the [forward / reverse / no] direction to reach equilibrium."
2. "By Le Chatelier's principle, the system shifts to partially counteract the disturbance. Adding [species] increases its concentration, making Q less than K, so the reaction shifts toward [products / reactants]."
3. "K depends only on temperature. Adding more reactant changes the system's Q but not K; the system re-equilibrates at a new composition where Q again equals K."
4. "For an exothermic reaction, increasing temperature shifts the equilibrium toward reactants. The forward reaction releases heat, so adding heat is equivalent to adding a product, shifting the equilibrium backward. K decreases as temperature increases."
5. "Setting up an ICE table: initial concentrations are [values]; the change row reflects stoichiometric ratios with x defined as the amount reacted; equilibrium values are I + C. Substituting into K = [...] yields [equation]. Solving for x: [steps]."
6. "Because Ksp is small ([value]), the molar solubility is approximately s = [calculation]. The dissolution equilibrium is suppressed by the common-ion effect because [F⁻] from NaF dominates the contribution from CaF₂ dissolving."
7. "When two equilibrium reactions are added, K of the combined reaction is the product of the individual K's. Reversing a reaction inverts its K; multiplying by n raises K to the n-th power."
