# AP US History — Period 2: 1607–1754 (Colonial America) — Wayfinder Teaching Brain

## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** ~10% of the AP US History exam
- **Sub-topics covered:**
  - 2.1 Contextualizing Period 2
  - 2.2 European Colonization of North America
  - 2.3 The Regions of British Colonies
  - 2.4 Transatlantic Trade
  - 2.5 Interactions Between American Indians and Europeans
  - 2.6 Slavery in the British Colonies
  - 2.7 Colonial Society and Culture
- **Where this period appears on the exam:** Regional differences among the British colonies (Chesapeake vs New England vs Middle vs Lower South) is a perennial LEQ topic. Slavery's evolution from indentured-servant-dominant to chattel-slavery-dominant in the late 17th century shows up regularly in DBQs. The First Great Awakening + Enlightenment as colonial intellectual movements is a common SAQ. Bacon's Rebellion is the single most-cited specific event in DBQ rubrics for this period — know it cold. Colonial governance (House of Burgesses, Mayflower Compact, salutary neglect) frames Period 3 questions about the road to revolution.

## Big Ideas

1. **The British colonies were not one society but four distinct regional cultures.** Chesapeake (tobacco, indentured servants then slaves, scattered farms, Anglican). New England (mixed agriculture/fishing, Puritan towns, family migration). Middle (wheat, religious + ethnic diversity, urban Philadelphia and New York). Lower South (rice/indigo, large enslaved majority, planter elite). Don't treat the colonies as a unified bloc — they had different economies, religions, demographics, and political cultures.
2. **Slavery in British North America transformed from one labor system among many into the racialized foundation of colonial economy.** In 1640, indentured servants outnumbered enslaved Africans. By 1700, after Bacon's Rebellion (1676) and rising slave codes, enslaved Africans had become the dominant Southern labor force, locked into hereditary, racial, lifelong bondage.
3. **Colonial governance combined English political traditions with American distinctness.** Representative assemblies (Virginia House of Burgesses 1619, town meetings in New England), royal governors, written charters, and the practical experience of self-government during salutary neglect produced political habits that later sustained revolution.
4. **Religion was foundational AND fractious.** Puritan New England started as a "holy commonwealth" but fractured into dissent (Roger Williams, Anne Hutchinson, Quakers). The Great Awakening (1730s–1740s) cut across colonies, undermined deference to established clergy, and created shared experiences across previously isolated regions.
5. **Native-colonist relations deteriorated as colonial settlements expanded inland.** The early Powhatan-Jamestown alliance gave way to violent conflicts (1622 Powhatan attack, 1676 King Philip's War in New England, 1675 Bacon's Rebellion in Virginia). The pattern: trade or alliance early, displacement and violence as land hunger grew.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Jamestown (1607)** — first permanent English settlement in North America. Joint-stock venture of the Virginia Company. Initial years were catastrophic ("Starving Time" 1609–1610, 80% died). Saved by tobacco cultivation (John Rolfe, 1612). Established the **House of Burgesses (1619)** — first representative assembly in English America. Same year, **first enslaved Africans arrived** at Jamestown.
- **Plymouth (1620)** — Pilgrims (Separatists from the Church of England) landed at Plymouth on the *Mayflower*. Wrote the **Mayflower Compact**, a self-government agreement. Helped by Squanto and the Wampanoag (first Thanksgiving 1621). Smaller than Massachusetts Bay Colony, eventually absorbed into it (1691).
- **Massachusetts Bay (1630)** — Puritan colony led by **John Winthrop** ("city upon a hill" sermon). Governed as a "Bible Commonwealth" — only church members could vote. Founded Harvard (1636) to train ministers.
- **Religious dissenters from Massachusetts Bay:**
  - **Roger Williams** — banished 1635 for advocating church-state separation and Native land rights. Founded **Rhode Island** (1636), the first colony with religious liberty.
  - **Anne Hutchinson** — banished 1637 for antinomianism (claiming direct divine revelation). Joined Williams in Rhode Island, later killed by Natives in New York.
  - **Thomas Hooker** — left Massachusetts Bay 1636, founded **Connecticut**, drafted the **Fundamental Orders of Connecticut** (1639), arguably the first written constitution in America.
- **Middle Colonies:**
  - **New York** (originally Dutch New Netherland, founded 1624; conquered by English 1664). Diverse population (Dutch, English, Africans, Jews) from the start.
  - **Pennsylvania** (1681) — founded by **William Penn**, a Quaker. Established religious tolerance, peaceful relations with Lenape (Delaware) Natives, ethnically diverse from start (Quakers, German "Pennsylvania Dutch," Scots-Irish).
  - **New Jersey** and **Delaware** were carved off from Pennsylvania and New York.
- **Southern Colonies:**
  - **Maryland** (1634) — founded as a refuge for English Catholics by Lord Baltimore. **Maryland Toleration Act (1649)** granted religious freedom to all Trinitarian Christians.
  - **Carolinas** (1663) — proprietary colony. Split into North Carolina (small farmers, similar to Virginia) and South Carolina (rice and indigo plantations, large enslaved majority by 1700).
  - **Georgia** (1733) — founded by **James Oglethorpe** as a buffer against Spanish Florida and a refuge for English debtors. Initially banned slavery; reversed in 1751.
- **Bacon's Rebellion (1676)** — Nathaniel Bacon led frontier farmers (including freed indentured servants) against Governor William Berkeley over Native policy and elite political control. Burned Jamestown. Bacon died of disease; rebellion collapsed. **Long-term consequence:** Virginia elite, alarmed by interracial cooperation between poor whites and Africans, pivoted to RACIAL chattel slavery (vs indentured servitude) to divide the laboring classes.
- **Mercantilism and the Navigation Acts (1651–1673)** — economic policy that colonies existed to enrich the mother country. Required all colonial trade to use English ships, and enumerated certain goods (tobacco, sugar) had to be shipped to England. Led to smuggling and resentment but were largely unenforced under salutary neglect.
- **Salutary neglect** — informal British policy (especially under Robert Walpole, ~1721–1742) of NOT strictly enforcing colonial trade laws, in exchange for colonial loyalty and economic productivity. Allowed colonial self-government to develop. Ended after 1763 (Period 3) — the cause of revolutionary tensions.
- **Triangular trade** — economic system linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas. Pattern: European manufactured goods to Africa → enslaved Africans to the Americas (Middle Passage) → American raw materials (sugar, rum, tobacco) back to Europe. Variations: New England rum to Africa, Africans to Caribbean, Caribbean molasses to New England.
- **First Great Awakening (1730s–1740s)** — religious revival that swept the colonies. Key preachers:
  - **Jonathan Edwards** — Massachusetts Congregationalist, famous for "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741).
  - **George Whitefield** — Anglican itinerant preacher who toured the colonies, drawing massive crowds.
  - **Effects:** undermined established (paid, university-trained) clergy in favor of "New Light" emotional revivalism; created new denominations (Methodists, Baptists); fostered a SHARED colonial experience across regional boundaries; increased literacy and individual religious agency.
- **Enlightenment ideas in the colonies** — John Locke's social contract theory, natural rights (life, liberty, property), and rational government influenced colonial elites (especially Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson). The Enlightenment's secular rationalism contrasted with the Awakening's religious emotionalism, but both promoted individual judgment.

### Adds for [4]

- **Indentured servitude** — the dominant labor system in 17th-century Chesapeake. Poor English (and later Scots-Irish, Germans) signed contracts trading 4–7 years of labor for transatlantic passage and "freedom dues" (land, tools, sometimes a small payment) at the end. Up to 75% of immigrants to the Chesapeake before 1675 were indentured servants. Mortality was extraordinary — roughly half died before completing their contracts.
- **Why slavery replaced indentured servitude** (the central [4] question for Period 2):
  - Indentured servant supply declined as English economy improved and Bacon's Rebellion showed the political danger of armed, freed servants.
  - Africans could not "blend in" or escape easily, were enslaved for life (not just 7 years), and produced enslaved children — much more profitable in the long term.
  - Slave codes (Virginia 1660s–1705) progressively defined slavery as RACIAL, HEREDITARY, LIFELONG, and INHERITED THROUGH THE MOTHER (so children of enslaved women were enslaved regardless of paternity — protecting white owners who fathered children with enslaved women).
  - Royal African Company (chartered 1672) initially monopolized slave trade; in 1698, the trade was opened to all English merchants, accelerating slave imports.
- **Stono Rebellion (1739)** — largest slave uprising in colonial America. ~80 enslaved Africans in South Carolina rose, killed ~25 whites, attempted to flee to Spanish Florida (where freedom was promised). Suppressed brutally. Led to the **Negro Act of 1740** restricting movement, education, and assembly of enslaved people in South Carolina.
- **Halfway Covenant (1662)** — Puritan compromise allowing children of baptized but unconverted parents to be baptized and partial church members. Reflected declining religious fervor and demographic reality in second-generation Puritan New England. Sign of Puritan religious decline that the Great Awakening later attempted to reverse.
- **Salem Witch Trials (1692)** — Massachusetts crisis where 19 people were executed (mostly women, also one man pressed to death) on accusations of witchcraft. Causes debated: ergot poisoning, social tensions between farming and commercial Salem Village, anxieties from frontier warfare. Trials ended when accusations targeted prominent figures including the governor's wife. Symbol of Puritan religious authority's overreach and decline.
- **King Philip's War (1675–1676)** — Wampanoag chief Metacom (called King Philip by colonists) led an alliance against New England settlers after decades of land encroachment. Per capita, the deadliest war in American history (12% of New England settlers killed; 40% of Native population killed or displaced). Effectively ended major Native resistance in southern New England.
- **Pueblo Revolt (1680)** — already covered in Period 1 background; key date for Period 2 because it falls in this period. Popé led Pueblo peoples in driving Spanish out of New Mexico for 12 years. Most successful Native resistance in colonial North America.
- **Yamasee War (1715–1717)** — major Native uprising in South Carolina; Yamasee and Creek alliance attacked the colony in response to abuses by traders. Nearly destroyed the Carolina colony before being defeated. Reshaped Southeast Native politics and led to greater colonial dominance.
- **Albany Plan of Union (1754)** — Benjamin Franklin's proposal at the Albany Congress for a colonial union to coordinate defense against the French and Native peoples. Rejected by both colonial assemblies (too much central power) and the Crown (too much colonial autonomy). Notable as an early proposal for inter-colonial cooperation, foreshadowing later union efforts.
- **John Peter Zenger trial (1735)** — New York newspaper publisher acquitted of seditious libel after publishing criticism of the colonial governor. Established a colonial precedent for press freedom — that truth was a defense against libel charges. Did not become legal precedent in England, but became foundational to American press freedom culture.
- **Headright system** — Virginia's land grant system: colonists who paid for their own or another's passage received 50 acres per person. Encouraged the importation of indentured servants and concentrated land among elite who could afford to import many.
- **Virginia House of Burgesses (1619)** — first elected representative assembly in English North America. Initially just for free white men with property. Established the precedent of colonial self-government that would echo in the Continental Congresses.

### Adds for [5]

- **The "deference society" framing of colonial politics.** Despite representative assemblies, colonial society was deeply hierarchical. Political offices were held by the wealthy (merchants in New England, planters in the South), and ordinary voters generally deferred to elite leadership. The Revolution and especially Period 4's Jacksonian democracy disrupted this pattern. Sophistication move on Period 2 essays: noting that "representative government" did not mean democratic government in any modern sense.
- **Why Bacon's Rebellion is the most analytically powerful event of Period 2.** It illuminates THREE crucial dynamics simultaneously:
  - The class divide between coastal elite and frontier farmers.
  - The shifting Native policy (Bacon wanted aggressive Native displacement; Berkeley preferred peaceful trade).
  - The racial hardening of slavery (interracial alliance among Bacon's followers terrified the Virginia elite into pivoting to racial chattel slavery).
  - A strong essay on "what changed in the late 17th-century Chesapeake" should anchor on Bacon's Rebellion.
- **The "demographic shock" framing for slavery's expansion.** In the early 17th century, white indentured servants could become free landholders after their contracts. By 1700, they couldn't — frontier land was harder to obtain, the headright system had concentrated land among elites, and Indian wars had driven settlement back. The same forces that ended the freed-servant pathway also drove the elite preference for permanent enslaved labor. Slavery's racialization was a response to economic and demographic conditions, not a pre-existing ideology imposing itself on a blank slate.
- **Comparing the four British colonial regions in two-axis form** (a [5] sophistication move):
  - Economy axis: subsistence farming + fishing (NE) | mixed wheat agriculture + commerce (Middle) | tobacco plantations (Chesapeake) | rice/indigo plantations (Lower South).
  - Labor axis: family labor + apprentices (NE) | mixed family + servants + some slaves (Middle) | indentured servants → enslaved (Chesapeake) | enslaved African majority (Lower South).
  - Religion axis: Puritan Congregational (NE) | religious pluralism (Middle, especially PA) | Anglican (Chesapeake) | Anglican planter elite + diverse poor (Lower South).
  - Demography axis: family migration → balanced sex ratio + high birth rate (NE) | mixed (Middle) | male-dominated single migrants → low birth rate (Chesapeake early; balanced later).
  - **Strong LEQ argument:** "These four regions were so distinct that calling them collectively 'colonial America' obscures the real story — they were closer to four separate societies sharing only nominal allegiance to a distant Crown."
- **The Atlantic World framework continued.** Period 2 colonial trade was deeply embedded in Atlantic commerce: New England rum + fish to Africa and the Caribbean, Chesapeake tobacco directly to England, Lower South rice to Caribbean and back to England. Africans, sugar, rum, ships, Bibles, and ideas (Enlightenment, evangelical revivalism, abolitionist arguments) all circulated. Strong essays frame colonial America as an Atlantic node, not an isolated outpost.
- **The First Great Awakening's political legacy.** Often understated. By undermining established (state-sponsored) church authority and emphasizing individual conscience, the Awakening:
  - Created vocabulary for resisting authority ("Old Light" vs "New Light").
  - Trained colonists to listen to itinerant preachers crossing colonial borders, building inter-colonial communication networks.
  - Established the practice of large public gatherings around contested ideas — a template for Revolutionary mobilization.
  - Many historians argue the Awakening was a necessary precondition for the political mobilization of the Revolution.

## Worked Examples (Thesis & DBQ-Style Argumentation)

### Example 1 [3] — Thesis on regional differences (LEQ)

**Prompt:** Compare and contrast the development of New England and Chesapeake regions of British North America in the 17th century.

- **Weak thesis:** "New England and the Chesapeake were different. New England was more religious, and the Chesapeake had more slaves."
- **Strong thesis [4–5]:** "While both New England and the Chesapeake were established as English colonial ventures in the early 17th century, they developed in fundamentally different directions: New England, settled by Puritan families seeking religious community, established town-based subsistence agriculture with balanced demographics and a Bible Commonwealth political culture, while the Chesapeake, settled by single male profit-seekers as a tobacco economy, produced a scattered plantation society with skewed demographics, indentured servitude that hardened into racial chattel slavery, and an Anglican planter-elite political order. These differences in motivation, economy, demographics, and labor systems made the two regions effectively separate societies under nominal common allegiance."
- **Why it works:** names the specific motivations, economies, demographic patterns, labor systems, and political cultures of each region; uses parallel structure; sets up an analytical claim about how foundational differences persist.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Bacon's Rebellion SAQ

**Prompt:** Identify and explain ONE long-term consequence of Bacon's Rebellion (1676) for the development of British North America.

- **Acceptable answer [3]:** "Bacon's Rebellion led the Virginia elite to rely more heavily on enslaved African labor instead of white indentured servants. They feared that armed freed servants would rebel again."
- **Strong answer [4]:** "Bacon's Rebellion accelerated the transition from indentured servitude to racial chattel slavery in Virginia. The Virginia elite was alarmed that Bacon's followers had included both poor whites and enslaved or freed Africans cooperating across racial lines. To prevent future interracial alliances among the laboring classes, the colony passed a series of slave codes between 1680 and 1705 that progressively defined slavery as racial, hereditary, and lifelong, while granting modest legal privileges to even poor whites — driving a permanent racial wedge through the laboring class. This racialized labor system became the economic and social foundation of the colonial South for the next two centuries."
- **Why it earns the point:** specific mechanism (alarm at interracial cooperation), specific legal response (slave codes 1680–1705), specific long-term consequence (racial wedge, foundation of Southern society), connects to later periods.

### Example 3 [4] — HIPP-style sourcing of Winthrop's "City upon a Hill"

**Document:** John Winthrop, "A Model of Christian Charity" (1630), delivered aboard the *Arbella* en route to Massachusetts Bay.

- **Historical situation:** Winthrop spoke as the leader of a major Puritan migration fleeing increasing repression of nonconformist Protestants in England under Charles I and Archbishop Laud. The Puritans were not just emigrating but consciously trying to build a model Christian society as a witness to a corrupt England.
- **Intended audience:** Immediately, the Puritans on the *Arbella* and in the larger fleet (about 700 settlers). More broadly, the entire English nation that the Puritans hoped would observe and emulate them.
- **Point of view:** Winthrop was an English lawyer and Puritan elder, the first governor of the Massachusetts Bay Colony. His framing assumed Puritan religious authority should structure every aspect of colonial life, including politics and economics.
- **Purpose:** Persuasive — to unify the migrants under a shared religious mission and to set expectations of mutual obligation, hierarchy, and discipline. The "city upon a hill" image was a call to accountability before God and the watching world.
- **Sophisticated extension [5]:** the "city upon a hill" framing has been recycled throughout American history (Reagan and others used it to claim American exceptionalism), but Winthrop's original meaning was almost the opposite of triumphalist — it was a warning that failure would mean public disgrace, not a celebration of inevitable greatness.

### Example 4 [4][5] — Slavery's evolution argument with continuity AND change

**Prompt:** To what extent did the nature of slavery in the British North American colonies change between 1607 and 1750?

- **Continuity claim:** Slavery existed throughout the period — the first enslaved Africans arrived at Jamestown in 1619, and enslaved labor was present in the colonies continuously thereafter.
- **Change claim:** The CHARACTER of slavery transformed dramatically. In the early period (1620s–1660s), some Africans were treated similarly to indentured servants — they could buy or earn their freedom, accumulate property, and even own enslaved people themselves (Anthony Johnson is the famous example). By 1700, slave codes had hardened slavery into a racial, hereditary, lifelong system from which Africans could not legally escape.
- **Causal mechanism:** Bacon's Rebellion (1676), Royal African Company expansion, falling indentured servant supply, and the racialization of slave codes (Virginia 1660s–1705) collectively transformed slavery from "one labor system among many" to "the racial foundation of the Southern colonial economy."
- **Sophistication [5]:** The transformation was uneven across regions. Lower South (rice cultivation in South Carolina) imported slaves in massive numbers and developed enslaved-majority populations by 1700. New England had few slaves but participated in the Atlantic slave trade through shipping. Middle Colonies had enslaved people but never to the demographic dominance of the South. So "American slavery" was not a single institution but a regional system with very different intensities.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Great Awakening as inter-colonial unifier (continuity-change)

**Prompt:** Evaluate the extent to which the First Great Awakening unified the British North American colonies in the 18th century.

- **Argument for unification:** The Awakening was the first event experienced across all 13 colonies at roughly the same time. Whitefield drew tens of thousands across the colonies. Inter-colonial communication networks formed around shared religious experience. New denominations (Baptists, Methodists) crossed colonial borders.
- **Counter-argument [5]:** The Awakening also DIVIDED. "Old Light" vs "New Light" splits fractured Congregational churches in New England. Established clergy resented itinerant preachers. The Awakening's emphasis on individual religious experience eroded communal religious authority — sometimes producing unity around emotional experience, but also producing schisms over which experiences counted as authentic.
- **Synthesis:** The Awakening unified colonies in the SHARED EXPERIENCE of religious revival but divided them THEOLOGICALLY between traditionalists and revivalists. This dual character — shared experiences across regions producing common political vocabulary and communication networks, even when participants disagreed — is exactly what made the Awakening a necessary precondition for the Revolutionary mobilization a generation later.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Treating the 13 colonies as one society.** They were FOUR distinct regional cultures with different economies, religions, demographics, and political habits. Always specify which region.
2. **Saying slavery existed "from the beginning" without nuance.** It existed from 1619 but in evolving forms — early Africans were sometimes treated similarly to indentured servants; racial chattel slavery emerged through specific legal changes between 1660 and 1705.
3. **Confusing Pilgrims (1620 Plymouth, Separatists) with Puritans (1630 Massachusetts Bay, non-Separatists who wanted to reform from within).** They had different theological positions, even if often grouped together.
4. **Misattributing "city upon a hill."** John Winthrop, 1630, sermon on the *Arbella*, NOT the Mayflower Compact (1620, Plymouth, Separatists), NOT the Declaration of Independence.
5. **Misdating Jamestown (1607) as 1620.** 1607 = Jamestown. 1620 = Plymouth.
6. **Misattributing the Mayflower Compact.** It's Plymouth/Pilgrims (1620), not Massachusetts Bay/Puritans (1630).
7. **Confusing Roger Williams (Rhode Island) with William Penn (Pennsylvania).** Both founded religiously tolerant colonies but for different reasons (Williams for separation of church and state, Penn for Quaker tolerance).
8. **Overstating Native depopulation as exclusively biological.** Yes, disease was the dominant cause, but warfare (King Philip's War, Yamasee War, Pequot War 1637), enslavement, and forced displacement also contributed.
9. **Treating Bacon's Rebellion as just a "Native policy dispute."** It was that, but more importantly it was about CLASS (frontier farmers vs coastal elite) and led to the racialization of slavery.
10. **Missing the difference between mercantilism (theory) and Navigation Acts (specific laws).** Mercantilism = the economic theory that colonies should enrich the mother country. Navigation Acts = the specific 1651–1673 laws implementing it.
11. **Salutary neglect dates confused.** Roughly 1721–1763, peaking under Walpole. Ended with the Sugar Act (1764) onset of strict enforcement, which triggered Period 3 conflicts.
12. **Crediting the wrong founder for each colony.** Massachusetts Bay = Winthrop. Plymouth = Bradford (William, the chronicler). Rhode Island = Roger Williams. Connecticut = Thomas Hooker. Pennsylvania = William Penn. Maryland = Lord Baltimore (Calvert family). Georgia = Oglethorpe. Virginia = Virginia Company (no single individual).
13. **Treating the Great Awakening as just religious.** It had political consequences — undermining deference to clergy, building inter-colonial communication networks, training colonists in mass-mobilization tactics later useful for revolution.
14. **Dating the Salem Witch Trials wrong.** 1692, not 1692–1700 or any later date. Specifically Salem Village (now Danvers), Massachusetts.
15. **Forgetting Stono Rebellion (1739).** Largest slave uprising in colonial America, in South Carolina. Often missed in essays about colonial slavery resistance.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For regional comparison essays:**
- Name SPECIFIC regions (don't just say "the colonies").
- Use parallel structure across multiple axes (economy, demography, religion, labor).
- Acknowledge a SHARED feature before pivoting to differences.

**For slavery-evolution essays:**
- Distinguish early (1619–1660s) from later (1680–1705) phases.
- Cite specific slave codes (Virginia 1662 hereditary slavery; 1705 comprehensive slave code).
- Connect Bacon's Rebellion (1676) to the racial pivot.
- Note regional intensity differences (Lower South vs New England).

**For Great Awakening / Enlightenment questions:**
- Name specific figures (Edwards, Whitefield for Awakening; Locke, Franklin for Enlightenment).
- Note both cultural AND political consequences.
- Acknowledge the tension between religious enthusiasm (Awakening) and rational inquiry (Enlightenment) — and how both contributed to revolutionary thought.

**For colonial governance questions:**
- Name specific institutions (House of Burgesses 1619, Mayflower Compact 1620, Fundamental Orders of Connecticut 1639).
- Connect representative assemblies to later revolutionary self-government claims.
- Note that "representative" did not mean "democratic" — voting was restricted to property-owning white men.

**For HIPP sourcing of period documents:**
- Identify the specific religious or political movement the author belonged to.
- Note the audience (often other colonists, sometimes the Crown or English Parliament).
- Connect document features to the author's purpose.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The British colonies developed not as one society but as four distinct regions: New England's family-migrant Puritan towns, the Middle Colonies' ethnically and religiously diverse commercial society, the tobacco-based Chesapeake with its evolving labor system, and the rice/indigo Lower South with its enslaved African majority."
2. "Bacon's Rebellion (1676) was a turning point in colonial labor systems. Frontier farmer Nathaniel Bacon led an uprising of poor whites and Africans against Governor Berkeley's elite government. The rebellion's interracial cooperation alarmed Virginia's elite, who responded by passing slave codes between 1680 and 1705 that hardened slavery into a racial, hereditary, lifelong institution and granted modest legal privileges to poor whites — fragmenting the laboring class along racial lines."
3. "John Winthrop's 'city upon a hill' (1630) was a call to Puritan accountability before God and the world, not the triumphalist celebration of American greatness it later became. Winthrop warned that failure would mean disgrace, signaling the Puritan understanding of their colonial venture as a covenant requiring constant vigilance."
4. "The First Great Awakening (1730s–1740s) unified the colonies in shared religious experience while simultaneously dividing them theologically. Whitefield's tours and the Old Light/New Light split created inter-colonial networks of communication and shared political vocabulary that would prove essential for revolutionary mobilization a generation later."
5. "Salutary neglect — the British policy of loose enforcement of trade laws between roughly 1721 and 1763 — allowed colonial assemblies to develop habits of self-government and economic autonomy. When Parliament ended this policy after 1763, colonists experienced strict enforcement not as the resumption of normal governance but as a violation of established practice."
6. "The transition from indentured servitude to racial chattel slavery transformed the colonial labor system between 1660 and 1700. Slave codes — culminating in Virginia's 1705 comprehensive code — defined slavery as racial, hereditary, lifelong, and inherited through the mother, creating the foundational institution of the colonial Southern economy."
7. "By 1750, Pennsylvania exemplified the religious and ethnic pluralism of the Middle Colonies. William Penn's 'holy experiment' attracted Quakers, German Pietists ('Pennsylvania Dutch'), Scots-Irish frontier settlers, and others, producing a society without a single dominant religious or ethnic identity — a sharp contrast to Puritan New England or Anglican Virginia."

## If You Do Nothing Else for This Period

*Master the four regions framework (New England / Middle / Chesapeake / Lower South) and Bacon's Rebellion as the hinge that explains the racial transformation of colonial slavery. These two anchor concepts unlock most Period 2 LEQs and DBQs, and they scaffold understanding of every subsequent period's discussion of race, labor, region, and politics.*
