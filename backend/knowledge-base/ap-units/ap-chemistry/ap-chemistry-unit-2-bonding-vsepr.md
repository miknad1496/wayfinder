## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 7–9% of the AP Chemistry exam
- **Sub-topics covered:**
  - 2.1 Types of Chemical Bonds (ionic, covalent, metallic)
  - 2.2 Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy (bond length vs energy curves)
  - 2.3 Structure of Ionic Solids (lattice, lattice energy)
  - 2.4 Structure of Metals and Alloys (sea of electrons, substitutional vs interstitial)
  - 2.5 Lewis Diagrams
  - 2.6 Resonance and Formal Charge
  - 2.7 VSEPR and Bond Hybridization
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Lewis structures and VSEPR geometry are the most heavily tested topic on the entire AP Chem exam — they appear in nearly every FRQ either as a sub-question or as scaffolding for a longer particulate-level question. Lattice-energy ranking ("which has the highest melting point?") is a perennial MCQ. Resonance and formal-charge questions usually appear as MCQs but occasionally as FRQ sub-parts. Hybridization (sp/sp²/sp³) appears in roughly half of recent FRQs as a one-point freebie.

## Big Ideas

1. **Bond type lives on a spectrum, not in three buckets.** Ionic, polar covalent, and nonpolar covalent are three regions on the same continuum of electronegativity difference. The bond type tells you what kind of structure forms (lattice, molecule, network) and what properties to expect.
2. **Coulomb's law explains lattice energy AND bond strength.** The same F ∝ q₁q₂/r² that drove every periodic trend in Unit 1 explains why MgO has a higher melting point than NaCl, why C–O is shorter than C–N, and why a triple bond is harder to break than a single bond.
3. **Lewis structures are about counting first, geometry second.** Count valence electrons, distribute to satisfy octets, check formal charge, draw resonance if applicable. The diagram is a hypothesis about where electrons live; VSEPR is the geometry that hypothesis predicts.
4. **VSEPR is electron-pair repulsion, not atom repulsion.** Geometry is set by the total number of electron domains around the central atom — bonding pairs AND lone pairs both count as domains, but only bonding pairs determine the molecular shape.
5. **Bond polarity does not equal molecular polarity.** A molecule with polar bonds can be nonpolar overall if its geometry cancels the bond dipoles by symmetry. CO₂ is the classic example — two polar C=O bonds, but linear geometry makes the molecule nonpolar.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Bond types by EN difference** (rough rule):
  - ΔEN < 0.4 → nonpolar covalent
  - 0.4 ≤ ΔEN < 1.7 → polar covalent
  - ΔEN ≥ 1.7 → ionic (and a metal + nonmetal pairing)
  - Don't memorize the cutoffs as gospel — they're approximate. The pattern (metal + nonmetal = ionic; two nonmetals = covalent; metal + metal = metallic) is more reliable.
- **Ionic bonding:** complete electron transfer, forms a 3D lattice, high melting point, brittle, conducts when molten or dissolved (mobile ions).
- **Covalent bonding:** electron sharing, forms discrete molecules (or a network solid like diamond), lower melting points, doesn't conduct.
- **Metallic bonding:** "sea of electrons" — cations in a lattice surrounded by delocalized electrons. Conducts electricity, malleable, ductile, lustrous.
- **Lewis structure procedure:**
  1. Count total valence electrons (sum across all atoms; add electrons for negative ions, subtract for positive).
  2. Place the least electronegative atom in the center (H is never central).
  3. Connect peripheral atoms with single bonds.
  4. Distribute remaining electrons to satisfy octets on peripheral atoms first, then central.
  5. If the central atom is short, form double or triple bonds.
- **Octet rule and exceptions:**
  - H wants 2 electrons (duet).
  - B and Be can have 6 (electron-deficient).
  - Period 3+ elements (P, S, Cl, etc.) can have expanded octets up to 12.
- **VSEPR geometries to memorize cold** (steric number = bonded atoms + lone pairs on central):
  - 2 domains: linear (180°) — e.g., CO₂, BeCl₂
  - 3 domains, 0 LP: trigonal planar (120°) — e.g., BF₃, SO₃
  - 3 domains, 1 LP: bent (~118°) — e.g., SO₂, O₃
  - 4 domains, 0 LP: tetrahedral (109.5°) — e.g., CH₄, NH₄⁺
  - 4 domains, 1 LP: trigonal pyramidal (~107°) — e.g., NH₃
  - 4 domains, 2 LP: bent (~104.5°) — e.g., H₂O
  - 5 domains, 0 LP: trigonal bipyramidal (90°/120°) — e.g., PCl₅
  - 5 domains, 1 LP: see-saw — e.g., SF₄
  - 5 domains, 2 LP: T-shaped — e.g., ClF₃
  - 5 domains, 3 LP: linear — e.g., XeF₂
  - 6 domains, 0 LP: octahedral (90°) — e.g., SF₆
  - 6 domains, 1 LP: square pyramidal — e.g., BrF₅
  - 6 domains, 2 LP: square planar — e.g., XeF₄
- **Hybridization shortcut:** count electron domains.
  - 2 domains → sp
  - 3 domains → sp²
  - 4 domains → sp³
  - 5 domains → sp³d
  - 6 domains → sp³d²
- **Molecular polarity:** if bond dipoles cancel by symmetry → nonpolar. If they don't → polar.
- **Resonance:** when one Lewis structure can't capture the electron distribution, draw multiple structures with different placements of double bonds. The actual molecule is the AVERAGE (a hybrid).
- **Formal charge:** FC = (valence electrons in free atom) − (lone-pair electrons) − ½(bonding electrons). The best Lewis structure minimizes formal charges and places negative formal charge on the most electronegative atom.

### Adds for [4]

- **Lattice energy magnitude scales with charges and inversely with distance:** U ∝ |q₁q₂| / r. Higher charges → much larger lattice energy. Smaller ions → larger lattice energy. So MgO (2+/2−, smallish ions) > NaF (1+/1−, similar size) > KI (1+/1−, big ions).
- **The most-tested lattice-energy comparison:** MgO has lattice energy ~3795 kJ/mol; NaCl has ~787 kJ/mol. The ratio is roughly 5×, driven mainly by the (2)(2) vs (1)(1) charge product. This is why MgO melts at 2852 °C and NaCl at 801 °C.
- **Coulombic justification of bond strength:** triple bond > double bond > single bond, because more shared electron pairs = stronger attraction between atoms. Triple bonds are also SHORTER (smaller r) which compounds the strength.
- **Bond length trend:** for the same atom pair, length decreases with bond order. C–C ≈ 154 pm, C=C ≈ 134 pm, C≡C ≈ 120 pm.
- **Bond strength is RELEASED on formation, REQUIRED for breaking.** Breaking bonds is endothermic (positive); forming bonds is exothermic (negative). Reaction enthalpy ≈ Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed).
- **Potential energy vs internuclear distance curve:** atoms attract at moderate r, repel strongly at very small r. The minimum of the curve is the equilibrium bond length; the depth of the well is the bond dissociation energy.
- **Resonance averaging:** in benzene, the C–C bond length (140 pm) is between single (154 pm) and double (134 pm) — direct evidence of delocalized bonding. Same logic for ozone, carbonate, nitrate.
- **Formal charge tiebreaker:** when two valid Lewis structures exist, prefer the one where:
  1. Formal charges are smallest in magnitude.
  2. Negative FC is on the more electronegative atom.
  3. Like charges are not on adjacent atoms.
- **Lone pairs distort idealized bond angles.** Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs because they're held by only one nucleus. So in NH₃, the H–N–H angle is 107° (less than the ideal tetrahedral 109.5°) because the lone pair pushes the H atoms closer together. In H₂O, the angle drops to 104.5° because there are TWO lone pairs.
- **Polar vs nonpolar molecule worked rule:**
  - Symmetric distribution of identical peripheral atoms → bond dipoles cancel → nonpolar (CH₄, CO₂, BF₃, SF₆, XeF₄).
  - Asymmetric distribution OR lone pair on central → bond dipoles don't cancel → polar (H₂O, NH₃, CHCl₃, SO₂).
- **Network covalent solids** (diamond, graphite, SiO₂, SiC): held together by extended covalent bonding throughout the lattice. Extremely high melting points, hard, brittle, non-conducting (except graphite, which has delocalized electrons within sheets).
- **Alloys:** substitutional (similar-sized atoms swap places — bronze = Cu/Sn, brass = Cu/Zn) vs interstitial (smaller atoms fit into gaps — steel = Fe/C). Alloys are typically harder than pure metals because the size mismatch hinders sliding of metal layers.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why lone pairs distort angles, in mechanistic detail.** A bonding pair is held by two nuclei (shared electrostatic attraction). A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so its electron cloud spreads out further. The repulsion order is: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair. So molecules with lone pairs have compressed bonding-pair angles.
- **Why expanded octets only happen for Period 3+ elements.** Period 2 atoms (C, N, O, F) have only 2s and 2p orbitals available — maximum 8 electrons. Period 3+ atoms (P, S, Cl, etc.) historically were said to use empty 3d orbitals, but modern bonding theory (more accurate) says they use highly polarized bonds with significant ionic character — the "expanded octet" in PCl₅ is partly best described as PCl₄⁺ Cl⁻. For AP, the "uses d-orbitals" explanation is accepted; the more nuanced version is bonus.
- **Sigma vs pi bonds:**
  - Sigma (σ) = head-on overlap, free rotation, present in every bond (single, double, triple).
  - Pi (π) = sideways overlap, locks geometry (no rotation around double bonds — that's why cis/trans isomers exist), only present in addition to sigma in double/triple bonds.
  - Single bond = 1 σ. Double = 1 σ + 1 π. Triple = 1 σ + 2 π.
  - Counting sigma and pi in a complex molecule is a [5] FRQ move.
- **Resonance hybrid average for AP-style ranking.** The bond order in nitrate (NO₃⁻) is 4/3 (one double bond divided across three resonance structures), so the N–O bond length is between single and double. Same logic applies to carbonate (CO₃²⁻), sulfate (SO₄²⁻), and benzene.
- **VSEPR and the trigonal bipyramidal exception.** In TBP geometry (5 domains), lone pairs always go in EQUATORIAL positions, never axial — because equatorial positions have more room (only two 90° neighbors) than axial (three 90° neighbors). This is why SF₄ is see-saw (one equatorial LP), ClF₃ is T-shaped (two equatorial LPs), and XeF₂ is linear (three equatorial LPs leaving the two F atoms axial).
- **MOLECULAR POLARITY — checking by symmetry argument.** Treat the molecule as a vector sum of bond dipoles. If the molecule has a center of inversion or other symmetry that makes the bond-dipole vector sum zero, it's nonpolar. A useful trick: a molecule is nonpolar if every peripheral atom is identical AND there are no lone pairs on the central atom.
- **Bond enthalpy estimation as a [5] move on FRQs.** ΔH_rxn ≈ Σ(BE bonds broken) − Σ(BE bonds formed). Memorizing a rough table — C–H ≈ 414, C=O ≈ 745, O–H ≈ 463, O=O ≈ 498, H–H ≈ 436, C=C ≈ 614, C–C ≈ 347 (kJ/mol) — lets you estimate combustion enthalpies on the fly. Actual ΔH from formation enthalpies is more accurate; bond enthalpy is an approximation valid only in the gas phase.
- **The hybrid orbital count must equal the number of atomic orbitals used.** sp³ uses one s and three p orbitals → makes four hybrid orbitals (which then form 4 σ bonds in CH₄). For a triple bond like in C₂H₂: each C is sp hybridized (uses 1 s + 1 p → 2 sp orbitals for σ bonds; the remaining 2 p orbitals form 2 π bonds with the other carbon).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Lewis structure of SO₂

Draw the Lewis structure of SO₂ and predict its geometry, polarity, and hybridization.

- **Step 1.** Count valence electrons: S contributes 6, each O contributes 6. Total = 6 + 6 + 6 = 18 electrons.
- **Step 2.** S is the central atom (lower EN than O).
- **Step 3.** Connect S to each O with a single bond: uses 4 electrons. Remaining: 14.
- **Step 4.** Complete octets on the O atoms: each O gets 6 lone pair electrons. Uses 12 more. Remaining: 2.
- **Step 5.** Place the remaining 2 electrons as a lone pair on S. Now check: O has 8, but S has only 6 (2 bonds × 2 + 2 lone-pair) — so add a double bond from S to one of the O atoms.
- **Final structure:** O=S–O with one lone pair on S. (Resonance: the double bond can be drawn on either O, so there are two equivalent resonance structures.)
- **Geometry:** S has 3 electron domains (2 bonds + 1 LP) → trigonal planar electron geometry → bent molecular geometry (angle ~118° due to LP compression).
- **Polarity:** polar — bond dipoles don't cancel because the molecule is bent.
- **Hybridization:** sp² (3 electron domains).

### Example 2 [3][4] — Comparing lattice energies (the always-on MCQ)

Rank the following by increasing lattice energy: NaCl, MgO, KCl, CaO.

- **Step 1.** Identify the charges on each ion pair:
  - NaCl: 1+, 1−
  - KCl: 1+, 1−
  - MgO: 2+, 2−
  - CaO: 2+, 2−
- **Step 2.** Higher charge product → much higher lattice energy. So {MgO, CaO} >> {NaCl, KCl}.
- **Step 3.** Within each pair, smaller ions → higher LE. K⁺ > Na⁺ in size, so NaCl > KCl. Mg²⁺ < Ca²⁺ in size, so MgO > CaO.
- **Final ranking (lowest LE to highest):** KCl < NaCl < CaO < MgO.
- **[4] explanation:** "Lattice energy scales as |q₁q₂|/r. Doubling each charge quadruples the numerator, which dominates over the modest size differences within each row, so the alkaline-earth oxides have much higher lattice energies than the alkali halides. Within each pair, smaller ionic radius reduces r, increasing the magnitude of the Coulombic attraction."

### Example 3 [4] — Resonance and formal charge in NO₃⁻

Draw the Lewis structure of nitrate (NO₃⁻), assign formal charges, and explain why all three N–O bonds are the same length experimentally.

- **Step 1.** Count valence electrons: N = 5, each O = 6, plus 1 extra for the negative charge. Total = 5 + 18 + 1 = 24.
- **Step 2.** N is central. Connect to three O atoms with single bonds: 6 electrons used. Complete octets on O: 18 more electrons. Total used = 24.
- **Step 3.** Check N: only 3 bonds (6 electrons). Need 8. So convert one N–O single bond to a double bond.
- **Step 4.** Formal charges:
  - Double-bonded O: FC = 6 − 4 − 2 = 0
  - Two single-bonded O: FC = 6 − 6 − 1 = −1 each
  - N: FC = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1
- **Step 5.** The double bond can be placed on any of the three O atoms → three equivalent resonance structures.
- **Why all bonds are the same length:** the actual molecule is a resonance hybrid, with each N–O bond having a bond order of 4/3 (one double bond averaged over three positions). Experimentally measured N–O bond length in nitrate (~125 pm) sits between single (~136 pm) and double (~120 pm), confirming delocalization.
- **Phrase that scores:** "The actual structure of NO₃⁻ is a resonance hybrid, in which the π electrons are delocalized over all three N–O bonds. Each bond has an average bond order of 4/3, which is why all three N–O bond lengths are equivalent and intermediate between a single and double bond."

### Example 4 [4][5] — VSEPR with lone pairs: SF₄

Predict the molecular geometry, polarity, and hybridization of SF₄.

- **Step 1.** Lewis: S has 6 valence electrons, 4 F contribute 7 each = 28. Total = 34. Connect S to 4 F atoms (8 electrons). Complete octets on F (24 more electrons). Place remaining 2 on S as a lone pair.
- **Step 2.** S now has 5 electron domains (4 bonds + 1 LP) → trigonal bipyramidal electron geometry.
- **Step 3.** [5] move — place the lone pair EQUATORIALLY (not axially) because equatorial positions have more space. The molecule is see-saw shaped: 2 axial F atoms at ~177° and 2 equatorial F atoms at ~102°.
- **Polarity:** polar — the lone pair creates asymmetry, so bond dipoles don't cancel.
- **Hybridization:** sp³d (5 electron domains).

### Example 5 [5] — Sigma and pi bond counting in acetonitrile (CH₃CN)

Count the total number of sigma and pi bonds in CH₃CN.

- **Step 1.** Draw structure: H₃C–C≡N (methyl carbon bonded to nitrile carbon, which is triple-bonded to N).
- **Step 2.** Count bonds:
  - 3 C–H single bonds = 3 σ
  - 1 C–C single bond = 1 σ
  - 1 C≡N triple bond = 1 σ + 2 π
- **Step 3.** Total: 5 σ and 2 π.
- **Hybridization:** methyl C is sp³ (4 single bonds, 4 domains). Nitrile C is sp (2 domains: 1 σ to methyl C + 1 σ to N). N is sp (2 domains: σ + LP).
- **Phrase that scores:** "Every single bond contains exactly one σ bond. Each additional bond order beyond single contributes a π bond. So the C≡N triple bond consists of one σ and two π bonds."

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Lewis structure failures from missing the charge.** For a polyatomic anion, ADD electrons equal to the negative charge (NO₃⁻ adds 1). For a cation, SUBTRACT. Forgetting this is the #1 source of wrong electron counts.
2. **H is never central.** And H makes only one bond. If you find yourself making H central or making it form 2+ bonds, restart.
3. **Confusing electron geometry with molecular geometry.** Electron geometry counts ALL domains (bonding + LP). Molecular geometry counts only the positions of the atoms. Water has tetrahedral electron geometry but bent molecular geometry.
4. **Forgetting that lone pairs change the molecular geometry name.** 4 domains, 0 LP = tetrahedral. 4 domains, 1 LP = trigonal pyramidal. 4 domains, 2 LP = bent. The electron geometry is the same; the shape isn't.
5. **TBP lone pair placement.** In trigonal bipyramidal geometry, lone pairs ALWAYS go equatorial. SF₄ is see-saw, not trigonal pyramidal.
6. **Octet rule expansion.** Period 2 elements (C, N, O, F) cannot expand their octet. Don't draw 5 bonds on N. Don't put 10 electrons on O.
7. **Resonance hybrids drawn as one structure.** The molecule isn't switching between forms — it exists as a single average. AP graders accept either drawing all valid resonance structures or stating the bond order explicitly.
8. **Formal charge calculation errors.** Common slip: counting all bonding electrons toward the atom instead of half. FC = valence − lone pair electrons − ½ bonding electrons.
9. **Bond polarity vs molecular polarity confusion.** CO₂ has two polar bonds but is nonpolar overall. Polar bonds + symmetric geometry = nonpolar molecule.
10. **Lattice energy ranking errors from forgetting charge dominance.** Charge product dominates over ionic radius. MgO (2+/2−) always beats NaF (1+/1−) regardless of size.
11. **Sigma/pi miscounting.** Every bond has exactly one σ. Pi bonds add only on top of σ for double and triple bonds. Common error: counting a double bond as 2 σ.
12. **Hybridization counting bonds instead of electron domains.** Lone pairs count toward hybridization. NH₃ has 3 bonds but 4 domains (3 + 1 LP) so it's sp³, not sp².
13. **Drawing bond polarity arrows backward.** The arrow points from the LESS electronegative atom (positive end) TO the MORE electronegative atom (negative end). H–F dipole points from H toward F.
14. **Calling network covalent solids "ionic" because of high melting points.** Diamond and SiO₂ have very high melting points but are NOT ionic. They are network covalent — held by extended covalent bonding.
15. **Saying metals conduct because of "free electrons" without naming the model.** AP wants "delocalized valence electrons in a sea of electrons" or "mobile electrons in the metallic lattice." Generic "free electrons" is too vague.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

AP Chemistry FRQs on this unit want SPECIFIC, MECHANISM-NAMING language.

**For Lewis structure problems:**
- Show all valence electrons explicitly (lone pairs as dots).
- Show formal charges where helpful.
- Draw all relevant resonance structures connected by double-headed arrows.
- For partial credit, the structure must satisfy octet and total electron count.

**For VSEPR problems:**
- State the number of electron domains.
- Identify both electron geometry AND molecular geometry.
- For lone-pair-distorted angles, state "less than the idealized X°" with the reason ("because lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs").

**For polarity problems:**
- Identify the polar bonds (state ΔEN > 0 or specific pair).
- Argue the geometry (symmetric or not).
- Conclude with whether bond dipoles cancel.
- Don't forget: a single sentence connecting geometry to polarity is the rubric point.

**For lattice-energy comparisons:**
- Cite Coulomb's law explicitly: U ∝ |q₁q₂| / r.
- Compare BOTH charges and ionic radii.
- State which factor dominates if they conflict.

**For hybridization:**
- Count electron domains (not bonds).
- Map: 2 → sp, 3 → sp², 4 → sp³, 5 → sp³d, 6 → sp³d².
- For multi-atom molecules, hybridization can vary atom by atom.

**For bond enthalpy / strength comparisons:**
- Triple > double > single (more shared electrons, stronger pull).
- Within same atom pair, shorter bond = stronger bond.
- Across atom pairs, smaller atoms tend to form stronger bonds (closer approach).

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The structure has X electron domains around the central atom: Y bonding pairs and Z lone pairs. This results in a [electron geometry] electron geometry and a [molecular geometry] molecular shape."
2. "The bond angle is less than the idealized value because lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs and compress the bonding-pair angles."
3. "The molecule is nonpolar because the [symmetric geometry] causes the individual bond dipoles to cancel by vector addition."
4. "The molecule is polar because the [asymmetric geometry / lone pair on central atom] prevents the bond dipoles from canceling, producing a net molecular dipole moment."
5. "MgO has a higher lattice energy than NaCl because the product of the ionic charges is larger (2 × 2 = 4 vs 1 × 1 = 1), and per Coulomb's law (U ∝ |q₁q₂|/r), the larger charge product produces a stronger ionic attraction."
6. "The actual structure is a resonance hybrid; the π electrons are delocalized across all equivalent positions, giving each bond an order of [average] and identical bond lengths intermediate between single and double bonds."
7. "The C≡N bond consists of one σ bond and two π bonds. Every bond contains exactly one σ; each additional bond order beyond single adds one π."
8. "The central atom is sp³ hybridized because it has 4 electron domains (X bonding pairs and Y lone pairs)."
