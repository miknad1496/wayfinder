## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 8–10% of the AP World History exam
- **Era:** 1200–1450
- **Sub-topics covered:**
  - 2.1 The Silk Roads
  - 2.2 The Mongol Empire and the Modern World
  - 2.3 Exchange in the Indian Ocean
  - 2.4 Trans-Saharan Trade Routes
  - 2.5 Cultural Consequences of Connectivity
  - 2.6 Environmental Consequences of Connectivity
  - 2.7 Comparison of Economic Exchange
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Unit 2 emphasizes the BIG TRADE NETWORKS that connected Afro-Eurasia: Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, Trans-Saharan, Mongol Empire facilitation. The Mongols are a perennial topic — they unified vast trade networks across Eurasia. Cultural and environmental consequences (especially Black Death) are key.

## Big Ideas

1. **Pre-1500 trade networks already connected most of Afro-Eurasia.** Silk Roads (overland Asia), Indian Ocean (maritime Africa-Asia), Trans-Saharan (West Africa to Mediterranean). Goods, religions, technologies, and DISEASES traveled these networks.
2. **The Mongol Empire (1206-1368) facilitated unprecedented Eurasian integration.** Pax Mongolica enabled safe travel and trade across the largest contiguous empire in history.
3. **Indian Ocean trade was the most economically important.** Maritime, seasonal monsoon winds, Muslim merchant networks. Connected East Africa to East Asia.
4. **Trans-Saharan trade enriched West African empires.** Gold and salt were primary commodities. Mali's wealth derived from this trade.
5. **Disease (plague) traveled trade networks.** The Black Death (1347-1351) spread along the same routes that carried silk and spices.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **The Silk Roads:**
  - Overland routes connecting China to the Mediterranean.
  - **Goods:** silk (China), spices (India), gunpowder, paper, gold, slaves.
  - **Religions spread:** Buddhism (from India to East Asia), Islam (after 700s), Christianity (Nestorian).
  - **Technologies spread:** paper-making, printing, compass.
  - Active for over 1,500 years, with peaks during periods of strong centralized empires (Han China, Tang/Song, Mongol Empire).
- **The Mongol Empire (1206-1368):**
  - **Genghis Khan** unified Mongol tribes (1206), began conquests.
  - At peak: largest contiguous land empire in history (~24 million km²).
  - **Conquests:** China, Central Asia, Russia, Middle East, parts of Eastern Europe.
  - **Pax Mongolica:** "Mongol Peace" — political stability across Eurasia enabled safe travel and trade.
  - **Khanates:** Yuan (China), Ilkhanate (Persia/Middle East), Golden Horde (Russia), Chagatai Khanate (Central Asia).
  - **Decline:** by 1368 (Yuan replaced by Ming); other khanates fragmented.
- **Indian Ocean Trade:**
  - **Maritime network** connecting East Africa, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, China.
  - **Monsoon winds:** seasonal patterns made navigation predictable. Sail east in summer; west in winter.
  - **Goods:** spices (cloves, pepper, cinnamon), textiles, porcelain, slaves.
  - **Major ports:** Calicut (India), Malacca (Strait of Malacca), Hormuz (Persian Gulf), Kilwa (East Africa).
  - **Muslim merchant networks** dominated; many port cities became Islamicized.
  - **Pre-existing for centuries** before European arrival in 1500.
- **Trans-Saharan Trade:**
  - Camel caravans across Sahara desert.
  - **Goods:** gold (south to north), salt (north to south), slaves.
  - **Mali Empire** dominated this trade in our period.
  - **Timbuktu** as major commercial and intellectual center.
- **Cultural Consequences of Connectivity:**
  - **Spread of religions:** Islam expanded through trade routes; Buddhism along Silk Roads; Hinduism in Southeast Asia.
  - **Spread of technologies:** paper, printing, gunpowder, compass moved from China westward.
  - **Spread of crops:** champa rice, sugar, citrus traveled trade routes.
  - **Cultural exchanges** at ports and trading cities created cosmopolitan societies.
- **Environmental Consequences of Connectivity:**
  - **Diffusion of pathogens.** The most important: BLACK DEATH (1347-1351). Bubonic plague spread from Central Asia along Mongol-protected trade routes to Europe. Killed 30-60% of European population.
  - **Diffusion of crops:** new agricultural products in different regions.
  - **Diffusion of agricultural practices.**

### Adds for [4]

- **Why Mongol conquests succeeded.**
  - **Mobility** of cavalry-based Mongol armies.
  - **Brilliant tactics** (siege warfare, integration of conquered peoples' technologies).
  - **Ruthless** when resisted; merciful when surrendered.
  - **Adaptability:** integrated diverse peoples into administration.
- **Mongol impact on world history (per brain file's signature):**
  - **United Eurasia under one military system.**
  - **Pax Mongolica** enabled trade across entire continent.
  - **Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta** could travel safely.
  - **Disease spread** (Black Death traveled Mongol trade routes).
  - **Technological exchange** accelerated (gunpowder reached Europe via Mongol-controlled territory).
  - **Mongol decline** (1368) coincided with major shifts (Black Death, Ming restoration).
- **Why Indian Ocean trade was different from Silk Roads.**
  - **Maritime** vs land — could carry more bulk goods.
  - **Less affected by political fragmentation** (sea more democratic than land).
  - **Monsoon predictability** enabled safer commerce.
  - **Muslim merchant networks** provided shared legal and religious framework.
  - **Larger volume** of goods than overland Silk Roads.
- **Why Mali's wealth was distinctive.**
  - **Gold supply** controlled (Wangara goldfields).
  - **Trans-Saharan caravans** required protection that Mali provided.
  - **Mansa Musa's hajj** displayed wealth on global stage.
  - **Timbuktu's University of Sankoré** attracted Islamic scholars.
- **Black Death's path.**
  - Originated in Central Asia.
  - Spread westward along Silk Roads (Mongol-protected).
  - Reached Crimean ports (Kaffa, 1346) — siege used plague-infected corpses.
  - Genoese ships brought plague to Europe (1347).
  - Spread rapidly: Sicily → Italy → France → Spain → Britain → Scandinavia → Russia.
  - **Lost:** 30-60% of Europe's population. Major economic, social, religious upheaval.
- **Why pre-1500 trade routes matter.** Without understanding these, the European "Age of Exploration" looks more revolutionary than it was. Europeans were primarily ENTERING existing trade networks — not creating them.

### Adds for [5]

- **The "Pax Mongolica" framework.** Despite Mongol violence in conquests, ONCE PEACE was established, the unified rule across Eurasia enabled unprecedented trade and cultural exchange. The Pax Mongolica is sometimes credited as the period that made later globalization possible.
- **Why religious networks shape trade networks.** Muslim merchant networks dominated Indian Ocean trade because shared religion provided shared law, language (Arabic for commerce), and trust. Similarly, Christian merchant networks linked Mediterranean cities. Religion structured commerce.
- **The "Columbian Exchange" preview.** Pre-1500 networks linked Afro-Eurasia. The Americas were ISOLATED from these networks. This isolation explains:
  - Why diseases like smallpox were devastating to Native populations after 1492.
  - Why neither side knew of the other for thousands of years.
  - Why the Columbian Exchange (1492 onward) was such a transformative event.
- **Comparing trade networks (per the brain file's emphasis):**
  - **Silk Roads:** overland, slow, expensive, luxury goods, religious diffusion, vulnerable to political instability.
  - **Indian Ocean:** maritime, faster, larger volume, Muslim merchant networks, monsoon-dependent.
  - **Trans-Saharan:** desert caravan, gold-salt focused, Islamic learning networks, slower than Indian Ocean.
- **The "first globalization" thesis.** Some historians argue that the Mongol-era Pax Mongolica and Indian Ocean trade represent the FIRST GLOBALIZATION — the initial integration of Afro-Eurasia into a single economic and cultural system. The Americas joined this system after 1492; full globalization arrived in the 19th century.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Trade route identification

A merchant in 1300 brings silk from China to Italy via overland routes. Which trade network is this?
- **Silk Roads** — overland routes connecting China to Mediterranean.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Pax Mongolica significance

How did the Pax Mongolica facilitate trade?
- **Political unity:** single empire ruling vast Eurasian territory.
- **Safe travel:** Mongol authority protected merchants from bandits.
- **Standardized practices:** trade laws, weights, measures.
- **Encouraged exchange:** Mongols promoted commerce as economically beneficial.
- **Result:** unprecedented Eurasian integration; Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta could travel safely.

### Example 3 [4] — Comparing Silk Roads and Indian Ocean

Compare these networks.
- **Both:** facilitated cultural exchange, technological diffusion, religious spread.
- **Silk Roads:** overland, slower, smaller volume, luxury goods primarily, vulnerable to political fragmentation.
- **Indian Ocean:** maritime, faster, larger volume (sea carries bulk goods), Muslim merchant networks, monsoon-dependent.

### Example 4 [4][5] — Black Death analysis

Explain how the Black Death spread and its consequences.
- **Spread:** originated in Central Asia, traveled westward along Mongol-protected Silk Roads.
- **Mechanism:** rats and fleas carried plague bacteria. Trade ships and caravans transported them.
- **Reach:** Genoese ships brought to Europe (1347). Spread rapidly across continent.
- **Death toll:** 30-60% of European population.
- **Economic consequences:** labor shortage → higher wages → weakened serfdom.
- **Social consequences:** questioned authority of Catholic Church (couldn't prevent plague).
- **Long-term:** contributed to Renaissance, Reformation, eventual modern Europe.

### Example 5 [5] — Sophistication move on global connectivity

Common framing: Europeans "discovered" world trade after 1500. Sophisticated framing: Afro-Eurasian trade networks integrated most of the OLD world for centuries before European maritime expansion. The Mongol Pax (13th-14th centuries), Indian Ocean Muslim merchant networks, and Trans-Saharan caravans constituted what some historians call the "first globalization." Europeans in 1500 were primarily ENTERING this existing system, NOT creating it. The novel European contribution was integrating the AMERICAS into the system, not creating global trade.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Treating trade networks as static.** They expanded and contracted; depended on political stability.
2. **Overcrediting Europeans for trade networks.** Most networks predated European involvement by centuries.
3. **Misunderstanding Mongol legacy.** Conquests were violent; subsequent Pax Mongolica enabled trade.
4. **Wrong dates for Black Death.** 1347-1351 (peak); continued in waves through 1400s.
5. **Confusing types of religious diffusion.** Buddhism along Silk Roads. Islam along Indian Ocean and Trans-Saharan. Hinduism in Southeast Asia.
6. **Treating Indian Ocean trade as European.** Pre-1500: dominated by Muslim merchants. Europeans entered later.
7. **Forgetting trade networks carried diseases.** Black Death spread along the same routes as silk and spices.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For comparative trade essays:**
- Compare on multiple dimensions (geography, goods, merchants, scale).
- Use specific examples (Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, Trans-Saharan).

**For Mongol Empire essays:**
- Address conquest period AND subsequent Pax Mongolica.
- Trace consequences (trade, disease, cultural exchange).

**For DBQ-style sourcing:**
- HIPP for documents.
- Note merchant perspectives may differ from rulers, religious authorities, or peasants.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The Silk Roads facilitated overland trade between China, Central Asia, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean — carrying not only goods but also religions (Buddhism, Islam, Nestorian Christianity), technologies (paper, gunpowder), and diseases."
2. "The Pax Mongolica — the period of Mongol-imposed peace and stability across Eurasia (c. 1250-1350) — enabled unprecedented trade, travel, and cultural exchange. Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta were among many who traveled safely across vast distances."
3. "Indian Ocean trade, made predictable by seasonal monsoon winds, connected East Africa, the Middle East, India, Southeast Asia, and China. Muslim merchant networks dominated this trade through shared religion, language (Arabic for commerce), and legal frameworks."
4. "The Black Death (1347-1351) killed 30-60% of Europe's population. The plague spread from Central Asia along Mongol-protected trade routes, eventually reaching Europe through Genoese trading ships. Its consequences transformed European labor, social structure, religion, and culture."
5. "Trans-Saharan trade routes connected West Africa to North Africa across the Sahara Desert. Salt traveled south; gold traveled north. The Mali Empire's wealth derived from this trade, and Timbuktu became a major commercial and Islamic intellectual center."
6. "Pre-1500 trade networks integrated most of Afro-Eurasia. Europeans entering Indian Ocean trade in the 1500s primarily JOINED an existing economic system rather than created one. The truly novel European contribution was the integration of the Americas, which had been isolated from these networks."
