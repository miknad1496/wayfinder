## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 10–13% of the AP Microeconomics exam
- **Sub-topics covered:**
  - 5.1 Introduction to Factor Markets
  - 5.2 Changes in Factor Demand and Factor Supply
  - 5.3 Profit-Maximizing Behavior in Perfectly Competitive Factor Markets
  - 5.4 Monopsonistic Markets
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Unit 5 applies the supply/demand framework to LABOR and other inputs (rather than goods). The MRP (Marginal Revenue Product) concept and the firm's hiring decision (MRP = MFC) are essential. Monopsony (single buyer of labor) is a perennial topic — graphs and analysis appear regularly.

## Big Ideas

1. **Factor markets are markets for INPUTS to production.** Labor, land, capital, entrepreneurship. Same supply/demand logic but applied to inputs.
2. **DERIVED DEMAND.** Demand for inputs is DERIVED from demand for the products they help produce. If product demand rises, input demand rises.
3. **The hiring rule: MRP = MFC.** A firm hires inputs up to where the additional revenue from one more unit (Marginal Revenue Product) equals the additional cost (Marginal Factor Cost).
4. **In competitive labor markets, MFC = wage.** Firm is price-taker in labor market. Wage is set externally.
5. **Monopsony: single buyer of labor.** Firm has market power as employer. MFC > wage (must raise wage for ALL workers to hire one more). Hires fewer workers and pays lower wage than competitive market.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Factor markets:** markets for inputs to production (labor, land, capital, entrepreneurship).
- **Derived demand:** demand for an input depends on demand for the OUTPUT the input helps produce.
  - Example: demand for autoworkers depends on demand for cars.
- **Marginal Product (MP):** additional output from one more unit of input.
- **Marginal Revenue Product (MRP):** additional revenue from selling the extra output produced by one more input.
  - **MRP = MP × P** (in perfect competition).
  - **MRP = MP × MR** (in imperfect competition, where MR < P).
- **Marginal Factor Cost (MFC):** additional cost of one more unit of input.
- **Hiring rule:** hire inputs up to where MRP = MFC.
- **Perfect Competition in factor markets:**
  - **Many firms hiring** the input.
  - **Many workers (or input suppliers)** offering the input.
  - **Identical inputs.**
  - **Free entry/exit.**
  - **Each firm is a PRICE TAKER** in the input market.
  - **MFC = wage rate** (since firm faces horizontal supply at market wage).
- **Demand for labor curve = MRP curve.** Downward-sloping (diminishing returns).
- **Supply of labor curve:** generally upward-sloping (higher wages attract more workers).
- **Equilibrium wage = market wage** where labor supply meets labor demand.
- **Monopsony:** single BUYER of input (labor).
  - Examples: company town with one employer; major government as employer.
- **Monopsony graph:**
  - **Labor SUPPLY** is upward-sloping (firm faces entire labor supply curve).
  - **MFC is ABOVE supply** (must raise wage for ALL workers to hire one more — unlike competitive case where MFC = wage).
  - **Firm hires** where MRP = MFC.
  - **Wage is read from the SUPPLY curve** (lower than MFC).
  - **Result:** fewer workers hired AND lower wage compared to competitive labor market.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why MRP = MP × P in perfect competition:** the additional output (MP) sold at market price (P) gives MRP. Since price doesn't depend on quantity sold, MRP and MP move together.
- **Why MRP = MP × MR in imperfect competition:** the firm's price falls as it sells more output (downward-sloping product demand). MR = additional revenue from one more unit. So MRP = MP × MR < MP × P.
- **Hiring rule in detail:**
  - If MRP > MFC: hiring more would add to profit.
  - If MFC > MRP: hiring less would add to profit.
  - **Optimal:** MRP = MFC.
- **Why labor demand is downward-sloping (besides diminishing returns):**
  - Higher wage → fewer workers cost-effective at given output level.
  - Substitution effect: at higher wages, firms substitute capital for labor.
- **Shifters of labor demand:**
  - **Demand for output** (more demand → more derived demand for labor).
  - **Productivity** (more productive workers → more demand).
  - **Price of substitutes/complements** (e.g., capital — if capital cheaper, demand for labor falls).
  - **Number of firms hiring.**
- **Shifters of labor supply:**
  - **Population** (more workers → supply right).
  - **Immigration policy.**
  - **Worker preferences** (working vs leisure).
  - **Wages in alternative occupations.**
  - **Cost of training/education.**
- **Monopsony in detail:**
  - Firm is the SOLE buyer.
  - Faces UPWARD-sloping labor supply (must offer higher wage to attract more workers).
  - **Marginal Factor Cost (MFC):** additional cost of one more worker.
  - For monopsonist: MFC = wage of new worker + wage increase paid to ALL existing workers.
  - This is HIGHER than the wage from the supply curve.
- **Monopsony hiring decision:**
  - Hire where MRP = MFC.
  - Read wage from labor supply curve at this quantity.
  - Wage < MFC.
  - Compared to competitive market: monopsony hires FEWER workers at LOWER wage.
- **Inefficiency in monopsony:**
  - Workers receive less than their marginal contribution (MRP).
  - Some workers willing to work (at any wage above their reservation wage) but firm doesn't hire because MFC of additional worker exceeds MRP.
  - DEADWEIGHT LOSS in factor market.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why minimum wages can INCREASE employment in monopsony markets.** In a competitive labor market, minimum wage above equilibrium creates unemployment. But in MONOPSONY: a properly-set minimum wage can simultaneously RAISE wages AND INCREASE employment by forcing the firm to face a horizontal supply (essentially turning the firm into a price-taker for labor).
- **Why labor markets often have monopsony elements.** Local labor markets may have few employers in specific industries. Hospital systems, school districts, and large local employers can have monopsony power. Even in competitive industries, search costs and geographic constraints create local monopsony.
- **Why MRP captures everything that matters for hiring.** A firm cares about the additional revenue from a worker (MRP), not about output (MP) per se. In imperfect competition, lower price for additional output reduces MRP below MP × P.
- **Wage discrimination and discrimination in factor markets.** Just as price discrimination in product markets, employers can sometimes pay different wages to different workers. Antidiscrimination laws limit this.
- **Why factor markets matter for income distribution.** Wages, rents, and interest rates determined in factor markets determine how income is distributed. Higher labor demand → higher wages → workers' share of national income.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — MRP calculation

A firm sells output at $10 per unit. Each additional worker produces 5 units of output.

(a) What is MRP for this worker?

- MRP = MP × P = 5 × $10 = $50.
- The firm earns an additional $50 from hiring this worker.

### Example 2 [3] — Hiring decision (perfect competition)

A firm faces market wage of $40 per hour. The marginal revenue product schedule is:

| Worker | MRP |
|--------|-----|
| 1st | $80 |
| 2nd | $60 |
| 3rd | $40 |
| 4th | $20 |

(a) How many workers should the firm hire?

- **Rule:** hire while MRP > wage (MFC).
- Worker 1: MRP $80 > $40. Hire.
- Worker 2: MRP $60 > $40. Hire.
- Worker 3: MRP $40 = $40. Indifferent (hire).
- Worker 4: MRP $20 < $40. Don't hire.
- **Hire 3 workers.**

### Example 3 [4] — Monopsony graph

In a small town, there's only one major employer. The firm faces upward-sloping labor supply.

| Workers | Wage from Supply | MFC |
|---------|-------------------|-----|
| 1 | $10 | $10 |
| 2 | $12 | $14 |
| 3 | $14 | $18 |
| 4 | $16 | $22 |

MRP at each level: 1st: $25, 2nd: $20, 3rd: $15, 4th: $10.

(a) How many workers will the firm hire?
(b) What wage will it pay?

- **(a)** Hire while MRP ≥ MFC.
  - Worker 1: MRP $25 > MFC $10. Hire.
  - Worker 2: MRP $20 > MFC $14. Hire.
  - Worker 3: MRP $15 < MFC $18. Don't hire.
- Hire 2 workers.
- **(b)** Wage at 2 workers (from SUPPLY curve, not MFC): $12.
- **Note:** in competitive market, equilibrium would be where MRP = wage from supply curve. Workers would receive higher wage AND firm would hire more.

### Example 4 [4] — Comparing competitive labor market vs monopsony

(a) In which market are wages higher?
(b) In which is employment higher?
(c) Which is efficient?

- **(a) Competitive market has higher wage** (workers receive their MRP).
- **(b) Competitive market has higher employment** (more jobs at equilibrium).
- **(c) Competitive market is efficient.** Monopsony causes deadweight loss — workers willing to work at wages firm could afford, but firm doesn't hire.

### Example 5 [5] — Why minimum wage may help in monopsony

A monopsonist firm pays $12 wage and hires 50 workers. Government imposes $15 minimum wage.

(a) Predict effect.

- **In a competitive market:** minimum wage above equilibrium creates unemployment.
- **In monopsony:** minimum wage forces firm to face HORIZONTAL labor supply at $15.
- The firm's MFC becomes constant at $15.
- Firm now hires where MRP = $15. May hire MORE workers than before.
- Wages rise AND employment rises.
- This is the counterintuitive monopsony result — context matters for minimum wage effects.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing demand for output and demand for input.** Demand for inputs is DERIVED from demand for outputs.
2. **Wrong MRP formula.** MRP = MP × P (perfect competition). MRP = MP × MR (imperfect competition).
3. **Forgetting MFC = wage in competitive market.** Firm is price taker in labor market.
4. **Treating monopsony like perfect competition.** Monopsony has UPWARD-sloping supply that firm sees, with MFC ABOVE supply.
5. **Forgetting to read wage from supply curve in monopsony.** MFC tells you total cost; wage comes from supply.
6. **Wrong hiring rule.** Hire while MRP > MFC. Stop when MRP = MFC.
7. **Confusing MFC with wage.** In competitive: equal. In monopsony: MFC > wage.
8. **Misnaming factor markets.** Labor, land, capital, entrepreneurship.
9. **Forgetting derived demand explanation.** When product demand changes, factor demand changes too.
10. **Wrong interpretation of monopsony minimum wage effect.** In monopsony, a properly-set minimum wage can BOTH raise wages AND increase employment.
11. **Confusing MP and MRP.** MP = additional output. MRP = additional REVENUE.
12. **Treating labor market separately from product market.** They're connected through derived demand.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For factor market questions:**
- Identify factor type (labor, land, capital).
- Recognize derived demand.
- Apply MRP = MFC for hiring decision.

**For competitive labor market:**
- MFC = wage (firm is price taker).
- Hire where MRP = wage.

**For monopsony:**
- Draw upward-sloping supply.
- MFC ABOVE supply.
- Hire where MRP = MFC.
- Wage from supply curve at that quantity.
- Lower employment AND lower wage than competitive.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Demand for inputs (labor, capital, etc.) is DERIVED from demand for the outputs they help produce. When product demand rises, input demand rises proportionally."
2. "A firm hires inputs up to where Marginal Revenue Product (MRP) equals Marginal Factor Cost (MFC). MRP = MP × P in perfect competition; MRP = MP × MR in imperfect competition."
3. "In a competitive labor market, the firm is a PRICE TAKER. MFC = wage rate. The firm hires where MRP = wage."
4. "Monopsony is a single-buyer market. The firm faces an upward-sloping labor supply curve. To hire one more worker, the firm must raise wages for ALL workers, making MFC > wage."
5. "Compared to a competitive labor market, monopsony results in BOTH lower wages AND fewer workers hired. Workers willing to work at wages the firm could afford are excluded — creating allocative inefficiency."
6. "In a monopsony, a properly-set minimum wage can SIMULTANEOUSLY raise wages AND increase employment by transforming the firm's MFC into a horizontal line at the minimum wage."
7. "The Marginal Revenue Product is downward-sloping due to diminishing returns: as more workers are added with fixed capital, each worker's marginal product decreases."
