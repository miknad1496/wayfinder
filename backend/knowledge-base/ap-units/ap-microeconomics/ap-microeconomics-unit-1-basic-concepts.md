## Big Ideas

1. **Scarcity is the universal economic problem.** All economic concepts derive from limited resources facing unlimited wants.
2. **Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative.** Every choice has a cost.
3. **Comparative advantage drives gains from trade.** Specialization based on lower opportunity cost benefits both parties.
4. **Marginal analysis = comparing additional benefit to additional cost.** Decisions made at the margin maximize utility.
5. **Production Possibilities Curve illustrates trade-offs and opportunity costs.** Points on curve are efficient; inside are inefficient; outside are currently impossible.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Scarcity:** unlimited wants meet limited resources.
- **Opportunity cost:** value of the next-best alternative foregone.
- **Resources / factors of production:** Land, Labor, Capital, Entrepreneurship.
- **Three economic questions:**
  - WHAT to produce?
  - HOW to produce?
  - FOR WHOM to produce?
- **Economic systems:** market, command, mixed, traditional.
- **Production Possibilities Curve (PPC):**
  - Y-axis: one good. X-axis: another good.
  - Curve shows maximum combinations possible.
  - Points ON curve: efficient.
  - Points INSIDE: inefficient.
  - Points OUTSIDE: currently impossible.
  - Outward shift: economic growth.
- **PPC shape:**
  - **Linear:** constant opportunity costs.
  - **Concave (bowed out):** increasing opportunity costs (typical).
- **Absolute advantage:** producing more with same resources.
- **Comparative advantage:** producing at lower opportunity cost.
- **Comparative advantage drives gains from trade.** Both parties benefit when each specializes in their lower-opportunity-cost good.
- **Two methods for comparative advantage:**
  - **Output method:** when producing output (e.g., bushels per worker).
  - **Input method:** when using inputs (e.g., hours per bushel).
- **Marginal analysis:**
  - **Marginal benefit (MB):** additional benefit from one more unit.
  - **Marginal cost (MC):** additional cost of one more unit.
  - **Decision rule:** do the activity if MB ≥ MC. Stop when MB = MC.
- **Consumer choice and utility maximization:** consumers choose to maximize utility per dollar spent. Equimarginal principle: MU_A/P_A = MU_B/P_B at optimum.

### Adds for [4]

- **Why PPC bows outward.** Resources aren't perfectly substitutable. As you specialize more in one good, you have to use less-suited resources, requiring more sacrifice of the other good.
- **Comparative advantage worked example:**
  - Country X: 100 cars OR 200 wheat per year.
  - Country Y: 50 cars OR 150 wheat per year.
  - X's opportunity cost of 1 car = 2 wheat. Y's = 3 wheat.
  - X has comparative advantage in cars; Y in wheat.
  - Trade benefits both at terms between 2 and 3 wheat per car.
- **Marginal utility per dollar (consumer choice):**
  - Maximize: choose where MU/P is equal across all goods.
  - If MU_A/P_A > MU_B/P_B, buy more A.
- **Diminishing marginal utility:** each additional unit of a good provides less additional satisfaction. Why we don't consume infinite of any one thing.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why comparative advantage matters even with absolute disadvantage.** A country that's worse at producing EVERYTHING still benefits from specializing in what it's "least bad" at and trading. This is what makes free trade beneficial in principle.
- **Consumer choice as marginal optimization.** The marginal utility per dollar equation captures rational consumer behavior — allocating spending to maximize total utility.
- **The "law of equimarginal principle"** is the consumer-choice equivalent of MR = MC for firms.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — PPC interpretation

A country produces wheat and cars. With no growth, the PPC is concave (bowed out).

(a) Identify points: on the curve, inside, outside.

- **(a)** ON the curve: maximum production using all resources efficiently. INSIDE: underutilizing resources (unemployed labor, idle capital). OUTSIDE: currently impossible without economic growth.

### Example 2 [3] — Comparative advantage

USA and Mexico:
- USA: 100 cars OR 50 wheat per worker.
- Mexico: 30 cars OR 20 wheat per worker.

Identify comparative advantages.

- USA's opportunity cost of 1 car = 50/100 = 0.5 wheat.
- Mexico's opportunity cost of 1 car = 20/30 = 0.67 wheat.
- USA's lower → USA has COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE in cars.
- Mexico's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 30/20 = 1.5 cars.
- USA's opportunity cost of 1 wheat = 100/50 = 2 cars.
- Mexico has lower → COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE in wheat.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing absolute and comparative advantage.** Comparative advantage drives trade.
2. **Wrong direction for PPC shifts.** Outward = growth. Inward = contraction.
3. **Treating marginal as average.** Marginal = ADDITIONAL. Average = total/quantity.
4. **Forgetting opportunity cost includes implicit costs.** Time, foregone alternatives.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For PPC questions:** identify points, predict shifts.

**For comparative advantage:** compute opportunity costs both ways; compare.

**For marginal analysis:** compare MB to MC.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Country [X] has COMPARATIVE ADVANTAGE in [good] because its opportunity cost of producing [good] is LOWER than [other country]."
2. "The PPC is concave because of increasing opportunity costs — resources aren't perfectly substitutable."
3. "Marginal analysis: do the activity if MB ≥ MC; stop when MB = MC."
