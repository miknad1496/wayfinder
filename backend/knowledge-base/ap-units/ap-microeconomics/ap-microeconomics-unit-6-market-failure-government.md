## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 8–13% of the AP Microeconomics exam
- **Sub-topics covered:**
  - 6.1 Socially Efficient and Inefficient Market Outcomes
  - 6.2 Externalities
  - 6.3 Public and Private Goods
  - 6.4 The Effects of Government Intervention in Different Market Structures
  - 6.5 Income and Wealth Inequality
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Unit 6 addresses why and how markets sometimes fail. Externalities (positive and negative), public goods, and government interventions are common FRQ topics. The graphs showing externalities and the policy responses (Pigouvian taxes, subsidies) are essential. Income inequality often appears as conceptual MCQ.

## Big Ideas

1. **Markets sometimes fail to achieve efficient outcomes.** Causes: externalities, public goods, market power, information asymmetries.
2. **Externalities are costs or benefits that affect parties outside the transaction.** Negative (pollution): MSC > MPC, market overproduces. Positive (vaccines): MSB > MPB, market underproduces.
3. **Pigouvian taxes/subsidies internalize externalities.** Tax on pollution makes producers face full social cost; subsidy on education makes consumers face full social benefit.
4. **Public goods are non-rivalrous AND non-excludable — markets fail to provide them.** Defense, lighthouses, basic research. Often provided by government.
5. **Income and wealth inequality** are often addressed by progressive taxation, transfer programs, and other policies.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Market failure:** when markets don't allocate resources efficiently. Multiple causes:
  - **Externalities.**
  - **Public goods.**
  - **Market power (monopolies — Unit 4).**
  - **Information asymmetries.**
- **Externalities:** costs or benefits of an activity that affect THIRD PARTIES (not buyer or seller).
  - **Negative externality:** activity creates COSTS to third parties (pollution, secondhand smoke, traffic congestion).
  - **Positive externality:** activity creates BENEFITS to third parties (vaccinations, education, well-maintained property).
- **Marginal Social Cost (MSC) vs Marginal Private Cost (MPC):**
  - **MSC = MPC + cost imposed on third parties (negative externality).**
  - In negative externality: MSC > MPC.
- **Marginal Social Benefit (MSB) vs Marginal Private Benefit (MPB):**
  - **MSB = MPB + benefit to third parties (positive externality).**
  - In positive externality: MSB > MPB.
- **Optimal social outcome:** where MSB = MSC.
- **Free market outcome:** where MPB = MPC.
- **Negative externality:**
  - Market produces TOO MUCH (where MPC = MPB, but MSC > MPC).
  - Quantity exceeds socially optimal.
  - Deadweight loss.
- **Positive externality:**
  - Market produces TOO LITTLE (where MPB = MPC, but MSB > MPB).
  - Quantity below socially optimal.
  - Deadweight loss.
- **Pigouvian tax (corrective tax):** tax on negative externality activities to make producers face MSC.
  - Shifts MPC up to equal MSC.
  - Reduces output to socially optimal quantity.
  - Examples: carbon tax, alcohol tax, gas tax.
- **Pigouvian subsidy:** subsidy on positive externality activities to make consumers face MSB.
  - Shifts MPB up to equal MSB.
  - Increases output to socially optimal quantity.
  - Examples: education subsidies, vaccination subsidies.
- **Public goods:** characterized by:
  - **Non-rivalrous:** one person's use doesn't reduce another's (defense, broadcast TV).
  - **Non-excludable:** can't prevent non-payers from benefiting (national defense).
  - **Result:** "free rider" problem — markets fail to provide them.
- **Free rider problem:** people benefit without paying. Markets can't charge for non-excludable goods.
- **Common pool resources:** rivalrous AND non-excludable (fish stocks, atmosphere). Subject to "tragedy of the commons" — overuse.
- **Private goods:** rivalrous AND excludable (most goods). Markets work well for these.
- **Income inequality measures:**
  - **Lorenz curve:** plots cumulative income share vs cumulative population share.
  - **Gini coefficient:** 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality).
- **Government policies for inequality:**
  - **Progressive taxation:** higher rates on higher incomes.
  - **Transfer programs:** Social Security, food stamps, unemployment.
  - **Public goods:** education, healthcare access.
  - **Minimum wages, union rights.**

### Adds for [4]

- **Negative externality graph:**
  - **Demand curve = MPB = MSB** (no consumption externalities assumed).
  - **Supply curve = MPC** (private supply curve, ignoring externalities).
  - **MSC = MPC + external cost (above MPC).**
  - **Free market equilibrium:** at MPC = MPB.
  - **Socially optimal:** at MSC = MSB.
  - **Output is too high; DWL** is triangle between MSC and MPC from socially optimal Q to free market Q.
- **Positive externality graph:**
  - **Supply curve = MPC = MSC** (no production externalities).
  - **Demand curve = MPB.**
  - **MSB = MPB + external benefit (above MPB).**
  - **Free market equilibrium:** at MPC = MPB.
  - **Socially optimal:** at MSC = MSB.
  - **Output is too low; DWL** is triangle from free market Q to socially optimal Q.
- **Pigouvian tax visualization:**
  - Tax shifts MPC UP by exactly the external cost.
  - New equilibrium achieves MSC = MSB.
  - Optimal output achieved.
- **Pigouvian subsidy visualization:**
  - Subsidy shifts MPB UP by exactly the external benefit.
  - New equilibrium achieves MSC = MSB.
  - Optimal output achieved.
- **Coase Theorem:** if property rights are clearly defined and bargaining costs are low, parties can reach efficient outcomes through PRIVATE NEGOTIATION (not government regulation needed).
  - **Limit:** in practice, transaction costs and many parties make this impractical.
- **Common pool resource problem:**
  - Each user has incentive to use more (private benefit accrues to them).
  - But each user's overuse imposes costs on others (negative externality).
  - Result: depletion (overfishing, deforestation, climate change).
  - **Solutions:** privatization (assign property rights), regulation (catch limits, pollution standards), community management.
- **Asymmetric information:**
  - **Adverse selection:** one party knows more than other when entering into transaction (e.g., used cars — sellers know quality, buyers don't; insurance — applicants know health, insurers don't).
  - **Moral hazard:** one party changes behavior after transaction (e.g., insured drivers driving more recklessly).
- **Government failure:** government policies can also create inefficiencies. Reasons:
  - **Information limitations** — government may not know optimal policy.
  - **Political pressure** — special interests influence policy.
  - **Implementation problems.**
  - **Unintended consequences.**

### Adds for [5]

- **Why externalities are market failures.** In a free market without externalities, producers and consumers internalize all costs and benefits, leading to efficient outcomes. With externalities, third parties bear costs or receive benefits NOT reflected in the market — leading to over- or under-production from society's perspective.
- **Why public goods can't be provided efficiently by markets.** Non-excludability means producers can't charge users; non-rivalry means society loses if production is restricted to those who pay. Markets fail. Government provision (or other collective mechanisms) needed.
- **Why income inequality has both efficiency and equity implications.** Unequal income may reflect productivity differences (efficient) but also create unequal opportunities (inefficient) and unfairness (equity concerns). Trade-offs matter.
- **The "tragedy of the commons" framework.** Garrett Hardin (1968) — when resources are commonly held without regulation, individual users have incentive to take more than is sustainable. The atmosphere is the largest commons; climate change is the largest tragedy of the commons.
- **Why corrective taxes/subsidies work theoretically but face implementation problems.** Calculating the right tax/subsidy requires knowing the magnitude of the externality. Politicians may resist (carbon tax has been politically difficult). Distributional effects (carbon tax may hit lower-income disproportionately).

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Negative externality

A factory pollutes nearby streams. Each ton of factory output imposes $5 of damage on third parties.

(a) What's the socially optimal quantity vs free-market quantity?

- Free market: producers maximize private profit (P = MPC).
- MPC ignores the $5 external cost.
- Free market produces MORE than socially optimal.
- Socially optimal: P = MSC = MPC + $5.
- DWL = triangle between MSC and MPC from socially optimal Q to free market Q.
- **Pigouvian tax of $5 per ton** would internalize the externality and achieve socially optimal output.

### Example 2 [3] — Positive externality

Vaccinations protect both the recipient AND others (herd immunity). Each vaccination provides $30 in benefits to recipient and $20 to society from reduced disease spread.

(a) Compare market vs socially optimal quantity.
(b) What policy would correct this?

- **(a)** **Free market:** MPB = $30. People vaccinate up to where MPB = price.
- **Socially optimal:** MSB = $30 + $20 = $50. More vaccinations would benefit society.
- Free market produces TOO LITTLE vaccination.
- **(b)** **Pigouvian subsidy of $20 per vaccination** would internalize the external benefit. Vaccination would be cheaper to consumers, and they'd vaccinate at socially optimal rate.

### Example 3 [3] — Public good

National defense is non-rivalrous (one person's protection doesn't reduce another's) and non-excludable (can't exclude non-payers from defense).

(a) Why won't markets provide it?
(b) How is it provided?

- **(a)** **Free rider problem:** if people can benefit without paying, they won't pay. Producers can't charge users (non-excludable). Markets fail.
- **(b)** Government provides through tax-funded budgets. Tax requirements ensure everyone contributes proportionally.

### Example 4 [4] — Asymmetric information

In a used car market, sellers know each car's quality but buyers can only judge from outside.

(a) What problem arises?
(b) What solutions exist?

- **(a)** **Adverse selection:** buyers, unable to distinguish good from bad cars, only willing to pay an "average" price. Sellers of good cars receive less than their car is worth, so they exit market. Quality declines, prices fall further. Eventually only "lemons" remain.
- **(b) Solutions:**
  - Warranties (signal quality).
  - Independent inspections.
  - Reputation (CarMax, dealer reviews).
  - Government-required disclosures.
  - Mandatory inspections.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Income inequality

A country's Gini coefficient = 0.5.

(a) Interpret.
(b) What policies could reduce inequality?

- **(a)** Gini of 0.5 indicates significant inequality. The Lorenz curve is significantly bowed away from the line of perfect equality.
- **(b)** **Policies:**
  - **Progressive taxation:** higher tax rates on high incomes.
  - **Transfer programs:** Social Security, unemployment, food assistance.
  - **Public goods:** universal education, healthcare access.
  - **Wage policies:** minimum wage, support for unions.
  - **Investment in education and skills training.**
- **Trade-off:** policies that reduce inequality may have efficiency costs (high marginal tax rates may reduce work incentives). Optimal policy balances equity and efficiency.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing private and social costs.** Private = costs to producer. Social = total costs (private + external).
2. **Wrong direction for externality effect.** Negative externality → market produces TOO MUCH. Positive externality → too LITTLE.
3. **Confusing externalities and public goods.** Externalities = third-party effects. Public goods = non-rivalrous/non-excludable goods.
4. **Forgetting Coase Theorem conditions.** Requires defined property rights and low transaction costs.
5. **Treating all government failures as market failures.** Government policies can create inefficiencies too.
6. **Wrong tax/subsidy size.** Pigouvian tax should equal marginal external cost; subsidy should equal marginal external benefit.
7. **Confusing rivalry and excludability.** Rival vs non-rival = whether use depletes. Excludable vs non-excludable = whether can prevent access.
8. **Wrong DWL location.** For negative externality, DWL is between MSC and MPC from socially optimal Q to free market Q.
9. **Ignoring distribution implications of efficiency policies.** Carbon tax may be efficient but regressive in income terms.
10. **Treating market failure as binary.** Markets often partially fail (some externality, some public good characteristic, some information asymmetry).
11. **Confusing private goods with private companies.** A private company can produce a public good (research labs); a private good can be provided by government (public housing).
12. **Misinterpreting Lorenz curve.** Steeper deviation from 45-degree line = MORE inequality.
13. **Using progressive tax incorrectly.** Progressive = higher rates on higher incomes (not just higher taxes).
14. **Forgetting deadweight loss in inefficient markets.** Externalities, market power, public goods all create DWL.
15. **Treating monopoly's DWL as separate from market failure.** Monopoly is a market failure (lack of competition).

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For externality questions:**
- Identify positive or negative.
- Identify which curve (MPC vs MSC, MPB vs MSB).
- Compare market output to socially optimal.
- Identify Pigouvian tax or subsidy.

**For public goods:**
- Identify non-rivalry and non-excludability.
- Explain free rider problem.
- Explain need for government provision.

**For asymmetric information:**
- Identify type (adverse selection or moral hazard).
- Explain consequences.
- Identify potential solutions.

**For income inequality:**
- Use Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient.
- Connect to government policies.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "A negative externality occurs when a transaction imposes COSTS on third parties not involved in the transaction. Marginal social cost exceeds marginal private cost (MSC > MPC), and the market produces MORE than the socially optimal quantity, creating deadweight loss."
2. "A Pigouvian tax of [amount] equal to the marginal external cost would internalize the externality, shifting MPC up to equal MSC. The new equilibrium would achieve the socially optimal quantity."
3. "Public goods are characterized by NON-RIVALRY (one person's use doesn't reduce another's) AND NON-EXCLUDABILITY (cannot prevent non-payers from benefiting). The free-rider problem prevents markets from providing them efficiently."
4. "The free-rider problem: people can benefit from a non-excludable good without paying. Markets cannot charge users; private provision is unprofitable. Government provision (funded by taxes) addresses this."
5. "Adverse selection occurs when one party in a transaction has more information than the other BEFORE the transaction. Moral hazard occurs when one party changes behavior AFTER the transaction in ways the other party cannot observe."
6. "Income inequality is measured by the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient. The Gini coefficient ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality). Government policies — progressive taxation, transfer programs, public goods provision — can reduce inequality but may have efficiency costs."
7. "The Coase Theorem states that if property rights are clearly defined and transaction costs are low, parties can reach efficient outcomes through private negotiation, eliminating the need for government regulation. In practice, transaction costs often prevent this."
