## Big Ideas

1. **Demand slopes downward; supply slopes upward.** This generates the basic supply-demand graph. Equilibrium is where they intersect.
2. **A SHIFT in demand or supply changes equilibrium.** Distinguish CHANGE IN QUANTITY DEMANDED (movement along curve due to price change) from CHANGE IN DEMAND (shift of entire curve).
3. **Elasticity measures responsiveness.** Price elasticity of demand: how much quantity changes when price changes. Inelastic (rigid) vs elastic (responsive).
4. **Government intervention (price controls, taxes, subsidies) creates DEADWEIGHT LOSS.** When prices are forced away from equilibrium, total surplus falls.
5. **Consumer and producer surplus measure economic welfare.** Consumer surplus = area below demand above price. Producer surplus = area above supply below price. Total surplus is maximized at equilibrium.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Law of Demand:** as price RISES, quantity demanded FALLS. Inverse relationship. Downward-sloping demand curve.
- **Law of Supply:** as price RISES, quantity supplied RISES. Direct relationship. Upward-sloping supply curve.
- **Demand curve shifters (causes change in DEMAND):**
  - **Income** (normal goods: income up → demand right; inferior goods: income up → demand left).
  - **Tastes/preferences.**
  - **Prices of related goods:**
    - **Substitutes** (price of substitute up → demand for original up).
    - **Complements** (price of complement up → demand for original down).
  - **Number of buyers** (more buyers → demand right).
  - **Expectations** (expecting future price increase → demand now up).
- **Supply curve shifters (causes change in SUPPLY):**
  - **Input prices** (cheaper inputs → supply right).
  - **Technology** (better tech → supply right).
  - **Expectations** of future prices.
  - **Number of sellers.**
  - **Government policies** (subsidies → supply right; taxes → supply left).
- **Market equilibrium:** where supply = demand. Establishes equilibrium price (P*) and equilibrium quantity (Q*).
- **Surplus (excess supply):** when price is ABOVE equilibrium → quantity supplied > quantity demanded.
- **Shortage (excess demand):** when price is BELOW equilibrium → quantity demanded > quantity supplied.
- **Price floor:** government-imposed MINIMUM price. Binding only if ABOVE equilibrium → creates SURPLUS. Examples: minimum wage.
- **Price ceiling:** government-imposed MAXIMUM price. Binding only if BELOW equilibrium → creates SHORTAGE. Examples: rent control, gas price caps.
- **Price elasticity of demand (PED):**
  - **Formula:** % change in Q_d / % change in P (in absolute value).
  - **Elastic (PED > 1):** quantity changes more than price.
  - **Inelastic (PED < 1):** quantity changes less than price.
  - **Unit elastic (PED = 1):** equal proportional changes.
- **Determinants of elasticity:**
  - **Substitutes:** more substitutes → more elastic.
  - **Necessity vs luxury:** necessities inelastic; luxuries elastic.
  - **Time:** longer time → more elastic.
  - **Proportion of income:** larger share → more elastic.
- **Income elasticity of demand:**
  - **Positive (>0):** normal good (more income → buy more).
  - **Negative (<0):** inferior good (more income → buy less).
  - **Greater than 1:** luxury (highly responsive to income).
- **Cross-price elasticity:**
  - **Positive:** substitutes (price of A up → demand for B up).
  - **Negative:** complements (price of A up → demand for B down).
- **Total revenue and elasticity:**
  - **Elastic demand:** price up → revenue down (people buy much less).
  - **Inelastic demand:** price up → revenue up.
  - **Unit elastic:** revenue unchanged.
- **Consumer surplus (CS):** difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay. Triangular area BELOW demand curve and ABOVE price.
- **Producer surplus (PS):** difference between what producers receive and their minimum acceptable price. Triangular area ABOVE supply curve and BELOW price.
- **Total surplus:** CS + PS. MAXIMIZED at competitive equilibrium.
- **Deadweight loss (DWL):** loss of total surplus when market is not at equilibrium (price controls, taxes, monopoly, etc.). Triangular area.

### Adds for [4]

- **Distinguishing "change in quantity demanded" from "change in demand":**
  - **Change in quantity demanded:** movement ALONG the demand curve, caused by price change. Same demand curve.
  - **Change in demand:** SHIFT of the demand curve, caused by anything OTHER than price (income, tastes, etc.).
- **Equilibrium changes when one curve shifts:**
  - **Demand right (S unchanged):** P↑ AND Q↑.
  - **Demand left:** P↓ AND Q↓.
  - **Supply right (D unchanged):** P↓ AND Q↑.
  - **Supply left:** P↑ AND Q↓.
- **Equilibrium changes when both shift simultaneously:**
  - Direction of P or Q is INDETERMINATE if shifts go opposite ways for that variable.
  - Need to know magnitudes.
- **Elasticity calculation example:**
  - Q drops from 100 to 80 when P rises from $5 to $6.
  - % change Q = (80-100)/100 = -20%.
  - % change P = (6-5)/5 = 20%.
  - PED = |-20%/20%| = 1. Unit elastic.
- **Tax incidence:**
  - Per-unit tax shifts supply curve UP (or LEFT) by amount of tax.
  - **Burden distribution depends on elasticity:**
    - More inelastic side bears MORE of the tax burden.
    - If demand is perfectly inelastic, consumers bear ALL of the tax.
    - If demand is perfectly elastic, producers bear ALL of the tax.
- **Consumer/producer surplus visualization:**
  - On a graph: CS is the triangle BELOW the demand curve, ABOVE the price.
  - PS is the triangle ABOVE the supply curve, BELOW the price.
  - Both meet at equilibrium where total surplus is maximized.
- **Deadweight loss from price ceiling:**
  - Ceiling below equilibrium → quantity falls below Q* → DWL is the triangle representing lost transactions that would have occurred at equilibrium.
- **Deadweight loss from price floor:**
  - Floor above equilibrium → quantity falls below Q* → DWL similar.
- **Deadweight loss from per-unit tax:**
  - DWL depends on elasticities — more elastic = more DWL.
- **International trade introduction:**
  - **Imports:** when world price < domestic equilibrium price.
  - **Exports:** when world price > domestic equilibrium price.
  - **Tariffs (taxes on imports)** raise domestic price, reduce imports, create DWL.
  - **Quotas:** quantity restrictions on imports.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why the demand curve slopes downward.** Three reasons:
  - **Substitution effect:** as price rises, consumers substitute toward cheaper alternatives.
  - **Income effect:** as price rises, real income falls, consumers can afford less.
  - **Diminishing marginal utility:** each additional unit provides less satisfaction.
- **Why the supply curve slopes upward.** Producers can earn more profit at higher prices, incentivizing more production. Also, producing more requires using less-efficient resources, raising marginal cost.
- **Why elasticity matters for tax incidence.** Markets where one side is more elastic shift more burden to the inelastic side. Cigarette taxes hit consumers heavily because demand for cigarettes is inelastic. This is why "sin taxes" raise lots of revenue but don't reduce consumption much.
- **The "law of one price" and global markets.** In competitive markets without trade barriers, prices tend toward a single global equilibrium. Trade barriers create wedges between domestic and global prices.
- **Why deadweight loss matters.** It represents PURE LOSS — gains by no one, but everyone collectively loses. Government policies that create DWL (price controls, certain taxes) produce inefficiency.
- **Consumer surplus as economic measure of welfare gain.** Higher CS means more value for consumers. Producer surplus represents profit to producers. Total surplus = social welfare from the market.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Supply and demand shifts

For each scenario, predict effect on equilibrium price (P*) and quantity (Q*):

(a) Income rises and the good is a normal good.
(b) Production technology improves.
(c) Number of firms decreases.
(d) Both: income rises AND technology improves.

- **(a)** Demand RIGHT. P*↑, Q*↑.
- **(b)** Supply RIGHT. P*↓, Q*↑.
- **(c)** Supply LEFT. P*↑, Q*↓.
- **(d)** Both right (D and S). P* INDETERMINATE (D pushes up, S pushes down). Q* DEFINITELY UP.

### Example 2 [3] — Elasticity calculation

A company raises price from $20 to $24. Quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80.

(a) Calculate price elasticity of demand.
(b) Determine if demand is elastic, inelastic, or unit elastic.
(c) What happens to total revenue?

- **(a)** % change Q = (80-100)/100 = -20%. % change P = (24-20)/20 = 20%. PED = |-20%/20%| = 1.
- **(b)** PED = 1, so demand is UNIT ELASTIC.
- **(c)** Original revenue: $20 × 100 = $2000. New revenue: $24 × 80 = $1920. Revenue DROPPED slightly. (For unit elastic, revenue should be unchanged in theory — this is approximation imprecision in midpoint vs starting-point methods.)

### Example 3 [3][4] — Price ceiling

The government imposes a price ceiling on apartments at $800 when equilibrium price is $1200.

(a) Predict effect.
(b) Identify DWL.

- **(a)** Ceiling BELOW equilibrium → BINDING.
  - At $800: quantity supplied < quantity demanded → SHORTAGE.
  - Some renters get $800 apartments who would have paid more.
  - Other renters cannot find apartments.
  - Landlords have less incentive to maintain or build apartments.
- **(b)** Deadweight loss = transactions that would have occurred at equilibrium but don't due to the ceiling. Triangular area.

### Example 4 [4] — Tax incidence

A per-unit tax of $2 is imposed on a good. Demand is INELASTIC; supply is ELASTIC.

(a) Predict who bears more of the tax burden.
(b) Show effect on graph.

- **(a)** Inelastic side bears more burden. Demand is inelastic → CONSUMERS bear most of the tax.
- **(b)** Tax shifts supply curve UP by $2 (from S to S+tax). New equilibrium has higher price (paid by consumers) and lower quantity. The price PRODUCERS receive after tax is lower than original equilibrium. The price difference is the consumer's share vs producer's share.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Consumer and producer surplus, DWL from tax

In a market, equilibrium is at P=$10, Q=100. Government imposes $2 per-unit tax.

(a) What happens to CS, PS, government revenue, and DWL?

- **CS:** decreases (consumers pay higher price, buy less).
- **PS:** decreases (producers receive lower net price, sell less).
- **Government revenue:** $2 × new quantity. Becomes part of total surplus calculation.
- **DWL:** triangle representing transactions that no longer occur (units between Q_new and 100 that consumers and producers would have agreed on but tax made unprofitable).

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing change in demand vs change in quantity demanded.** Change in demand = SHIFT. Change in quantity demanded = MOVEMENT along curve.
2. **Wrong direction of demand shift.** More income with normal good → demand RIGHT (more demand). Inferior good → income up → demand LEFT.
3. **Confusing substitutes and complements.** Substitutes: price of one up → demand for other UP. Complements: price of one up → demand for other DOWN.
4. **Wrong elasticity formula.** PED = |% change Q / % change P|. ABSOLUTE VALUE.
5. **Treating elastic vs inelastic backwards.** Elastic (PED > 1) means HIGH responsiveness. Inelastic (PED < 1) means LOW responsiveness.
6. **Wrong direction for total revenue change.** Inelastic + price up → revenue UP. Elastic + price up → revenue DOWN.
7. **Misunderstanding tax incidence.** Tax burden falls more on the INELASTIC side.
8. **Treating non-binding price controls as effective.** Price ceiling above equilibrium = NO EFFECT. Price floor below equilibrium = NO EFFECT.
9. **Calculating elasticity at wrong points.** Use percent change correctly.
10. **Forgetting deadweight loss is triangular.** DWL is the area lost.
11. **Confusing consumer surplus and producer surplus.** CS = below demand, above price. PS = above supply, below price.
12. **Wrong shift direction for tax.** Tax shifts supply UP (or LEFT) by tax amount.
13. **Treating elasticity as constant along entire curve.** Linear demand has elasticity that varies — elastic at higher prices, inelastic at lower.
14. **Forgetting determinants of elasticity.** Substitutes, time, necessity, income share.
15. **Confusing income elasticity with price elasticity.** Income elasticity uses change in income; price elasticity uses change in price.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For supply/demand graphs:**
- Draw axes with P (vertical) and Q (horizontal).
- Show shift (curve moves right or left).
- Identify new P* and Q*.
- Show direction of changes.

**For elasticity:**
- Calculate using percentage changes.
- Identify as elastic/inelastic/unit.
- Determine effect on revenue.

**For price controls:**
- Determine if binding (below or above equilibrium).
- Identify shortage or surplus.
- Show DWL if any.

**For tax/subsidy:**
- Tax shifts supply UP/LEFT; subsidy shifts RIGHT.
- Identify burden distribution based on elasticity.
- Compute DWL.

**For consumer/producer surplus:**
- Identify on graph.
- Calculate as triangular area when given specific values.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "An increase in income shifts the demand curve RIGHT for normal goods, raising both equilibrium price and quantity."
2. "Price elasticity of demand = |% change in Q / % change in P|. Elastic (PED > 1), Inelastic (PED < 1), Unit elastic (PED = 1)."
3. "When demand is INELASTIC, an increase in price RAISES total revenue. When demand is ELASTIC, an increase in price LOWERS total revenue."
4. "A binding price ceiling (below equilibrium) creates a SHORTAGE — quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. This results in deadweight loss."
5. "When a per-unit tax is imposed, the supply curve shifts UP by the tax amount. The burden is shared between buyers and sellers in proportion INVERSELY related to their elasticities — the more inelastic side bears more burden."
6. "Consumer surplus is the area BELOW the demand curve and ABOVE the price (the value consumers receive beyond what they pay). Producer surplus is the area ABOVE the supply curve and BELOW the price (the surplus producers receive beyond their minimum)."
7. "Deadweight loss represents lost economic surplus — transactions that would have occurred at equilibrium but don't due to government intervention or market failure."
