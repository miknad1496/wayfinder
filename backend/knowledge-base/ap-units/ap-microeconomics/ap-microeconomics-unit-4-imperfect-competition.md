- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Unit 4 covers all market structures BEYOND perfect competition. The Market Structure Comparison Table (per the brain file's signature) is essential. Understanding why each market structure produces different outcomes from perfect competition is the central skill.

## Big Ideas

1. **Four market structures span a spectrum from perfect to imperfect competition.** Perfect competition (Unit 3), Monopolistic Competition, Oligopoly, Monopoly. Each has distinctive features and outcomes.
2. **In imperfect competition, firms have MARKET POWER (price-setting ability).** Demand for the firm is downward-sloping (not horizontal as in perfect competition).
3. **Monopoly produces less and charges more than perfect competition.** Price > MC at the profit-maximizing quantity → DEADWEIGHT LOSS.
4. **Monopolistic competition reaches LONG-RUN ZERO PROFIT but with EXCESS CAPACITY.** Differentiated products + free entry/exit → not productively efficient.
5. **Oligopolies can collude or compete; game theory analyzes their interactions.** Prisoner's Dilemma is the classic example showing why cooperation can break down even when it's mutually beneficial.

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **The Market Structure Comparison Table** [SIGNATURE OF AP MICRO]:

| Feature | Perfect Competition | Monopolistic Competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------------|-----------|----------|
| Number of firms | Many | Many | Few | One |
| Product differentiation | Identical | Differentiated | Standardized OR differentiated | Unique product |
| Barriers to entry | None | Low | High | Very high |
| Demand curve for firm | Horizontal (perfectly elastic) | Downward-sloping (somewhat elastic) | Downward-sloping (often "kinked") | Market demand |
| Long-run profits | Zero | Zero | Can be positive | Positive |
| Examples | Agricultural products | Restaurants, clothing, hair salons | Auto manufacturing, airlines, oil | Local utilities, monopolist patents |

- **Monopoly:**
  - **One seller** with no close substitutes.
  - **Price maker** (sets price, not price taker).
  - **Demand curve is downward-sloping** (entire market demand).
  - **Marginal Revenue is BELOW demand** (selling more requires lowering price on ALL units).
  - **Profit maximization:** MR = MC. Then read price from DEMAND curve at that quantity.
  - **Price > MR > MC** at profit-maximizing Q.
- **Sources of monopoly:**
  - **Natural monopoly:** economies of scale make one firm cheaper than several.
  - **Government-granted monopoly:** patents, copyrights, exclusive contracts.
  - **Resource control:** owns key input.
  - **Network externalities:** product more valuable as more people use it (Microsoft, Facebook).
- **Monopoly inefficiency:**
  - Produces LESS than competitive market.
  - Charges HIGHER price than competitive market.
  - Creates DEADWEIGHT LOSS (consumer surplus lost not transferred to producer surplus or government).
- **Price discrimination:**
  - **Charging different prices to different consumers for the same good.**
  - **Conditions:** market power; distinguishable customer groups; can prevent resale.
  - **Three degrees:**
    - **First-degree (perfect):** charge each consumer their maximum willingness to pay. Maximum profit; eliminates deadweight loss but transfers all surplus to producer.
    - **Second-degree:** different prices for different quantities (bulk discounts).
    - **Third-degree:** different prices for different segments (student/senior discounts, peak/off-peak).
- **Monopolistic Competition:**
  - **Many firms** with **differentiated products** (e.g., restaurants — many but each unique).
  - **Free entry and exit** in long run.
  - **Demand curve is downward-sloping** (some market power due to differentiation).
  - **Short run:** can earn profit or loss.
  - **Long run:** entry/exit drives economic profit to ZERO. But firms operate at QUANTITY BELOW minimum ATC (excess capacity).
- **Excess capacity in monopolistic competition:**
  - At long-run equilibrium, firm produces less than minimum ATC quantity.
  - Could lower average cost by producing more, but downward-sloping demand means doing so requires lowering price below ATC.
- **Oligopoly:**
  - **Few firms** dominate the market.
  - **Standardized OR differentiated products.**
  - **High barriers to entry.**
  - **Strategic interdependence** (firms' decisions affect each other).
- **Game theory and oligopoly:**
  - **Prisoner's Dilemma:** classic example. Both firms have dominant strategy to compete (cheat on collusion). Both end up worse off than if they'd cooperated.
  - **Cartel:** explicit agreement to coordinate (e.g., OPEC). Often unstable because each member has incentive to cheat.
  - **Tacit collusion:** implicit coordination without formal agreement.
- **Game theory basics:**
  - **Payoff matrix:** shows outcomes for each combination of strategies.
  - **Dominant strategy:** strategy that's best regardless of opponent's choice.
  - **Nash equilibrium:** combination of strategies where no player can improve by changing strategy alone.
  - **Cooperate vs defect outcomes:** Prisoner's Dilemma shows both prefer to defect (Nash equilibrium) but both would be better if both cooperated.

### Adds for [4]

- **Monopoly profit maximization worked through:**
  - **Step 1:** Find MR (lies BELOW demand for a downward-sloping demand curve).
  - **Step 2:** Set MR = MC.
  - **Step 3:** Read profit-maximizing Q*.
  - **Step 4:** Read price P* from DEMAND curve at Q*.
  - **Step 5:** Profit = (P* − ATC) × Q*.
- **Why MR < P for monopolist.** Because monopolist must lower price on ALL units to sell additional units. The revenue from one more unit is the price MINUS the loss from charging less on previous units.
- **Monopoly's deadweight loss (DWL):**
  - Triangular area between MC and demand from monopoly quantity to competitive quantity.
  - Represents lost transactions that benefit both consumers and producers.
- **Natural monopoly graph:**
  - Long-run ATC continuously decreasing → one firm can serve entire market at lower cost.
  - Government may regulate (allow natural monopoly but control prices).
- **Monopolistic competition long-run equilibrium:**
  - Demand curve is TANGENT to ATC at the quantity where MR = MC.
  - Tangency means P = ATC → zero economic profit.
  - But Q is below minimum ATC quantity → EXCESS CAPACITY.
- **Why product differentiation matters in monopolistic competition:**
  - Each firm has slight monopoly over its differentiated product.
  - Brand loyalty creates downward-sloping demand.
  - But many close substitutes → demand is somewhat elastic.
- **Oligopoly game theory worked through:**
  - **Prisoner's Dilemma payoff matrix:**
    - Both cooperate: 5, 5.
    - One defects, other cooperates: 7, 1.
    - Both defect: 3, 3.
  - **Dominant strategy for each:** defect (since 7 > 5 if other cooperates, 3 > 1 if other defects).
  - **Nash equilibrium:** both defect (3, 3).
  - **Cooperative outcome (5, 5):** would be better but unstable.
- **Real-world oligopoly examples:**
  - Airline industry (only a handful of major US carriers).
  - Auto manufacturing.
  - Smartphone industry (Apple, Samsung, etc.).

### Adds for [5]

- **Why monopolists don't always make profit.** Even monopolists must have demand high enough to cover costs. A monopolist with very high costs might lose money. Monopoly position alone doesn't guarantee profit.
- **Why price discrimination requires market power AND no resale.** A firm without market power can't charge different prices (competitors would undercut). If consumers can resell, low-price buyers will resell to high-price buyers, eliminating discrimination.
- **Allocative inefficiency in monopoly.** P > MC means consumers value additional units more than they cost to produce. Society would benefit from more production. But monopolist won't produce more because it would lower revenue per unit.
- **Why monopolistic competition is "the worst of both worlds":**
  - **Like monopoly:** P > MC (allocative inefficiency).
  - **Unlike perfect competition:** doesn't operate at minimum ATC (productive inefficiency).
  - **However:** consumers benefit from variety and choice.
- **Why cartels are unstable.** Each member has individual incentive to cheat (produce more, capture larger market share). With cheating, cartel collapses. OPEC has periodically failed to maintain agreed production quotas.
- **The "kinked demand curve" model.** In oligopoly, firms believe:
  - If they raise price, others won't follow (lose market share dramatically).
  - If they lower price, others will follow (no advantage).
  - This produces a "kink" at current price → price stickiness.
- **Why oligopolies tend toward implicit collusion.** Even without formal agreement, firms recognize mutual benefits of high prices. Tacit coordination through pricing patterns. But this is unstable — same Prisoner's Dilemma logic.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Monopoly profit maximization

A monopolist faces demand P = 100 − Q. Cost function: TC = 20 + Q² (so MC = 2Q).

(a) Find MR.
(b) Find profit-maximizing Q.
(c) Find profit-maximizing P.
(d) Calculate profit.

- **(a)** From P = 100 − Q, total revenue TR = PQ = 100Q − Q². MR = dTR/dQ = 100 − 2Q.
- **(b)** Set MR = MC: 100 − 2Q = 2Q → Q = 25.
- **(c)** P = 100 − 25 = $75.
- **(d)** TR = 75 × 25 = $1875. TC = 20 + 25² = $645. Profit = 1875 − 645 = $1230.
- **Compared to perfect competition:** if perfectly competitive, P = MC at the relevant Q → MC = MR + Q = 2Q. Set 100 − Q = 2Q → Q = 33.3, P = $66.7.
- Monopoly produces less (25 vs 33.3) and charges more ($75 vs $66.7). Inefficiency = DWL.

### Example 2 [3][4] — Monopoly DWL

In Example 1, the monopoly produces Q = 25 at P = $75. The competitive market would produce Q = 33.3 at P = $66.7.

(a) Calculate deadweight loss.

- **DWL** = triangular area between MC and demand from Q = 25 to Q = 33.3.
- **Base of triangle:** Q = 33.3 − 25 = 8.3.
- **Height:** P − MC at Q = 25 = 75 − 50 (MC at Q=25 is 2(25) = 50) = 25.
- **Area:** (1/2) × 8.3 × 25 ≈ $103.7.
- **Interpretation:** $103.7 of total surplus is lost compared to competitive equilibrium.

### Example 3 [4] — Monopolistic competition

A monopolistic competition firm in long-run equilibrium has its demand curve TANGENT to ATC at the quantity where MR = MC.

(a) What does this mean for profit?
(b) What does this mean for efficiency?

- **(a)** Tangency means P = ATC → ZERO ECONOMIC PROFIT. Free entry/exit drives profits to zero in long run.
- **(b)** **Allocative inefficiency:** P > MC (since demand is downward-sloping, MR < P, and P is above MC at the optimum quantity).
- **Productive inefficiency:** firm operates at QUANTITY BELOW minimum ATC (excess capacity).
- **Trade-off:** consumers benefit from product variety but pay higher prices and producers operate at higher costs.

### Example 4 [4] — Prisoner's Dilemma

Two competing firms must decide to set prices low or high.

| Firm B Low | Firm B High |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Firm A Low:** A=$3, B=$3 | A=$8, B=$1 |
| **Firm A High:** A=$1, B=$8 | A=$5, B=$5 |

(a) Find dominant strategy for each.
(b) Find Nash equilibrium.
(c) Identify cooperative outcome.

- **(a) Firm A's choice:**
  - If B chooses Low: A gets $3 (Low) or $1 (High). A prefers Low.
  - If B chooses High: A gets $8 (Low) or $5 (High). A prefers Low.
  - DOMINANT STRATEGY for A: LOW.
  - Same for B by symmetry.
- **(b)** Nash equilibrium: both choose LOW. Payoff: ($3, $3).
- **(c)** Cooperative outcome (both High): ($5, $5). Both better off, but unstable — each has incentive to defect.
- **This is the Prisoner's Dilemma.** Individual rationality leads to collectively worse outcome.

### Example 5 [4][5] — Comparing market structures

For each: identify the market structure.

(a) Many farmers selling identical wheat.
(b) Few automakers competing on similar features.
(c) One local cable TV provider.
(d) Many fast-food restaurants offering different menus.

- **(a) Perfect competition.** Many sellers, identical product.
- **(b) Oligopoly.** Few firms, similar products, strategic interdependence.
- **(c) Monopoly.** One seller, unique product, no close substitutes.
- **(d) Monopolistic competition.** Many sellers, differentiated products.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing monopoly graph with perfect competition.** Monopoly: downward-sloping demand, MR below demand. Perfect competition: horizontal firm demand.
2. **Wrong rule for finding monopoly price.** Use MR = MC to find Q, then read P from DEMAND curve at Q.
3. **Forgetting MR < P for monopoly.** Selling more requires lowering price on all units → MR is less than P.
4. **Treating monopolistic competition like monopoly.** Mono comp has MANY firms; monopoly has ONE.
5. **Forgetting long-run zero profit in monopolistic competition.** Free entry/exit drives profits to zero.
6. **Misidentifying excess capacity.** Mono comp's long-run equilibrium is below minimum ATC quantity.
7. **Confusing types of price discrimination.** First-degree (perfect), second-degree (quantity), third-degree (segmentation).
8. **Wrong outcome of Prisoner's Dilemma.** Nash equilibrium = both defect. Cooperative outcome = both cooperate (better but unstable).
9. **Treating cartels as stable.** Cartels are inherently unstable due to individual incentive to cheat.
10. **Forgetting why oligopolies have kinked demand.** Asymmetric expectations about competitor responses to price changes.
11. **Misreading profit areas on graph.** (P − ATC) × Q.
12. **Wrong direction for DWL on monopoly graph.** DWL is triangle BETWEEN MC and demand, RIGHT of monopoly Q.
13. **Confusing dominant strategy with Nash equilibrium.** Dominant strategy is best regardless of opponent. Nash is optimal given opponent's choice.
14. **Forgetting natural monopoly characteristics.** Long-run ATC continuously decreasing; one firm cheaper than multiple.
15. **Ignoring product differentiation in monopolistic competition.** This creates the slight market power.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For monopoly graphs:**
- Demand DOWNWARD-sloping.
- MR BELOW demand.
- Find MR = MC for Q*.
- Read P* from DEMAND at Q*.
- Identify profit/loss as (P − ATC) × Q.
- Identify DWL.

**For monopolistic competition long-run:**
- Demand tangent to ATC at MR = MC.
- Zero economic profit.
- Excess capacity.

**For oligopoly:**
- Use payoff matrix.
- Find dominant strategy if exists.
- Identify Nash equilibrium.
- Compare to cooperative outcome.

**For market structure identification:**
- Number of firms.
- Product differentiation.
- Barriers to entry.
- Market power / demand curve shape.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "A monopolist faces the entire market demand curve, which is downward-sloping. Marginal revenue lies BELOW demand because selling additional units requires lowering the price on ALL units sold."
2. "A monopolist maximizes profit by producing where MR = MC, then setting price by reading from the DEMAND CURVE at that quantity. Price exceeds marginal cost, creating allocative inefficiency."
3. "Monopoly creates DEADWEIGHT LOSS — the loss of total surplus from producing less than the competitive quantity. Some transactions that would benefit both consumers and producers don't occur."
4. "In monopolistic competition's long-run equilibrium, the demand curve is TANGENT to the ATC curve at the profit-maximizing quantity. This means P = ATC (zero economic profit) but the firm operates BELOW minimum ATC (excess capacity)."
5. "In a Prisoner's Dilemma, both firms have a dominant strategy of [defecting]. The Nash equilibrium is mutual defection, even though mutual cooperation would yield a higher payoff for both. Cartels are inherently unstable because each member has individual incentive to deviate."
6. "Price discrimination requires three conditions: (1) market power, (2) ability to distinguish customers by willingness to pay, and (3) ability to prevent resale between groups."
7. "Natural monopoly arises when long-run average costs continuously decline due to economies of scale, making it more efficient for one firm to supply the entire market than for multiple firms to compete."
