# AP Art History — Unit 6: Africa (1100-1980 CE) — Wayfinder Teaching Brain

> Surfaces in chat when student mentions topics in this unit. Use to ground custom teaching responses + on-demand teaching guide generation.

## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** ~6% of the AP Art History exam
- **Sub-topics covered:** Great Zimbabwe; Benin; Yoruba (Ife); Bamileke; masks (Dan, Senufo); contemporary African artists.
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** ~14 of 250 images. Focus on West/Central Africa; sculptural traditions; functional/ritual art.

## Big Ideas

1. **African art** is hugely diverse; ~3000 distinct cultures and languages.
2. **Functional art:** masks, sculptures often serve religious, social, political functions.
3. **Royal art** of Benin, Ife, Asante reflects political power.
4. **Western "primitivism" misread** African art as simple; actually highly sophisticated.
5. **Contemporary African artists** engage tradition + globalization.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Key works:**
  - **Great Zimbabwe** (~1000-1450 CE): massive stone city; soapstone bird carvings.
  - **Benin plaques (Nigeria)** (~1500s+ CE): brass relief showing Benin court.
  - **Ife head (Yoruba, Nigeria)** (~1100-1500 CE): naturalistic terracotta/brass.
  - **Mblo mask (Baule, Côte d'Ivoire).**
  - **Sowei mask (Mende, Sierra Leone).**
  - **Reliquary figure (Fang, Gabon).**
  - **Power figure (Nkisi n'kondi, Kongo).**
  - **Kente cloth (Asante, Ghana).**
- **Sculptural traditions:**
  - **Wood carving** common (impermanent in tropical climates).
  - **Stylized vs naturalistic varies** by tradition.
  - **Often abstract** body proportions for symbolic emphasis.
- **Mask traditions:**
  - **Worn in ceremonies** (initiation, funerals, agriculture).
  - **Combined with costume; performance.**
  - **Mediate between human and spirit world.**
- **Royal art:**
  - **Benin (Nigeria):** brass plaques, ivory carvings showing court hierarchy.
  - **Ife (Yoruba):** naturalistic heads of rulers.
  - **Asante (Ghana):** golden stool symbolized kingdom.
- **Contemporary African artists:**
  - **El Anatsui (Ghana):** monumental tapestries from bottle caps.
  - **Yinka Shonibare (UK/Nigeria).**
  - **Wangechi Mutu (Kenya).**
  - **Engaging African heritage and globalization.**

### Adds for [4]

- **Why "Primitivism" misread African art:**
  - **Picasso, Matisse drew on African masks** (~1907+) for Cubism, Fauvism.
  - **Inspired modernist abstraction.**
  - **But "primitive" label** wrong: African traditions sophisticated and functional.
- **Benin Bronzes:**
  - **British looted ~1897** during punitive expedition.
  - **Major repatriation movement** (some returns recently).
- **Great Zimbabwe:**
  - **Conical tower, walls** of fitted stone.
  - **Capital of Shona civilization** (~1100-1450).
  - **Trade with Indian Ocean.**
  - **Colonial-era denial** that Africans built it (now thoroughly documented).
- **Nkisi n'kondi (Kongo) power figures:**
  - **Wood with attached materials** (nails, blades, mirrors).
  - **Activated by spiritual specialist (nganga).**
  - **Each nail driven** records oath, agreement, or grievance.

### Adds for [5]

- **Why African sculpture influenced modernism:**
  - **Departure from Western naturalism** showed European artists alternative aesthetic.
  - **But European appropriation often stripped function/context.**

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Match works

For each, identify culture/region:
(a) Great Zimbabwe.
(b) Benin plaques.
(c) Nkisi n'kondi.

- (a) **Shona, southern Africa (modern Zimbabwe).**
- (b) **Edo (Benin Kingdom, Nigeria).**
- (c) **Kongo (Central Africa).**

### Example 2 [4] — Mask function

How do African masks function in their cultural context?
- **Worn in ceremonies** (initiations, funerals, agricultural rituals).
- **Combined with costume and performance** (movement, music).
- **Mediate** between human and spirit world.
- **Activated by performance;** dormant when stored.
- **Western museum display** typically misses this performative context.

### Example 3 [5] — Modernism's debt

How did African sculpture influence European modernism?
- **Picasso encountered African masks** (~1907) at Trocadéro Museum.
- **Influenced *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*** (1907).
- **Influenced Cubism, Fauvism** more broadly.
- **But European appropriation** stripped context, function.
- **"Primitivism" label** problematic; African art is highly sophisticated.

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **"African art" is not monolithic** — thousands of distinct traditions.
2. **Great Zimbabwe was African;** colonial-era claims of foreign origin discredited.
3. **Masks are performance objects,** not just sculptures.
4. **Benin Bronzes were looted** by British (1897); active repatriation issue.
5. **Avoid "primitive"** — African art is sophisticated.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For attribution:** identify culture, region, function.
**For analysis:** address performance/ritual context, not just object.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "Africa's artistic traditions are vast and diverse — Great Zimbabwe's stone city (~1000-1450 CE), Benin's brass plaques, Ife's naturalistic terracotta heads, Kongo power figures (Nkisi n'kondi), and countless mask traditions across West and Central Africa."
2. "African masks function within performance contexts — combined with costume, music, and ritual movement — mediating between human and spirit worlds in initiations, funerals, and agricultural ceremonies. Western museum display typically strips this performative dimension."
3. "Picasso's encounter with African masks (~1907) at the Trocadéro Museum influenced *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* and the development of Cubism. But European 'primitivism' labels misread African art as simple, when it is in fact highly sophisticated, function-laden, and stylistically purposeful."

## If You Do Nothing Else for This Unit

*Master key works (Great Zimbabwe, Benin plaques, Ife head, Nkisi n'kondi). Master mask function in performance. Master African influence on European modernism. Avoid "primitive" framing.*
