## Unit at a Glance

- **Exam weight:** 12–17% of the AP Human Geography exam
- **Sub-topics covered:** population distribution; density (arithmetic, physiological, agricultural); demographic transition model; population pyramids; Malthus and critics; migration types; Ravenstein's laws; push-pull factors; refugees.
- **Where this unit appears on the exam:** Demographic Transition Model (DTM, 5 stages) tested constantly. Population pyramids interpretation. Push-pull migration factors. Ravenstein's laws of migration.

## Big Ideas

1. **Population distribution is uneven** — concentrated in temperate, coastal, fertile areas.
2. **Demographic Transition Model (DTM)** describes population changes as countries develop.
3. **Population pyramids** reveal age/sex structure and predict future trends.
4. **Migration follows patterns** described by Ravenstein, push-pull factors.
5. **Population policies vary** from pronatalist to antinatalist.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Population distribution:**
  - **Most live in 4 clusters:** East Asia, South Asia, Europe, Eastern North America.
  - Concentrated in **temperate climates, coasts, river valleys, fertile lowlands.**
  - **Sparsely populated:** deserts, polar regions, dense forests, high mountains.
- **Density measures:**
  - **Arithmetic density:** total population / total area. Simple but misleading.
  - **Physiological density:** population / arable land. Better measure of pressure on agriculture.
  - **Agricultural density:** farmers / arable land. Measure of agricultural development.
- **Demographic Transition Model (DTM) — 5 stages:**
  - **Stage 1 (High Stationary):** high birth rate, high death rate; low growth. Pre-industrial. (Few countries today.)
  - **Stage 2 (Early Expanding):** high birth rate, declining death rate; rapid growth. Sub-Saharan Africa.
  - **Stage 3 (Late Expanding):** declining birth rate, low death rate; slowing growth. Mexico, India.
  - **Stage 4 (Low Stationary):** low birth rate, low death rate; stable population. US, China.
  - **Stage 5 (Declining):** birth rate < death rate; population decline. Japan, Germany, Italy.
- **Key population terms:**
  - **Crude Birth Rate (CBR):** births per 1,000 per year.
  - **Crude Death Rate (CDR):** deaths per 1,000 per year.
  - **Total Fertility Rate (TFR):** average children per woman.
    - **Replacement level:** ~2.1.
    - **Sub-replacement:** below 2.1; eventual decline.
  - **Natural Increase Rate (NIR):** (CBR - CDR) / 10 = percentage growth.
  - **Doubling time:** years to double; 70 / NIR.
  - **Infant mortality rate:** deaths under 1 year per 1,000 births. Strong indicator of development.
  - **Life expectancy:** years person expected to live at birth.
- **Population pyramids:**
  - **Wide base, narrow top:** Stage 2 (rapid growth, young population).
  - **Even distribution:** Stage 3-4 (slowing growth).
  - **Narrow base:** Stage 5 (declining; aging population).
  - **Asymmetry** can show migration patterns or one-child policies.
- **Dependency ratio:**
  - **Youth + elderly dependent on working-age** population.
  - High dependency = burden on workers.
- **Malthusian theory (Thomas Malthus, 1798):**
  - Population grows **exponentially**; food grows **arithmetically**.
  - Will outstrip food supply unless checked.
  - **Critics:** technology (Green Revolution) expanded food production; demographic transition reduced birth rates.
  - **Neo-Malthusians:** still warn of population pressures (water, climate).
- **Migration types:**
  - **Internal migration:** within country.
  - **International migration:** between countries.
  - **Voluntary migration:** by choice.
  - **Forced migration:** refugees, slavery, etc.
  - **Step migration:** series of shorter moves.
  - **Chain migration:** people follow earlier migrants from same area.
  - **Seasonal migration:** workers follow harvests.
- **Ravenstein's Laws of Migration (1880s):**
  - Most migrants travel **short distances**.
  - Migration occurs in **steps**.
  - Long-distance migrants go to **major centers**.
  - Each migration produces **counter-migration**.
  - **Females** migrate more for short distances; **males** more long-distance (historically).
  - **Most migrants are young adults.**
  - **Urban areas grow more from migration than natural increase.**
- **Push-pull factors:**
  - **Push factors:** drive away (war, persecution, lack of jobs, environmental degradation).
  - **Pull factors:** attract (jobs, family, freedom, climate).
  - **Economic** most common (jobs).
  - **Political** (refugees, asylum).
  - **Environmental** (drought, disaster, climate).
  - **Cultural** (education, religion, family).
- **Refugees:**
  - **Refugees:** crossed international border due to persecution/violence.
  - **Internally displaced persons (IDPs):** within their country.
  - **Asylum seekers:** seeking refugee status.
  - **UN High Commissioner for Refugees** coordinates response.
- **Population policies:**
  - **Pronatalist:** encourage births (France, Singapore today; Romania historically).
  - **Antinatalist:** discourage births (China's One-Child Policy 1979-2015; India sterilization).

### Adds for [4]

- **Stages of DTM with examples:**
  - **Stage 2:** Sub-Saharan Africa (Niger, DRC) — death rate dropped (medicine, sanitation) but birth rate still high.
  - **Stage 3:** Mexico, India, Brazil — birth rate declining (urbanization, women's education, contraception).
  - **Stage 4:** US, China, much of Europe — both rates low.
  - **Stage 5:** Japan, Germany, Italy, South Korea — TFR below replacement; aging.
- **Why birth rates decline:**
  - **Urbanization** — children less economically valuable.
  - **Women's education and employment.**
  - **Contraception availability.**
  - **Lower infant mortality** — fewer "extra" births needed.
  - **Cultural shifts** — smaller family preference.
- **China's One-Child Policy (1979-2015):**
  - Reduced births dramatically.
  - **Skewed sex ratio** (preference for sons; female infanticide/abortion).
  - **"4-2-1" problem:** 4 grandparents, 2 parents, 1 child.
  - **Aging population** challenge today.
  - Replaced with two-child (2016) then three-child (2021) policies.
- **Why migration patterns:**
  - **Most migrants follow well-established corridors.**
  - **Family/social networks** facilitate chain migration.
  - **Distance decay:** migration declines with distance.
  - **Economic opportunity** is main driver.
- **Brain drain:**
  - Educated migrants leave origin country.
  - Loss of skilled labor for sending countries.
  - Gain for receiving countries.
- **Remittances:**
  - Money sent home by migrants.
  - Major income source for many developing countries (Mexico, Philippines, India).

### Adds for [5]

- **Why DTM matters.**
  - Powerful framework for understanding population change.
  - Connects demography to development.
  - Helps predict future trajectories.
  - **Limits:** Western-centric model; not all countries follow same pattern.
- **Why aging population matters.**
  - Stage 5 countries face: shrinking workforce, increased pension/healthcare burden, immigration pressure to fill labor.
  - Japan, Germany, Italy, South Korea grappling with this.
- **Migration and globalization.**
  - Global migration at all-time highs.
  - Climate migration expected to increase.
  - Migration politically contentious in many destination countries.
  - **Remittances** vital for many origin economies.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Density measures

A country has 100M people, 1M square km total, 200,000 sq km arable land, 10M farmers.
- **Arithmetic density:** 100M / 1M = 100/sq km.
- **Physiological density:** 100M / 200,000 = 500/sq km arable.
- **Agricultural density:** 10M / 200,000 = 50 farmers/sq km arable.

### Example 2 [3] — DTM stages

For each, identify the DTM stage:
(a) Niger — high birth, high but falling death rate, rapid growth.
(b) US — low birth, low death, ~stable.
(c) Japan — birth below death, population declining.

- (a) **Stage 2.**
- (b) **Stage 4.**
- (c) **Stage 5.**

### Example 3 [4] — Population pyramid interpretation

A pyramid has wide base, narrow top.
- **Stage 2** (rapid growth).
- **Young population.**
- **High dependency ratio** (many children).
- **Future implications:** large workforce coming, but needs jobs/education.

### Example 4 [4] — Push-pull factors

Mexican migrant to US:
- **Push:** lack of jobs, low wages, violence (in some areas).
- **Pull:** US jobs, family already there, higher wages.
- **Type:** voluntary, international, often chain migration.

### Example 5 [5] — Stage 5 challenges

What challenges does Japan (Stage 5) face?
- **Population decline.**
- **Aging population:** shrinking workforce.
- **Pension and healthcare** burden.
- **Need for immigration** (politically resisted in Japan).
- **Reduced economic dynamism.**
- **Government incentives** for births (largely ineffective so far).

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing density measures.** Arithmetic (total area), physiological (arable land), agricultural (farmers/arable).
2. **Wrong DTM stage assignments.** Stage 2 = Sub-Saharan Africa. Stage 5 = Japan, Germany, Italy.
3. **Confusing CBR/CDR/TFR/NIR.** CBR = births/1000. CDR = deaths/1000. TFR = avg children/woman. NIR = (CBR-CDR)/10.
4. **Replacement TFR.** ~**2.1**, not 2.0 (accounts for some mortality).
5. **Confusing migration types.** Internal vs international, voluntary vs forced, step vs chain.
6. **Wrong refugee definition.** **Crossed international border** due to persecution. IDPs are within country.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For DTM questions:**
- Identify stage by birth/death/growth rates.
- Connect to development level.

**For migration questions:**
- Identify type (internal/international, voluntary/forced).
- Identify push and pull factors.
- Apply Ravenstein's laws.

**For population pyramid questions:**
- Identify shape (expansive, stationary, contractive).
- Connect to DTM stage.
- Note implications (dependency, future trajectory).
