# AP Latin — Unit 3: Grammar and Syntax — Wayfinder Teaching Brain

> Surfaces in chat when student mentions topics in this unit.

## Unit at a Glance

- **Implicit throughout** AP Latin exam — every section requires grammar mastery.
- **Sub-topics covered:** noun cases (5+ uses each); verb tenses, moods, voices; common subordinate clauses; participles; ablative absolute; gerunds/gerundives.

## Big Ideas

1. **Five noun cases:** nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, ablative (vocative + locative).
2. **Verbs:** 6 tenses, 3 moods (indicative, subjunctive, imperative), 2 voices.
3. **Subjunctive uses:** purpose, result, indirect question, indirect command, etc.
4. **Ablative absolute:** noun + participle in ablative; sets context.
5. **Participles** = verbal adjectives; agree with noun in case/number/gender.

## Tier-Tagged Content

### Foundational [3] — needed for any score

- **Noun cases:**
  - **Nominative:** subject, predicate nominative.
  - **Genitive:** possession ("of"), partitive, descriptive.
  - **Dative:** indirect object ("to/for"), reference, possession.
  - **Accusative:** direct object, place to which (with prep), duration of time.
  - **Ablative:** means/manner/place from which/agent (with ab/ā), absolute, separation, comparison.
  - **Vocative:** direct address.
- **Verbs:**
  - **Tenses:** present, imperfect, future, perfect, pluperfect, future perfect.
  - **Moods:** indicative (statement), subjunctive (hypothetical/dependent), imperative (command).
  - **Voice:** active (subject does), passive (subject is acted on).
  - **Number:** singular, plural.
  - **Person:** 1st (I/we), 2nd (you), 3rd (he/she/it/they).
- **Common subjunctive uses:**
  - **Purpose clause** (ut + subj.): "in order to."
  - **Result clause** (ut + subj.): "so that... that."
  - **Indirect question** (subj. with interrogative).
  - **Indirect command** (ut + subj. with verbs of asking).
  - **Cum clauses** (cum + subj.): when, since, although.
  - **Conditional** (si...): contrary-to-fact uses subj.
- **Participles:**
  - **Present active** (-ans, -ens): doing.
  - **Perfect passive** (-tus, -ta, -tum): having been done.
  - **Future active** (-urus): about to do.
  - **Agree** with modified noun in case, number, gender.
- **Ablative absolute:**
  - **Noun + participle** in ablative.
  - **Sets context** ("with X having been done...").
  - **Example:** *Caesare duce* ("with Caesar as leader").
- **Gerunds/gerundives:**
  - **Gerund:** verbal noun ("of doing").
  - **Gerundive:** verbal adjective ("to be done").
- **Indirect statement:**
  - **Accusative + infinitive** after verbs of saying/thinking.
  - **Example:** *dixit eum venire* ("he said that he was coming").

### Adds for [4]

- **Conditional sentences:**
  - **Simple/Future:** indicative.
  - **Contrary-to-fact present:** imperfect subjunctive both clauses.
  - **Contrary-to-fact past:** pluperfect subjunctive.
- **Cum clauses tricky:** cum + indicative ("when literally") vs cum + subjunctive (circumstance).

### Adds for [5]

- **Why subjunctive complex:** Latin uses extensively where English uses other constructions.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1 [3] — Identify case

In *puella libros legit*: identify case of each.
- **puella:** **nominative** (subject).
- **libros:** **accusative** (direct object).
- **legit:** verb (3rd person singular present).

### Example 2 [4] — Ablative absolute

Translate *urbe capta, milites laeti erant.*
- ***urbe capta*** = ablative absolute ("with the city having been captured").
- ***milites laeti erant*** = "the soldiers were happy."
- **Together:** "When the city had been captured, the soldiers were happy."

### Example 3 [5] — Indirect statement

Translate *dixit Caesarem venire.*
- ***dixit*** = "he/she said."
- ***Caesarem venire*** = accusative + infinitive ("Caesar to be coming").
- **Indirect statement:** "He said that Caesar was coming."

## Top Traps & Common Errors

1. **Confusing cases:** dative vs ablative singular often look similar.
2. **Subjunctive mood markers:** -em, -es, -et, etc. (varies by conjugation).
3. **Ablative absolute** sets scene; doesn't refer to subject of main clause.
4. **Indirect statement** uses accusative subject + infinitive verb.

## Rubric-Aware Tactics

**For translation:** identify case/tense/mood; identify clause structure.
**For sight reading:** look for main verb first; then identify supporting structures.

## "Phrases That Score" — verbatim language for FRQs

1. "The ablative absolute (noun + participle in ablative) provides circumstantial context independent of the main clause: *Caesare duce* ('with Caesar as leader'); *urbe capta* ('with the city having been captured')."
2. "Indirect statement after verbs of saying or thinking uses accusative subject with infinitive verb: *dixit eum venire* ('he said that he was coming') — distinct from direct quotation."
3. "The Latin subjunctive marks dependency rather than uncertainty per se; common uses include purpose clauses (ut + subjunctive), result clauses, indirect questions, indirect commands, and cum-circumstantial clauses."
